# Awareness Strategy — Developing the Strategic Approach

## Strategic Models for Awareness

Your awareness strategy must flow from your positioning — it is the public-facing expression of how you solve market problems for specific personas. Choose a primary strategic model based on your market position and competitive context.

### Model 1: Problem-Led Awareness
**Best for:** Markets where the problem is well-understood but solutions are not yet associated with your brand.

Approach: Lead with the market problem, not your brand or product. Create content and programs that help the target audience understand, quantify, and articulate the problem they face. Position your brand as the entity that deeply understands their world.

Example: A cybersecurity company creates original research on the frequency and cost of a specific attack vector, publishes the findings through industry media, and becomes the go-to source when the market thinks about that threat category.

**Connection to Pragmatic:** This model directly reflects the outside-in philosophy. Your positioning documents define the problems you solve; problem-led awareness amplifies those problems in the market's language, not yours.

### Model 2: Thought-Leadership-Led Awareness
**Best for:** Markets where multiple solutions exist and differentiation comes from depth of expertise and trust.

Approach: Establish your brand's experts as credible voices in the industry. Create original perspectives, challenge conventional thinking, and provide actionable insight that demonstrates genuine understanding of the market. The brand earns awareness through the authority of its people and ideas.

Example: An analytics platform's VP of Product publishes a series of articles challenging industry assumptions about data quality, speaks at major conferences, and is quoted regularly by analysts — all grounded in the company's distinctive competencies.

**Connection to Pragmatic:** This model leverages Distinctive Competencies and requires strong storytelling skills — one of the key skills listed for the Awareness role in the framework.

### Model 3: Brand-Led Awareness
**Best for:** Markets where category awareness is high, competition is fierce, and differentiation requires emotional and mnemonic distinctiveness.

Approach: Invest in distinctive brand assets — visual identity, tone of voice, recurring themes, characters, or catchphrases — that make your brand unmistakable and memorable. Focus on broad reach and frequency to build mental availability.

**Connection to Pragmatic:** Brand-led awareness works best when positioning is strong and market definition is clear. The brand assets serve as shorthand for the positioning — they must encode the right message, not just be attention-grabbing.

### Hybrid Approach (Recommended for Most B2B)
Most B2B organizations benefit from a hybrid: problem-led content as the primary vehicle, delivered through thought-leadership voices, wrapped in distinctive brand assets. This combination builds both rational credibility and emotional memorability.

## Content Pillars from Positioning

Content pillars are the thematic territories your awareness programs will own. They are derived directly from your positioning documents.

### Deriving Content Pillars

For each priority segment-persona combination, extract from the positioning document:

1. **The core problem** — What urgent, pervasive problem does this persona face?
2. **The market context** — What industry trends or shifts make this problem more acute?
3. **Your differentiated perspective** — What unique insight does your organization bring to this problem based on distinctive competencies?
4. **The proof narrative** — What evidence demonstrates your ability to solve this problem?

Each combination of problem + context + perspective becomes a content pillar.

### Content Pillar Structure

| Pillar Element | Source | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Problem theme | Positioning → persona problems | "Security teams can't keep pace with cloud-native attack surfaces" |
| Market context | Market Problems + Competitive Landscape | "Multi-cloud adoption has outpaced security tooling evolution" |
| Differentiated angle | Distinctive Competencies | "Runtime observability reveals what static scanning misses" |
| Proof narrative | Win/Loss + customer stories | "How [customer type] reduced mean-time-to-detect by 70%" |

Aim for 3-5 content pillars. Fewer than 3 limits your reach; more than 5 dilutes your message.

## Messaging Hierarchy for Awareness

Awareness-stage messaging is different from consideration-stage or decision-stage messaging. It must operate at a higher level of abstraction — focused on problems and perspectives rather than features and comparisons.

### Messaging Levels

**Level 1: Brand Promise (Market Level)**
One sentence that captures why your brand matters to the market. This appears in brand advertising, taglines, and corporate positioning.
- Derived from: Positioning → overarching value proposition
- Example: "We help security teams protect what they can't see."

**Level 2: Pillar Messages (Segment Level)**
One paragraph per content pillar that connects a specific market problem to your differentiated perspective. These appear in thought leadership, PR pitches, and keynote themes.
- Derived from: Positioning → segment-specific positioning + content pillars
- Example: "Cloud-native environments create blind spots that traditional security tools miss. Runtime observability provides the visibility security teams need to detect and respond to threats in real time."

**Level 3: Proof Points (Persona Level)**
Specific evidence, data points, and stories that make pillar messages credible. These appear in case studies, research reports, and analyst briefings.
- Derived from: Win/Loss Analysis + customer evidence + original research

### The Awareness Messaging Test

Before launching awareness programs, test your messaging against these criteria:

1. **Problem resonance** — Would the target persona nod in recognition when they hear this message, even if they've never heard of your company?
2. **Distinctiveness** — Could a competitor say the same thing? If yes, it's not differentiated enough.
3. **Memorability** — Can someone recall the core idea after a single exposure? If it requires multiple paragraphs to explain, it's too complex for awareness.
4. **Emotional connection** — Does the message connect with something the persona cares about personally or professionally? Purely rational messages are forgettable.
5. **Authenticity** — Can your organization credibly deliver on this message? Street credibility is essential.

## Channel Strategy

### Channel Selection Principles

Select channels based on audience behavior, not internal preference. The Pragmatic approach starts with the market: where do your target personas go to learn, explore, and form opinions?

### Channel Categories

**Owned Channels** — You control the platform and the content
- Website and blog / content hub
- Email newsletter / owned media publication
- Podcast or video series
- Social media profiles (organic)
- Community forums or user groups

**Earned Channels** — Third parties amplify your message based on its merit
- Industry analyst relations (Gartner, Forrester, IDC, etc.)
- Media relations / PR (trade press, business press)
- Speaking engagements and conference keynotes
- Guest contributions to industry publications
- Peer-to-peer platforms (G2, TrustRadius, review sites)

**Paid Channels** — You pay for placement and reach
- Digital advertising (LinkedIn, programmatic display, search)
- Content syndication
- Sponsored events and webinars
- Print/digital trade publication ads
- Paid social amplification

### Channel Mix Guidance

For B2B awareness specifically:
- **Earned media is the highest-trust channel** — Analyst validation and media coverage carry more weight with B2B buyers than self-promotion
- **Owned media builds compounding value** — A strong content hub becomes a long-term awareness asset that grows over time through SEO and sharing
- **Paid media provides reach acceleration** — Use paid to amplify your best-performing owned and earned content, not as a primary awareness vehicle
- **Events create concentrated impact** — Industry events offer high-trust, face-to-face awareness opportunities but have limited reach. Select events where your target personas concentrate.
