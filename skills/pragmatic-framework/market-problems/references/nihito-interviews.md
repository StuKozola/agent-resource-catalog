# NIHITO Interviews: The Heart of Market Problems

## What NIHITO Means

NIHITO — "Nothing Important Happens In The Office" — is the foundational principle of the Pragmatic Framework's approach to market research. It means that the most valuable insights come from direct conversations with the market, not from internal meetings, opinion-driven roadmaps, or secondhand information filtered through sales.

NIHITO interviews are qualitative, open-ended conversations with customers, recent evaluators, and prospects. They are your primary discovery tool for uncovering market problems.

## What NIHITO Interviews Are Good At

- **Discovery** — understanding what problems exist and why they matter
- **Context** — providing the "why" behind quantitative data you already collect
- **Empathy** — building genuine understanding of your customer's world
- **Trend monitoring** — spotting shifts in the market before they show up in data
- **Competitive intelligence** — learning how customers perceive alternatives
- **Persona refinement** — deepening your understanding of who your buyers and users really are

## What NIHITO Interviews Are Not Good At

- **Validation at scale** — you can't talk to enough people to reach statistical significance through interviews alone. Use surveys and data for validation.
- **Predicting the future** — customers can tell you about problems they have, not problems they'll have next year
- **Designing solutions** — customers describe problems well but often propose solutions that aren't the best answer. Listen to the problem, not the proposed fix.

## Interview Structure

A well-structured NIHITO interview uses two types of questions:

### 1. Open-Ended Discovery Questions
These are broad questions designed to let the customer talk. You're listening for problems, pain points, workflows, and emotions.

**Core question library:**

About their world:
- "Walk me through a typical day/week in your role."
- "What are the biggest challenges you face in [relevant domain]?"
- "If you could wave a magic wand and fix one thing about [process/workflow], what would it be?"
- "What keeps you up at night professionally?"

About problems and pain:
- "What's the most frustrating part of [relevant process]?"
- "When was the last time [relevant problem] really cost you — in time, money, or headaches?"
- "What happens when [relevant process] goes wrong? What's the impact?"
- "How do you currently deal with [problem area]? What's your workaround?"

About buying and evaluation:
- "What was the catalyst that made you start looking for a solution like this?"
- "Once you realized you needed to investigate products like ours, what did you do first? Second? Third?"
- "Who else was involved in the decision?"
- "What criteria mattered most when evaluating options?"

About competition and alternatives:
- "Who do you consider our competitors, and what could we learn from them?"
- "What other tools or approaches have you tried for this problem?"
- "If you were looking for this type of product again, what would you do differently?"

About trends:
- "What trends do you see in your market right now?"
- "What's changing in your industry that affects how you do [relevant activity]?"
- "What do you think will be different about your job in two years?"

About your product (for current customers):
- "How do you feel about our products? Our service? Our company?"
- "What do you wish our product did that it doesn't do today?"
- "What feature do you use most? Least?"

### 2. Draw-Out Questions
These are follow-up questions that deepen the conversation when the customer touches on something interesting.

- "How long has this been happening?"
- "What did you do after that?"
- "Can you tell me more about that?"
- "What was the impact when that happened?"
- "You don't sound too convinced. Was there another problem?"
- "Why is that important to you specifically?"
- "What would happen if that problem weren't solved?"
- "How often does that come up?"
- "Who else is affected by that?"

The draw-out questions are crucial. When a customer gives a tentative answer or mentions something in passing, pursue it courteously. Some of the most important insights come from these follow-up moments.

## Who to Interview

### Target Selection Rules
- Do NOT let sales cherry-pick your interview list. They'll send you to their happiest customers, which gives you a skewed picture.
- Include a mix: current customers, recent wins, recent losses, and non-customers (see research-planning.md for ratios)
- For current customers, sample across segments: power users, light users, frustrated users, new users
- For evaluators, focus on the last 6 months — memories fade beyond that

### How to Recruit

**Current customers:**
- Ask customer success managers for introductions
- Leverage support ticket data to find customers who experienced specific problems
- Use product analytics to identify interesting usage patterns

**Recent evaluators:**
- Work with sales to get introductions, but be clear that you're doing research, not selling
- Frame it as: "We're trying to improve. Your perspective would be incredibly valuable."
- For losses, emphasize that the conversation is not about relitigating the deal

**Non-customers:**
- Industry conferences and trade show networking
- LinkedIn outreach (keep it brief, genuine, and offer value in return)
- Partner and channel introductions
- Customer advisory boards or user groups
- Industry analyst connections

### Scheduling
- Aim for 30-45 minute conversations
- Phone or video calls work well; in-person is ideal when possible
- Record the conversation (with permission) so you can focus on listening rather than note-taking
- Have a notetaker if possible, so the interviewer can maintain natural conversation flow

## Interview Best Practices

### Before the Interview
- Review any existing data about this customer/prospect (CRM notes, support tickets, usage data)
- Prepare your question guide but don't script it rigidly — let the conversation flow
- Set a clear internal goal for what you want to learn from this specific interview
- Test your recording setup

### During the Interview
- **Listen more than you talk.** An 80/20 rule (them talking 80% of the time) is a good target.
- **Don't pitch.** You're there to learn, not sell. If they ask about features, note it and redirect to their problems.
- **Don't lead.** Avoid questions like "Don't you think X is a problem?" Ask "What problems do you face with X?"
- **Don't react to the first interesting thing.** Let them finish their thought.
- **Follow the energy.** If they get animated about a topic, explore it. The emotion tells you about urgency.
- **Embrace silence.** After they answer, pause. Often they'll fill the silence with the most important insight.
- **Take verbatim notes on key phrases.** Customer language is gold for positioning later.
- **Ask about behavior, not hypotheticals.** "What did you do last time?" is better than "What would you do if?"

### After the Interview
- Write up notes within 24 hours while the conversation is fresh
- Capture key quotes verbatim (these will be powerful in internal presentations)
- Tag or categorize themes: problems mentioned, personas involved, competitive mentions, buying process steps
- Share highlights with your team — knowledge sharing is a key outcome of NIHITO
- Thank the interviewee and ask if you can call them back later with specific follow-up questions

Over time, you will build an advisory network of contacts who will help you make better decisions and give you the credibility to win internal arguments with data from the market.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Asking leading questions** — "Our product is great at X, right?" biases the answer.
2. **Talking about your product too early** — Focus on their world first, your product second (if at all).
3. **Interviewing only happy customers** — Losses and non-customers provide the most growth-relevant insights.
4. **Treating interviews as a one-time event** — NIHITO is a habit, not a project. Establish a regular cadence.
5. **Not documenting and sharing** — An interview that lives only in your head helps nobody. Write it up and share it.
6. **Reacting to individual interviews** — One customer's problem is an anecdote. Fifteen customers' shared problem is an insight. Wait for patterns.
7. **Confusing problems with solutions** — When a customer says "I need feature X," dig deeper: "What are you trying to accomplish? What happens without it?"
8. **Setting overly ambitious objectives** — A focused, narrow research cycle produces better results than trying to boil the ocean.
