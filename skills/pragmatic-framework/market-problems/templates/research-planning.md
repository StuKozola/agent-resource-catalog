# Research Planning for Market Problems

## Setting Research Objectives

The most common mistake in Market Problems research is setting objectives that are too broad. "Understand the market" or "find out what customers want" gives you no focus and produces scattered findings.

A good research objective is **specific, bounded, and actionable**. It answers: "What decision will this research help us make?"

**Examples of weak objectives:**
- "Learn about our customers"
- "Understand the competitive landscape"
- "Find out why sales are declining"

**Examples of strong objectives:**
- "Identify the top 3 unmet workflow problems for compliance officers at mid-market banks when onboarding new institutional clients"
- "Understand why recent evaluators in the healthcare vertical chose competitors over us in Q3-Q4"
- "Discover what problems drive procurement managers at manufacturing firms to begin searching for supply chain visibility solutions"

### Framing Your Objective

Use this structure:
> "We want to understand [what specific aspect] for [which persona/segment] in the context of [what domain or workflow] so that we can [what decision or action]."

**Example:**
> "We want to understand the pain points around data reconciliation for operations analysts at asset management firms in the context of their month-end close process so that we can prioritize features for our next release cycle."

## Identifying Interview Targets

The Pragmatic Framework identifies four categories of interview targets, each providing different types of insight:

### 1. Current Customers
- **What they reveal:** How your product is actually used, what workarounds they've built, what frustrations persist, feature gaps, and adjacent problems your product doesn't address
- **How to find them:** Customer success teams, support ticket data, power users identified through usage analytics
- **Watch out for:** Confirmation bias — current customers may tell you what they think you want to hear. Ask about problems broadly, not about your product specifically.

### 2. Recent Evaluators — Wins
- **What they reveal:** What problem drove the purchase decision, what the buying process looked like, who was involved, how they found you, what differentiated you
- **How to find them:** CRM data for closed-won deals in the last 6 months. Coordinate with sales to get introductions.
- **Watch out for:** Post-purchase rationalization. People justify their decisions after making them. Probe for the original trigger, not the polished narrative.

### 3. Recent Evaluators — Losses
- **What they reveal:** Where your product fell short, what competitors did better, what was missing from your solution, whether price, features, or trust was the deciding factor
- **How to find them:** CRM data for closed-lost deals. This requires a trusting relationship with sales — reassure them that you're evaluating the process and market fit, not the salesperson.
- **Watch out for:** They may be reluctant to be candid. Emphasize that the conversation is about improving the product, not rehashing the sale.

### 4. Non-Customers / Untapped Prospects
- **What they reveal:** Problems in segments you haven't penetrated, how they think about the problem space, what solutions they currently use, whether they even know your category exists
- **How to find them:** Industry conferences, trade associations, LinkedIn research, customer advisory networks, partner introductions
- **Watch out for:** Without an existing relationship, these interviews are harder to schedule. Offer something of value in return — insights, a report summary, or simply the chance to influence a product's direction.

### Target Mix

For a typical Market Problems research cycle, aim for a mix across all four categories. A common ratio:
- 30% current customers
- 25% recent wins
- 25% recent losses
- 20% non-customers

The exact mix depends on your research objective. If you're exploring a new market, weight toward non-customers. If you're trying to reduce churn, weight toward recent losses and current customers with declining usage.

## Getting Operational Buy-In

One of the most powerful (and often overlooked) steps in Market Problems research is securing buy-in from other teams before you begin.

### Why It Matters
If you conduct research in isolation, you'll face resistance when you share findings. Sales will say "we've heard differently." Development will question the methodology. Leadership will want to know why they weren't consulted.

### How to Do It
Before starting your research:

1. **Talk to a sales lead** — Ask what they want to know. What patterns are they seeing? What objections keep coming up? What do they wish they understood better about why deals are won or lost?

2. **Talk to someone in development** — Ask what assumptions they're making about users. What do they wish they knew about how the product is actually used? What technical constraints would benefit from user context?

3. **Talk to a support or customer success lead** — Ask what the most common complaints are. What causes the most escalations? Where are users getting stuck?

4. **Talk to leadership** — Ask what strategic questions they need answered. What market moves are they considering? What segments are they curious about?

By involving these stakeholders early, when you share your research later, they're already invested. They helped shape the questions, so they trust the answers.

## Building a Research Timeline

Market Problems research is not a one-time event — it should be an ongoing practice. But for a focused research initiative, plan for:

- **Week 1-2:** Define objectives, identify targets, build interview guide, schedule interviews
- **Week 3-6:** Conduct interviews (aim for 15-30 across your target mix)
- **Week 7-8:** Synthesize findings, identify patterns, draft problem statements
- **Week 8-10:** Design and run validation survey
- **Week 10-12:** Compile final report, present findings, integrate into product strategy

For ongoing practice, establish a cadence of 2-4 NIHITO interviews per month per product. Product managers should be sharing market knowledge with their team on at least a monthly basis.

## Research Dos and Don'ts

**Do:**
- Tie your research to the strategic goals of the business
- Include a mix of interview target types
- Set specific, bounded objectives
- Get buy-in from cross-functional partners before starting
- Plan for both discovery (interviews) and validation (surveys/data)

**Don't:**
- React to the first interview or second interview — wait for patterns
- Let sales dictate who you talk to (they'll send you to happy customers)
- Try to cover everything in one research cycle
- Skip the planning phase and jump straight to interviews
- Keep findings in your head — document and share them
