# Channel Types Reference

This reference covers the major distribution channel types, their characteristics, and when to use each. Use this to help users evaluate which channels align with their market's buying preferences.

## Direct Distribution Channels

Direct distribution means the company sells straight to the end customer with no intermediaries.

### Company Website / E-Commerce
- **Best for**: Products with straightforward purchasing decisions, SaaS, digital products, DTC brands
- **Pros**: Full control over brand experience and pricing, highest margins, direct customer data, fast iteration on messaging
- **Cons**: Requires investment in marketing to drive traffic, customer acquisition cost can be high, must handle fulfillment and support
- **Buyer preference signal**: Buyers who research independently, prefer self-service, and are comfortable purchasing online

### Direct Sales Team (Field or Inside Sales)
- **Best for**: Complex, high-value B2B products; enterprise software; solutions requiring customization or consultative selling
- **Pros**: Deep customer relationships, ability to handle complex deals, control over the sales narrative, opportunity for upsell/cross-sell
- **Cons**: Expensive (salary, commission, travel, benefits), limited geographic reach, longer sales cycles
- **Buyer preference signal**: Buyers who need to evaluate risk, require demonstrations, involve multiple stakeholders in decisions, and expect a trusted advisor

### Company-Owned Stores / Showrooms
- **Best for**: Premium consumer products, experiential brands, products that benefit from hands-on evaluation
- **Pros**: Immersive brand experience, direct customer feedback, full control over merchandising
- **Cons**: High fixed costs (rent, staff, inventory), limited geographic scalability
- **Buyer preference signal**: Buyers who want to see, touch, or try before buying; those who value premium in-store experiences

### Inside Sales / Telesales
- **Best for**: Mid-market B2B, transactional enterprise sales, renewals and expansion
- **Pros**: Lower cost than field sales, faster deal cycles, scalable
- **Cons**: Less effective for highly complex or relationship-driven sales, limited for products requiring physical demonstration
- **Buyer preference signal**: Buyers comfortable with phone/video interactions, mid-complexity purchases where some guidance is needed but a full in-person engagement isn't required

## Indirect Distribution Channels

Indirect distribution involves third-party intermediaries who sell on the company's behalf.

### Value-Added Resellers (VARs)
- **Best for**: Technology products that benefit from integration services, customization, or bundling with complementary solutions
- **Pros**: Partners add value through services and expertise, expand geographic reach, bring existing customer relationships
- **Cons**: Less control over pricing and customer experience, margin sharing, dependency on partner capabilities
- **Buyer preference signal**: Buyers who want a complete solution rather than a standalone product, who need implementation help, or who already have trusted technology advisors

### Distributors / Wholesalers
- **Best for**: Physical products with broad market demand, products requiring warehousing and logistics infrastructure
- **Pros**: Bulk handling, established logistics networks, existing retailer relationships, risk sharing on inventory
- **Cons**: Additional margin layer, less direct customer insight, potential brand dilution
- **Buyer preference signal**: Retailers or businesses that prefer to source from established distributors rather than individual manufacturers

### Online Marketplaces (Amazon, Alibaba, AWS Marketplace, Salesforce AppExchange, etc.)
- **Best for**: Products competing in established categories, products with strong brand awareness, SaaS products seeking discovery
- **Pros**: Massive built-in buyer traffic, trust and credibility of the platform, reduced customer acquisition costs, established purchasing infrastructure
- **Cons**: High competition, platform fees and commissions, limited brand differentiation, dependency on platform rules and algorithms
- **Buyer preference signal**: Buyers who start their search on marketplaces, who prefer the convenience and trust of established platforms, and who comparison-shop across vendors

### Retail Partners
- **Best for**: Consumer products targeting mass market or specific retail demographics
- **Pros**: Physical presence, impulse purchase opportunities, retailer brand association
- **Cons**: Slotting fees, margin pressure, loss of direct customer relationship, retailer control over merchandising
- **Buyer preference signal**: Consumers who prefer to shop in stores, who make purchases as part of broader shopping trips, or who want instant product access

### System Integrators (SIs)
- **Best for**: Enterprise technology that requires complex implementation, integration with existing systems, and ongoing professional services
- **Pros**: Access to large enterprise deals, credibility by association, deep implementation expertise
- **Cons**: Long sales cycles, heavy reliance on SI's priorities and capacity, margin and influence sharing
- **Buyer preference signal**: Large enterprises that rely on consulting firms and SIs for technology decisions and implementation

### Independent Agents / Brokers
- **Best for**: Products entering new geographic markets, industries with established agent networks (insurance, real estate, manufacturing)
- **Pros**: Cost-effective market entry (commission-based), existing relationships in target markets, local knowledge
- **Cons**: Agents represent multiple products (split attention), less control, variable quality
- **Buyer preference signal**: Buyers in industries or regions where agents are the norm and trusted intermediaries

### OEM / Embedded Partnerships
- **Best for**: Components, modules, or technology that can be embedded into another company's product
- **Pros**: Volume through partner's sales, reduced go-to-market cost, deep integration creates switching costs
- **Cons**: Brand is often invisible to the end user, dependency on partner's success, pricing pressure
- **Buyer preference signal**: End users who buy a complete product and may not know or care about individual components; the "buyer" here is really the OEM partner

## Hybrid Distribution

Many companies use a mix of direct and indirect channels to serve different segments or stages.

### Common Hybrid Patterns
- **Direct for enterprise, indirect for SMB** — use your sales team for large accounts and partners/marketplaces for smaller ones
- **Direct for new markets, indirect for mature ones** — enter a market directly to learn, then add partners for scale
- **Online direct + retail indirect** — sell through your own website and through retail partners simultaneously
- **Freemium/self-service + sales-assisted** — let small customers self-serve while offering sales support for larger deals

### Managing Channel Conflict in Hybrid Models
When using multiple channels, conflict is inevitable. Common mitigation strategies:
- **Segment-based routing** — assign specific segments or account sizes to specific channels
- **Geographic territories** — divide markets by region between direct and indirect
- **Deal registration** — give partners who register opportunities protection from direct competition
- **Differentiated offerings** — offer slightly different product bundles or editions through different channels
- **Transparent rules of engagement** — publish clear policies about how leads and accounts are allocated

## Channel Selection Matrix

When evaluating channels, score each option against these criteria (1-5 scale):

| Criterion | Question |
|-----------|----------|
| Buyer Alignment | Does this channel match how the target segment prefers to buy? |
| Reach | Can this channel access the target segments geographically and demographically? |
| Economics | Are the margins viable after accounting for channel costs? |
| Control | How much control do we retain over messaging, pricing, and experience? |
| Scalability | Can this channel grow with our business? |
| Speed to Market | How quickly can we begin selling through this channel? |
| Competitive Position | Does this channel offer differentiation from competitors? |
| Partner Viability | For indirect channels: are capable partners available and motivated? |

Score each candidate channel, then weight the criteria based on what matters most for your specific situation. Buyer alignment should almost always carry the highest weight — per the Pragmatic Framework's outside-in philosophy.
