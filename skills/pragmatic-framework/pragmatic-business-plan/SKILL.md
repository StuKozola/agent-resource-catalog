# Pragmatic Framework: Business Plan Activity

## What This Skill Does

This skill guides the creation of a **Product Business Plan** as defined by the Pragmatic Framework — the "Business Plan" box in the BUSINESS row of the framework. It helps product managers perform an objective, market-driven analysis of a product opportunity to justify investment decisions.

## Context Within the Pragmatic Framework

The Business Plan sits in the **BUSINESS** row of the Pragmatic Framework, alongside Pricing, Buy/Build/Partner, Product Profitability, and Innovation. It bridges the strategic work done in **MARKET** (Market Problems, Win/Loss Analysis, Distinctive Competencies, Competitive Landscape, Asset Assessment) and **FOCUS** (Market Definition, Distribution Strategy, Product Portfolio, Product Roadmap) with the tactical execution in PLANNING, PROGRAMS, ENABLEMENT, and SUPPORT.

The official Pragmatic Institute definition:

> **Business Plan**: Perform an objective analysis of a potential market opportunity to provide a basis for investment. Articulate what you learned in the market and quantify the risk, including a financial model.

The Business Plan is NOT a traditional corporate business plan or a startup pitch deck. It is a **product-level investment justification** that synthesizes market evidence into a clear recommendation.

## Key Responsibilities (from Pragmatic Framework Roles)

- Perform an objective analysis of a potential market opportunity to provide a basis for investment
- Articulate what you learned in the market and quantify the risk, including a financial model
- Make a go/no-go recommendation based on the data

## Required Skills for This Activity

- Written and verbal communications
- Presentation abilities
- Analytical capability — gathering data, synthesizing it, and telling a story
- Strategic thinking
- Internal influencing
- Financial acumen (forecasting, market share, top and bottom line)

## Pragmatic Principles That Guide the Business Plan

These principles from the Pragmatic Framework should shape every business plan:

1. **Outside-in approach** — The plan is built from market evidence, not internal opinions. As Pragmatic teaches: "Your opinion, although interesting, is irrelevant."
2. **The answer is not in the building** — Data comes from interviews with customers, recent evaluators, and untapped potential customers.
3. **Market-driven, not sales-driven** — The plan addresses market segments, not individual deals.
4. **Problem-oriented** — The plan is anchored to validated, urgent market problems — not features or technology.
5. **Distinctive competence matters** — The plan must articulate why *this* organization is uniquely positioned to solve the problem.

## When to Create a Product Business Plan

Product business plans are needed when:

- Evaluating a new product opportunity before committing development resources
- Justifying continued or increased investment in an existing product
- Making a go/no-go decision on entering a new market segment
- Presenting a market opportunity to leadership or investors for funding
- Comparing multiple product opportunities to decide where to allocate resources

All business plans within an organization should follow the same format and objectives so they can be compared on equal footing.

## Document Structure

Read `references/business-plan-template.md` for the full template with section-by-section guidance. Below is a summary of the structure.

A Pragmatic Product Business Plan should be **concise and action-oriented** — typically 4-8 pages or a focused presentation deck. Pragmatic Institute advocates for agile planning documentation that is manageable, predictable, complete, and current. The format can range from a Lean Canvas-style single page to a short presentation, depending on organizational culture.

### Required Sections

1. **Executive Summary** — One-page overview with the recommendation (go/no-go/conditional), the market opportunity in brief, and key financial highlights.

2. **Market Opportunity** — What market problems did you discover? How pervasive and urgent are they? This section synthesizes findings from the Market Problems and Win/Loss Analysis activities.

3. **Target Market & Segmentation** — Which segments will you pursue? Are they large enough to sustain the business? Draws from the Market Definition activity.

4. **Distinctive Competence & Competitive Position** — Why is your organization uniquely able to solve this problem? What is the competitive landscape? Synthesizes the Distinctive Competencies, Competitive Landscape, and Asset Assessment activities.

5. **Solution Overview** — High-level description of the proposed solution tied to market problems (not a feature list). References the Product Roadmap and Buy/Build/Partner considerations.

6. **Financial Model & Risk Quantification** — Revenue projections, cost estimates, key assumptions, break-even analysis, and an honest quantification of risks. This is where you make the numbers tell the market story.

7. **Go/No-Go Recommendation** — A clear, data-backed recommendation with conditions and next steps.

### Optional Sections (depending on organizational needs)

- Distribution strategy alignment
- Pricing strategy summary (cross-reference with the Pricing activity)
- Resource requirements and timeline
- Key milestones and decision gates

## How to Use This Skill

When the user asks for help with a Pragmatic Business Plan, follow this workflow:

### Step 1: Understand Context
Ask what stage they're at:
- Starting from scratch with a new product idea?
- Documenting an existing opportunity they've already researched?
- Reviewing/improving a draft business plan?
- Need the template to fill in themselves?

### Step 2: Gather Inputs
A Pragmatic Business Plan synthesizes outputs from earlier framework activities. Ask what the user already has:
- Market Problems documentation (interview findings, validated problems)
- Win/Loss Analysis data
- Market Definition / segmentation work
- Competitive Landscape analysis
- Distinctive Competencies assessment
- Asset Assessment
- Product Roadmap or vision
- Any existing financial data or projections

If the user doesn't have these, help them think through each area with guided questions rather than skipping them.

### Step 3: Build the Document
Read `references/business-plan-template.md` for the detailed template, then create the business plan document. Use the docx skill if the user wants a Word document, or create a markdown document for simpler needs.

### Step 4: Review Against Pragmatic Principles
Before finalizing, validate the plan against these checkpoints:
- Is every claim backed by market evidence, not internal opinion?
- Are market problems described from the customer's perspective?
- Is the financial model built on stated, testable assumptions?
- Does the distinctive competence argument hold up against alternatives?
- Is the recommendation clear and actionable?
- Could leadership compare this plan against other product opportunities?

## Common Pitfalls

- **Leading with the solution** — The plan should lead with the market problem, not the product idea. Technology is a means to an end.
- **Skipping market evidence** — A business plan without customer interview data is just a guess dressed up in a spreadsheet.
- **Overly optimistic financials** — Pragmatic teaches quantifying risk, not just upside. Include realistic scenarios and clearly state assumptions.
- **Too long or too detailed** — This is an investment decision document, not an encyclopedia. Keep it focused on what leadership needs to make a decision.
- **Confusing features with value** — Describe the product's ability to solve market problems, not its technical specifications.
- **Ignoring the competition** — Even if there's no direct competitor, there are always alternative ways customers solve their problem today (including doing nothing).
