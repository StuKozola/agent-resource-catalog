# Pragmatic Framework: Product Business Plan Template

This template provides section-by-section guidance for creating a product business plan aligned with the Pragmatic Framework. Each section includes what to include, why it matters, guiding questions, and examples of strong vs weak content.

## Table of Contents

2. Market Opportunity

4. Distinctive Competence & Competitive Position

## 1. Executive Summary

**Purpose:** Give leadership everything they need to understand the opportunity in one page. Many executives will read only this section before deciding whether to dig deeper.

**What to include:**
- Product or opportunity name
- One-sentence description of the market problem being solved
- Target market segment(s) and estimated market size
- Why your organization is positioned to win (distinctive competence in brief)
- Key financial highlights: projected revenue, investment required, expected break-even timeline
- Clear recommendation: Go, No-Go, or Conditional (with conditions stated)

**Guidance:**
- Write this section LAST, after all other sections are complete
- Keep it to one page maximum
- Lead with the market problem, not the product
- Include specific numbers — vague statements like "large market opportunity" are not useful for decision-making

**Strong example opening:** "Mid-market manufacturing companies (5,000+ in the US alone) lack real-time visibility into supply chain disruptions, costing them an average of $2.3M annually in expediting fees and lost production. Based on 47 customer interviews and analysis of 12 recent evaluators, we recommend a $1.2M investment to develop [Product], targeting $4.8M ARR within 24 months."

**Weak example opening:** "We have an exciting opportunity to build a supply chain platform that leverages cutting-edge AI to transform how manufacturers operate."

## 2. Market Opportunity

**Purpose:** Demonstrate that a real, validated, urgent market problem exists and is worth solving. This section is the heart of the outside-in approach.

**What to include:**

### 2a. Market Problems Discovered
- The top 3-5 problems discovered through market interviews
- For each problem: who experiences it, how frequently, and what impact it has
- Evidence of urgency — are people actively looking for solutions?
- How many interviews/data points support each problem (be specific)

### 2b. Problem Validation
- How many customers, recent evaluators, and potential customers were interviewed
- Percentage or count who confirmed each problem as urgent
- Any quantitative data on the cost or impact of the problem
- Quotes or anonymized anecdotes that bring the problem to life (sparingly — 2-3 max)

### 2c. Win/Loss Insights
- Why recent evaluators chose your product (if existing) or a competitor's
- What steps buyers took in their evaluation process
- Key barriers or objections encountered
- Patterns in buying behavior relevant to this opportunity

**Guiding questions:**
- What are the top problems you heard in customer interviews?
- How many people did you talk to, and how many confirmed each problem?
- Can you quantify the cost of the problem to the customer?
- What are buyers doing today to work around the problem?
- What triggers a buyer to start looking for a solution?

**Pitfall to avoid:** Don't just list features customers asked for. Translate feature requests back into the underlying problems. "Customers want a dashboard" is a feature request. "Operations managers can't identify bottlenecks until they've already caused delays" is a market problem.

## 3. Target Market & Segmentation

**Purpose:** Define exactly who you're building for and prove the market is large enough to sustain the investment.

**What to include:**

### 3a. Segment Definition
- Primary target segment(s) defined by shared problems (problem-based segmentation, not just demographics)
- For each segment: industry, company size, geography, role/persona, and most importantly, the specific problems they share
- Why these segments were chosen over others

### 3b. Market Sizing
- Total Addressable Market (TAM): The full universe of potential buyers
- Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM): The portion you can realistically reach
- Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM): Your realistic near-term capture target
- State the methodology and sources for each number
- Confirm segments are large enough to support current AND future business

### 3c. Segment Prioritization
- If multiple segments exist, which is the beachhead and why?
- What is the sequence for addressing additional segments?
- What is the rationale for prioritization (urgency, size, accessibility, strategic fit)?

**Guiding questions:**
- How do you define your target segments — by problem, industry, size, or some combination?
- How many potential buyers exist in each segment?
- Is the segment growing, stable, or shrinking?
- Can you reach this segment with your current distribution and sales model?
- Is this segment large enough to hit your financial targets?

## 4. Distinctive Competence & Competitive Position

**Purpose:** Make the case that your organization is uniquely able to win in this market, and honestly assess the competitive landscape.

**What to include:**

### 4a. Distinctive Competencies
- What unique capabilities, assets, expertise, or relationships does your organization have?
- How do these competencies directly enable solving the identified market problems?
- Are these competencies defensible — can competitors easily replicate them?

### 4b. Asset Assessment
- Relevant technical assets (patents, proprietary technology, infrastructure)
- Skills and expertise within the team
- Existing customer relationships or market presence
- Services, partnerships, or ecosystem advantages
- Data assets or network effects

### 4c. Competitive Landscape
- Direct competitors and their offerings
- Indirect competitors and alternative solutions (including "do nothing" or manual workarounds)
- For each key competitor: strengths, weaknesses, market position
- Your strategy for winning against each type of competitor
- Where competitors are strong and you should NOT compete

**Guiding questions:**
- What can your organization do that competitors cannot easily replicate?
- What assets do you have that give you a head start?
- Who are the top 3-5 alternatives a buyer would consider?
- What would a buyer say is each competitor's greatest strength?
- Where do you lose deals today, and why?

**Pitfall to avoid:** Don't claim you have "no competitors." Every market problem has alternatives, even if it's manual spreadsheets, outsourcing, or simply living with the problem. Honestly acknowledging the competitive reality builds credibility with leadership.

## 5. Solution Overview

**Purpose:** Describe what you intend to build (or have built), tied directly to the market problems identified.

**What to include:**

### 5a. Solution Description
- High-level description of the solution approach
- How it addresses each of the top market problems identified in Section 2
- What makes this approach better than the alternatives
- Key differentiators from the buyer's perspective (not technical features)

### 5b. Roadmap Alignment
- How does this fit within the existing product portfolio and roadmap?
- What are the key phases of delivery?
- What can be delivered in the near term vs. long term?
- Is this a plan, not a commitment (as Pragmatic teaches about roadmaps)?

### 5c. Buy, Build, or Partner
- For each component of the solution: will you build it in-house, acquire technology, or partner?
- Where do gaps exist in your current offering?
- What is the rationale for each make-vs-buy-vs-partner decision?

**Guidance:**
- Describe the product by its ability to solve market problems, not by its feature list
- Keep this section concise — this is a business plan, not a product requirements document
- Reference the roadmap for delivery details rather than duplicating them here

## 6. Financial Model & Risk Quantification

**Purpose:** Translate the market opportunity into numbers and honestly quantify the risks. This is where you prove the investment is sound AND where you demonstrate intellectual honesty about uncertainty.

**What to include:**

### 6a. Revenue Projections
- Projected revenue for 12, 24, and 36 months (minimum)
- Revenue model: how will you charge? (subscription, license, usage-based, etc.)
- Average deal size and projected number of customers by period
- Assumptions behind each projection — state these explicitly

### 6b. Cost Estimates
- Development costs (headcount, tools, infrastructure)
- Go-to-market costs (marketing, sales, channel)
- Ongoing operational costs
- Total investment required before break-even

### 6c. Financial Analysis
- Break-even timeline
- Gross margin projections
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC) and customer lifetime value (LTV) estimates
- Return on investment (ROI) calculation
- Comparison to company hurdle rate or investment thresholds

### 6d. Scenario Analysis
Present three scenarios to quantify the range of outcomes:
- **Optimistic:** What happens if adoption is faster than expected? What drives upside?
- **Base case:** The most likely scenario given current evidence. This is your primary projection.
- **Conservative:** What happens if adoption is slower, competition increases, or key assumptions prove wrong?

### 6e. Risk Quantification
This is critical and what distinguishes a Pragmatic Business Plan from wishful thinking.

For each major risk, document:
- **Risk description:** What could go wrong?
- **Likelihood:** High / Medium / Low (with reasoning)
- **Impact:** What would happen to the financial model if this risk materializes?
- **Mitigation:** What can you do to reduce the likelihood or impact?

Common risk categories to address:
- Market risk: Is the problem as pervasive as the data suggests?
- Technology risk: Can you actually build the solution as envisioned?
- Competitive risk: Could a competitor beat you to market or outspend you?
- Adoption risk: Will the target segment actually change behavior and buy?
- Execution risk: Does your team have the capacity and capability to deliver?
- Timing risk: Is the market ready now, or is this too early/too late?

### 6f. Key Assumptions
List every significant assumption underlying the financial model. For each:
- State the assumption clearly
- Provide the evidence supporting it
- Note what would change if the assumption is wrong
- Describe how and when you will validate the assumption

**Guiding questions:**
- What are the 3-5 assumptions that, if wrong, would kill this opportunity?
- What is the minimum viable outcome that still justifies the investment?
- How long before we know if this is working or not?
- What are the kill criteria — at what point should we stop and redirect?

**Pitfall to avoid:** Don't build a financial model that only works in the best case. Leadership needs to understand the realistic range of outcomes, and your credibility depends on presenting an honest assessment, not a sales pitch.

## 7. Go/No-Go Recommendation

**Purpose:** Make a clear, data-backed recommendation that enables a decision.

**What to include:**
- **Clear recommendation:** Go, No-Go, or Conditional
- **Summary of the case:** 3-5 bullet points with the most compelling evidence
- **If Go:** What resources are needed, what are the immediate next steps, and what are the first milestones?
- **If Conditional:** What conditions must be met before proceeding? What additional research or validation is needed?
- **If No-Go:** Why not, and what would have to change to reconsider?
- **Decision gates:** What future checkpoints should be built in to reassess the investment?

**Guidance:**
- Be direct. Don't hedge so much that the recommendation is unclear.
- The recommendation should flow naturally from the evidence presented. If it feels like a leap, something is missing from the analysis.
- Include kill criteria: under what circumstances should this investment be reconsidered or terminated?

## 8. Appendices (Optional)

Include supporting detail that doesn't belong in the main document but may be requested:
- Detailed financial model spreadsheets
- Customer interview summaries (anonymized)
- Competitive analysis detail
- Market sizing methodology
- Technical feasibility assessment
- Relevant industry research or analyst reports

## Formatting Guidelines

### For Short-Form Business Plans (4-5 pages or slide deck)
Best for organizations with streamlined funding processes or early-stage opportunity screening:
- Executive Summary: 1 page
- Market Opportunity + Segmentation: 1-2 pages
- Competitive Position + Solution: 1 page
- Financial Model + Recommendation: 1-2 pages

### For Comprehensive Business Plans (6-8 pages)
Best for organizations with formal funding processes, multiple stakeholders, or larger investments:
- Follow all sections above at full depth
- Include appendices with detailed supporting data

### For Lean Canvas / One-Page Format
When leadership wants the absolute essentials:
- Problem (top 3 market problems, with evidence)
- Solution (high-level approach)
- Key Metrics (projected revenue, cost, break-even)
- Unique Value Proposition (distinctive competence)
- Unfair Advantage (defensible assets)
- Customer Segments (target segments with sizing)
- Risks (top 3 risks with mitigation)
- Recommendation (Go/No-Go with conditions)

## Cross-References to Other Pragmatic Framework Activities

The Business Plan draws directly from these framework activities:

| Framework Activity | What It Contributes to the Business Plan |
|---|---|
| Market Problems | Validated problems that form the foundation of the opportunity |
| Win/Loss Analysis | Buyer behavior patterns and competitive insights |
| Distinctive Competencies | Why your organization is positioned to win |
| Competitive Landscape | Understanding of alternatives and competitive strategy |
| Asset Assessment | Inventory of leverageable assets |
| Market Definition | Target segment identification and sizing |
| Distribution Strategy | Channel alignment for reaching the target market |
| Product Portfolio | How this fits within the broader product portfolio |
| Product Roadmap | Delivery vision and phasing |
| Pricing | Revenue model and pricing strategy inputs |
| Buy, Build or Partner | Solution delivery strategy |

The Business Plan in turn feeds into:
- **Positioning** — the business case informs how the product is described
- **Marketing Plan** — investment level determines program scope
- **Launch** — the business plan sets launch goals and success metrics
- **Product Profitability** — the financial model becomes the baseline for tracking performance
