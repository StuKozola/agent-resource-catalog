# Innovation Brief Template

The Innovation Brief is the core deliverable of the Innovation activity. It connects a validated market problem to a proposed innovation approach, grounded in the organization's distinctive competencies. Think of it as the "pitch document" for an innovation — it makes the case that this idea is worth pursuing because it solves a real problem using what the organization is uniquely good at.

## When to Use

Create an Innovation Brief when:
- A team has identified a potential innovation opportunity and needs to articulate it clearly
- A product manager needs to present an innovation idea to leadership for prioritization
- An innovation needs to be evaluated against other opportunities in the portfolio
- The team wants to move from "interesting idea" to "actionable proposal"

## How to Guide the User

Walk through each section below. For each section, ask the user the guiding questions and help them draft clear, concise answers. Push back if answers are vague or disconnected from market evidence.

---

## Innovation Brief Structure

### 1. Market Problem Statement
*What pain are we addressing, and for whom?*

Guide the user to write a crisp problem statement that includes:
- **Who** has this problem (specific persona or segment)
- **What** the problem is (in the customer's language, not technical jargon)
- **Why** it matters (consequences of the problem going unsolved)
- **How urgent** it is (is the pain acute and immediate, or a slow burn?)
- **How pervasive** it is (rough estimate of how many in the segment face this)

**Evidence required**: Reference specific customer interviews, win/loss data, or market research. If the user can't point to external evidence, this is a red flag — the problem may be assumed rather than validated.

Example:
> Mid-market IT directors (our primary buyer persona) spend an average of 12 hours per week manually reconciling security alerts across 4+ disconnected tools. This leads to delayed incident response (avg 6 hours) and compliance gaps. In 23 of 30 customer interviews conducted in Q2, this was cited as a top-3 pain point. Three recent losses specifically cited our lack of a unified view as the deciding factor.

### 2. Distinctive Competency Alignment
*Why are we uniquely positioned to solve this?*

Guide the user to connect the innovation to specific PRIME attributes:
- Which of the organization's distinctive competencies directly apply?
- What makes the org's approach to this problem different from what competitors could do?
- What existing assets (technology, data, expertise, relationships) can be leveraged?

**The test**: If any well-funded competitor could build the same thing just as easily, the innovation may not be well-aligned with distinctive competencies. That doesn't mean don't do it — but it changes the risk profile.

Example:
> Our patented correlation engine (Method) processes heterogeneous log data 40x faster than competitors. Our 8-year history of SOC partnerships (Reputation) gives us access to threat intelligence that new entrants can't replicate. Our security research team of 15 PhDs (Expertise) has published 30+ papers on automated alert triage.

### 3. Innovation Description
*What are we proposing to build or do differently?*

Guide the user to describe the innovation clearly:
- What is it? (product feature, new product, service, process change, packaging change, etc.)
- What type of innovation is it? (incremental, adjacent, or transformational)
- How does it solve the stated market problem?
- What would the user experience look like for the target persona?

Keep this grounded and specific. Avoid buzzword-laden descriptions. A good test: could a reasonably informed person read this and understand what you're actually building?

### 4. Competitive Context
*How does this compare to what else is available?*

Guide the user to position the innovation against alternatives:
- What are customers doing today to address this problem? (including manual workarounds)
- What competitive products address this? How well?
- What gap does this innovation fill that nothing else does?
- How defensible is this advantage? (How long before competitors could catch up?)

### 5. Expected Value
*What outcomes do we expect for customers and for the business?*

Guide the user to articulate value in concrete terms:

**Customer value**:
- How will the target persona's life improve? (time saved, cost reduced, risk mitigated, etc.)
- What metrics would indicate success from the customer's perspective?

**Business value**:
- Revenue impact (new revenue, retention, upsell, competitive win rate)
- Strategic positioning (market leadership, new segment entry, platform expansion)
- Rough order-of-magnitude sizing (doesn't need to be precise at this stage)

### 6. Key Risks and Assumptions
*What could go wrong, and what are we assuming?*

Guide the user to be honest about:
- Technical risks (can we actually build this?)
- Market risks (will customers actually adopt/pay for this?)
- Competitive risks (could someone else get here first?)
- Organizational risks (do we have the skills, budget, and executive support?)
- Key assumptions that need validation before heavy investment

### 7. Recommended Next Steps
*What should happen next to move this forward?*

Typical next steps might include:
- Additional customer validation (prototype testing, concept interviews)
- Technical feasibility assessment
- Business case / financial modeling (feeds into the Business Plan activity)
- Executive review and prioritization decision
- Placement on the product roadmap

## Quality Checklist

Before considering the brief complete, verify:

- [ ] The market problem is backed by external evidence, not just internal opinion
- [ ] There's a clear line from market problem → distinctive competency → innovation approach
- [ ] The innovation type (incremental/adjacent/transformational) is identified and appropriate
- [ ] Customer value is described in the customer's terms, not the org's terms
- [ ] Key risks are acknowledged honestly, not minimized
- [ ] Next steps are concrete and actionable
- [ ] The brief is concise enough that a busy executive could read it in 10 minutes
