## 1. Interview Methodology

User persona interviews follow a NIHITO-inspired approach (Nothing Important Happens In The Office) adapted for user understanding rather than market problem discovery. The goal is to understand the user's world, not to validate product assumptions.

### Interview Structure

**Opening (5 minutes)**
- Thank the participant, explain the purpose (understanding their experience, not selling anything)
- Get consent for recording
- Set expectations: there are no right or wrong answers; you want to understand their reality

**Context Setting (10 minutes)**
- Understand their role, environment, and daily workflow
- Establish the landscape before diving into specifics

**Core Exploration (25-35 minutes)**
- Goals and motivations
- Current behaviors and workflows
- Pain points and frustrations
- Tools and environment
- Attitudes and emotional responses

**Wrap-Up (5 minutes)**
- Ask if there's anything you didn't cover that they think is important
- Ask who else you should talk to (snowball recruiting)
- Thank them and explain next steps

### Interview Best Practices

**Do:**
- Ask open-ended questions ("Tell me about..." "Walk me through..." "What happens when...")
- Follow up with "Why?" and "Can you give me an example?"
- Listen for stories and specific examples, not generalizations
- Pay attention to emotional language (frustrated, excited, confused, relieved)
- Note contradictions between what they say and what they describe doing
- Let silences breathe — users often fill them with deeper insights

**Don't:**
- Ask leading questions ("Don't you think it would be better if...")
- Pitch features or solutions during the interview
- Interview in groups (unless doing a focus group intentionally)
- Use jargon or internal product terminology
- Rush through questions to fit a schedule
- Assume you know what they mean — always ask for specifics

## 2. Interview Question Framework

Questions organized by persona dimension. Select and adapt questions based on your research objectives.

### Goals & Motivations
- "What are you ultimately trying to accomplish in your role?"
- "When you use [product/category], what are you trying to get done?"
- "What does a really good day look like for you at work? What makes it good?"
- "What would change for you if you could solve [relevant problem] completely?"
- "How do you define success in [relevant task area]?"

### Current Workflow & Behaviors
- "Walk me through a typical day. Where does [product/task area] fit in?"
- "When you need to [relevant task], what's the first thing you do?"
- "What tools do you use alongside [product/category]? How do they connect?"
- "How has your approach to [task] changed since you started?"
- "What shortcuts or workarounds have you figured out?"

### Pain Points & Frustrations
- "What's the most frustrating part of [relevant task area]?"
- "Tell me about a time when things went wrong with [task/product]."
- "What takes longer than it should?"
- "If you could wave a magic wand and fix one thing, what would it be?"
- "What do you wish someone had told you when you first started doing this?"

### Environment & Context
- "Where do you typically do this work? Describe your setup."
- "Who else is involved in [relevant workflow]? How do you hand off or collaborate?"
- "What interruptions or distractions do you deal with while doing this work?"
- "What other systems or tools are open on your screen while you use [product]?"
- "How much time pressure are you under when doing this task?"

### Skills & Experience
- "How did you learn to do [relevant task]? Formal training, self-taught, peer learning?"
- "On a scale of novice to expert, how would you rate your comfort with [domain/product]?"
- "What was the hardest thing to learn?"
- "How do you stay current with changes in [domain/product category]?"

### Attitudes & Emotions
- "How do you feel about [product category] in general?"
- "What excites you about the direction things are headed in [domain]?"
- "What worries you most about [relevant change or trend]?"
- "When you encounter a problem with [product/task], what's your first reaction? Who do you turn to?"

### Future-Looking
- "If you imagine your work two years from now, what's different?"
- "What would make you switch to a completely different approach or tool?"
- "What advice would you give to someone just starting in your role?"

## 3. Contextual Inquiry Methodology

Contextual inquiry means observing users in their actual environment while they perform real tasks. It reveals behaviors, workarounds, and environmental factors that users cannot or will not articulate in interviews.

### Planning the Observation

- Schedule during a time when the user will be doing relevant work (not a staged demo)
- Ask for permission to observe their workspace and screen
- Bring a note-taker so the observer can focus on watching
- Plan for 60-120 minutes; the first 30 minutes of "normal behavior" may be performative

### During the Observation

**Watch for:**
- Physical environment: desk setup, monitors, noise, interruptions, collaboration spaces
- Digital environment: what other applications are open, how they switch between tools
- Workarounds: sticky notes, spreadsheets, manual steps that should be automated
- Collaboration: who do they talk to, how do they share information
- Emotional cues: sighing, hesitation, excitement, speed changes
- Error recovery: what happens when something goes wrong

**Inquiry technique:**
- Observe silently for extended periods, then ask clarifying questions
- "I noticed you just did X — can you tell me why?"
- "You seemed to pause there — what were you thinking?"
- "That seemed quick/slow — is that typical?"

### After the Observation

- Debrief immediately while details are fresh
- Document the environment layout, tool ecosystem, and workflow sequence
- Note surprises — things you didn't expect
- Compare across multiple observations for patterns

## 4. Empathy Mapping

Empathy maps are a structured way to synthesize research observations into a visual format that builds shared understanding across teams. They are a bridge between raw research data and persona creation.

### The Four Quadrants

**SAYS** — Direct quotes from the user during interviews or observations
- Capture actual words, not paraphrased versions
- Include tone and emphasis where relevant
- Example: "I just need it to work without me having to think about it"

**THINKS** — What the user is thinking but may not say aloud
- Inferred from behavior, hesitation, contradictions
- What matters to them that they didn't explicitly state
- What are they worried about but reluctant to share?
- Example: "I wonder if there's a faster way to do this but I don't want to ask and look stupid"

**DOES** — Observable behaviors and actions
- Specific actions, not generalizations
- Include workarounds, habits, and patterns
- Example: "Exports data to Excel to manipulate it before putting it back"

**FEELS** — Emotional state during the experience
- Frustration, confusion, satisfaction, anxiety, confidence
- Note what triggers emotional responses
- Example: "Anxious when the system is slow because it might lose their work"

### Additional Areas (Optional)

**PAINS** — Obstacles, frustrations, and fears
- What keeps the user from achieving their goals?
- What risks do they face?

**GAINS** — Goals, desires, and measures of success
- What does winning look like for this user?
- What would delight them?

### Empathy Mapping Workshop Format

1. **Prepare:** Print or project the four-quadrant template. Have sticky notes and markers ready.
2. **Review research:** Walk through 2-3 interview summaries or observation notes as a team.
3. **Individual mapping (10 min):** Each participant writes observations on sticky notes and places them in quadrants.
4. **Group synthesis (20 min):** Cluster related sticky notes. Discuss patterns and disagreements.
5. **Identify gaps (10 min):** Which quadrants are sparse? Where do you need more research?
6. **Capture insights (10 min):** Document the key themes and surprising findings.

Create one empathy map per suspected user type. These become the foundation for persona creation.

## 5. Analyzing and Coding Research Data

### Affinity Diagramming

After completing interviews and observations, use affinity diagramming to identify patterns:

1. **Extract data points:** Write each distinct observation, quote, or behavior on a separate card or sticky note
2. **Cluster naturally:** Group related items together without pre-defined categories
3. **Name clusters:** Label each group with a descriptive theme
4. **Identify higher-order patterns:** Look for clusters that group together into behavioral archetypes
5. **Map to persona hypotheses:** Each distinct behavioral archetype is a candidate persona

### Behavioral Variables

Code your research data along these behavioral dimensions to identify natural clusters:

- **Activity level:** How frequently and intensely they engage with the product/domain
- **Motivation type:** Intrinsic (personal growth, satisfaction) vs. extrinsic (required by job, mandated by management)
- **Expertise trajectory:** Novice learning curve, developing proficiency, expert optimization
- **Attitude toward technology:** Eager adopter vs. reluctant user vs. pragmatic tool-user
- **Collaboration style:** Independent worker vs. team-oriented vs. manager/delegator
- **Problem-solving approach:** Methodical/process-driven vs. intuitive/exploratory

### Saturation Check

You've reached research saturation when:
- New interviews reveal patterns you've already identified
- You can predict how new participants will describe their goals and frustrations
- The behavioral clusters are stable and well-supported
- Typically occurs at 8-12 interviews per persona type

## 6. Common Research Pitfalls

**Confirmation bias:** Hearing what you expect to hear. Counter by having multiple researchers code data independently and compare.

**Availability bias:** Over-weighting the most recent or dramatic interview. Counter by systematically coding all interviews equally.

**Stakeholder projection:** Internal stakeholders projecting their own mental model of the user. Counter by grounding all persona decisions in direct user quotes and observations.

**The vocal minority:** Power users and community members are easiest to recruit but may not represent the majority. Counter by deliberately recruiting across usage intensity levels.

**Premature convergence:** Rushing to define personas before sufficient research. Counter by maintaining provisional personas and explicitly marking what's validated vs. hypothesized.

**Solution-mode interviews:** Slipping into discussing features instead of understanding the user. Counter by keeping interview guides focused on the user's world, not the product roadmap.
