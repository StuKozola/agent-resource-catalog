## 1. Assessing What You Already Know

Before planning new research, audit existing knowledge sources. In the Pragmatic Framework, upstream activities should have generated substantial market intelligence.

### Upstream Artifact Checklist

Review each artifact and extract user-relevant insights:

**Market Problems:**
- Which validated problems relate to product usage (vs. purchasing)?
- What urgency and pervasiveness data exists?
- Are there distinct problem clusters that suggest different user types?

**Market Definition:**
- What segments have been identified?
- How are segments characterized — by industry, role, company size, or behavior?
- Do segment profiles hint at different usage patterns?

**Win/Loss Analysis:**
- What did evaluators say about ease of use, workflow fit, or feature gaps?
- Did different evaluator types have different experience patterns?
- What steps did they take during evaluation (suggests workflow expectations)?

**Competitive Landscape:**
- How do users interact with competing or alternative solutions?
- What workarounds exist for users who lack a solution?

**Asset Assessment:**
- What technical assets or constraints shape the user experience?
- Are there platform or integration requirements that define the usage environment?

### Internal Stakeholder Knowledge

Interview internal stakeholders who interact with users:
- **Customer support** — recurring issues, common user profiles, frustration patterns
- **Sales** — what users say during demos and evaluations
- **Professional services/implementation** — onboarding challenges, user skill levels
- **Training** — learning curves, common misconceptions
- **Product development** — assumptions about users baked into current design

**Key insight from Pragmatic case studies:** Different internal teams often interact with different user types without realizing it. Customer support may hear from frustrated novices, while sales may interact with power users evaluating advanced features. These differing views of "the user" are an early signal that multiple personas exist.

### Existing Quantitative Data

Collect available usage analytics and survey data:
- Feature usage frequency and depth
- User session patterns (duration, frequency, time-of-day)
- Drop-off and abandonment points
- Net Promoter Score or satisfaction data segmented by user type
- Support ticket categorization and volume by user type

## 2. Identifying Knowledge Gaps

After auditing existing knowledge, identify what you still don't know. Common gaps include:

**Goals and motivations:**
- Why do users choose to use the product (not buy it — use it)?
- What are they ultimately trying to accomplish?
- What does success look like for them?

**Behavioral context:**
- In what physical and digital environment do they use the product?
- What is their workflow before, during, and after using the product?
- Who else is involved in their workflow?

**Attitudes and emotional state:**
- How do they feel about the problem domain?
- What are their frustrations with current solutions or workarounds?
- What are their fears and hopes related to the product category?

**Skills and capabilities:**
- What is their technical proficiency?
- What domain expertise do they bring?
- How do their skills change over time with the product?

## 3. Defining Research Objectives

Write research objectives as specific questions to answer. Good persona research objectives focus on understanding the user in context, not validating feature ideas.

### Strong Research Objectives (Examples)
- "Understand the primary goals, workflows, and frustrations of users who process more than 50 transactions per week"
- "Identify distinct behavioral clusters among current users based on their goals and product usage patterns"
- "Discover how the user's physical environment and surrounding tools influence their interaction with our product"
- "Understand how new users' needs and frustrations differ from experienced users over time"

### Weak Research Objectives (Avoid)
- "Validate that users want feature X" (this is feature validation, not persona research)
- "Determine the demographics of our users" (demographics don't drive persona creation)
- "Prove that our product is better than the competition" (this is competitive validation)

## 4. Selecting Research Methods

### Primary Methods for Persona Discovery

**One-on-One User Interviews (Essential)**
- Duration: 45-60 minutes
- Format: Semi-structured with open-ended questions
- Best for: Understanding goals, motivations, frustrations, attitudes
- Minimum sample: 8-12 interviews per suspected persona type; look for saturation (when new interviews stop revealing new patterns)

**Contextual Inquiry (Highly Recommended)**
- Duration: 60-120 minutes
- Format: Observe users in their natural environment while they work
- Best for: Understanding workflow, environment, tools, workarounds, and behaviors users may not articulate in interviews
- Minimum sample: 4-6 observations per suspected persona type

**Empathy Mapping Workshops (Recommended)**
- Duration: 60-90 minutes per workshop
- Format: Cross-functional team synthesizes research into Says/Thinks/Does/Feels quadrants
- Best for: Building shared understanding across teams; identifying gaps in knowledge
- When: After initial interviews, before finalizing personas

### Supplementary Methods

**Usage Analytics Review**
- Quantitative validation of behavioral patterns observed in qualitative research
- Segment users by behavior (frequency, feature usage, session patterns)
- Cross-reference with interview findings to validate or challenge clusters

**Survey Research**
- Scale qualitative findings across a larger population
- Validate the prevalence of behavioral clusters
- Best used after qualitative research has identified the right questions

**Support and Feedback Mining**
- Analyze support tickets, feature requests, and community forum posts
- Categorize by user type or behavioral pattern
- Reveals pain points users experience but may not articulate in interviews

## 5. Recruiting Participants

### Who to Interview

Target a diverse mix across these dimensions:

**Usage maturity:**
- New users (less than 3 months)
- Developing users (3-12 months)
- Experienced/power users (12+ months)

**Usage intensity:**
- Light/infrequent users
- Regular users
- Heavy/daily users

**Context:**
- Different roles that use the product
- Different industries or use cases (if multi-segment)
- Different company sizes (if relevant)

**Outcome:**
- Successful users (achieving their goals)
- Struggling users (not achieving goals, considering alternatives)
- Churned users (stopped using — reveals unmet needs)

### Recruitment Tips
- Customer success and support teams are the best source for identifying participants
- Offer incentives appropriate to the audience (gift cards for consumers, charitable donations for enterprise users)
- Ensure participants represent observed segments, not just the most vocal or accessible users
- Include 2-3 internal stakeholders who regularly interact with users as context interviews (not as persona subjects)
- Be transparent: this is research, not a sales call

## 6. Building a Research Plan

### Research Plan Template

Structure your plan with these elements:

**Objectives:** 2-4 specific research questions (from Section 3)

**Methods:** Which methods you'll use and why (from Section 4)

**Participants:** Target profiles, number per profile, recruitment approach (from Section 5)

**Timeline:** Research typically spans 3-6 weeks:
- Week 1: Stakeholder interviews and existing data audit
- Weeks 2-3: User interviews and contextual inquiries
- Week 4: Empathy mapping workshop and initial synthesis
- Weeks 5-6: Persona creation and validation

**Deliverables:**
- Research findings summary
- Empathy maps (one per identified persona)
- Provisional personas (to be refined after validation)
- Knowledge gaps requiring further research

**Resources needed:**
- Researcher/interviewer (product manager, designer, or dedicated researcher)
- Note-taker for interviews
- Recording equipment (with user consent)
- Analysis tools (affinity diagrams, digital whiteboard)
- Cross-functional workshop participants
