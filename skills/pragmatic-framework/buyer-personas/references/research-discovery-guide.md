# Buyer Persona Research & Discovery Guide

This reference covers how to gather the data needed to build accurate, research-backed buyer personas following the Pragmatic Framework's market-driven methodology. The Pragmatic Framework emphasizes that personas should come from real market data — not from internal brainstorms or assumptions.

## 1. Research Sources

Buyer personas should draw from multiple data sources. No single source gives the full picture — triangulation builds confidence.

### Primary Research (highest value)

- **Win/loss interviews**: The single most valuable input for buyer personas. Talk to people who recently evaluated your product (whether they bought or not) to understand who was involved, what they cared about, and how they decided. The Pragmatic Framework's Win/Loss Analysis activity feeds directly into persona development.
- **Market interviews (NIHITO)**: "Nothing Important Happens In The Office" — go talk to the market. Interview current customers, prospects, and non-customers about how they buy solutions in your category.
- **Sales ride-alongs**: Sit in on sales calls and demos to observe which personas show up, what questions they ask, and what concerns they raise.
- **Customer advisory boards**: Structured conversations with a curated set of customers who represent your key segments.

### Secondary Research (supplementary)

- **CRM data**: Analyze deal records — who are the contacts involved in closed-won and closed-lost deals? What titles appear? What objections were logged?
- **Customer support data**: Support tickets and escalation patterns reveal who uses the product, what problems they encounter, and who they escalate to.
- **Web analytics**: Which content do different personas consume? Gated content downloads can be mapped to job titles and company sizes.
- **Sales team input**: Sales reps interact with buyers daily and have pattern-matched on persona types, even if they haven't formalized them. Their input is valuable but should be validated — reps may overweight recent deals or their most vocal contacts.
- **Industry analyst reports**: Gartner, Forrester, and similar firms often publish buyer behavior research for specific technology categories.
- **Competitive intelligence**: How do competitors position to different buyer types? Their messaging reveals who they think the key personas are.

### What to avoid

- **Internal-only brainstorms**: Building personas from internal assumptions is the most common failure mode. The resulting personas reflect who the company *thinks* its buyers are, not who they actually are.
- **Demographic-only profiles**: Job title + company size is not a persona. Without buying behavior, problems, and decision criteria, a demographic profile doesn't help anyone make better decisions.
- **Single-source personas**: A persona based entirely on one data source (e.g., only CRM data, or only one interview) will have blind spots.

## 2. Interview Methodology

Buyer persona interviews follow many of the same principles as the Pragmatic Framework's NIHITO market interviews, adapted for the buying process.

### Interview principles

- **Open-ended questions**: Lead with "tell me about..." and "walk me through..." rather than yes/no questions. The goal is to hear the buyer's story in their own words.
- **Recent experience**: Ask about their most recent purchase decision in your category (within the last 6–12 months). Recent memory is more accurate and detailed than generalizations.
- **The full story**: Get the narrative from trigger to decision. What happened first? Who got involved? When did they enter? What changed their mind?
- **Their words, not yours**: Pay attention to the exact language buyers use to describe their problems, criteria, and concerns. Capture verbatim quotes — these become the foundation of messaging.
- **Non-judgmental exploration**: Especially in win/loss interviews, the buyer needs to feel safe being honest. Make it clear you're trying to learn, not defend your product.

### Interview logistics

- **Duration**: 30–45 minutes per interview. Longer than 45 minutes leads to fatigue; shorter than 30 rarely gets past surface-level answers.
- **Sample size**: Aim for 8–12 interviews per persona type to reach pattern saturation. You'll start seeing repeated themes after 5–6 interviews; the remaining interviews confirm patterns and surface edge cases.
- **Who conducts**: Ideally someone from Product Management or Product Marketing — not sales. Buyers are more candid when they're not talking to someone who sold (or tried to sell) them something. The Pragmatic Framework emphasizes that win/loss interviewers should be objective and not involved in the sales effort.
- **Recording**: Ask permission to record. Transcripts are invaluable for extracting verbatim quotes and catching details you missed in real-time.

## 3. Interview Target Selection

Who you interview shapes which personas you uncover. Cast a wide net across these categories:

### For win/loss interviews

- **Recent wins**: People who evaluated and chose your product in the last 6 months. They can tell you who was involved, what mattered, and why they chose you.
- **Recent losses**: People who evaluated but chose a competitor or the status quo. Often more illuminating than wins — they reveal what you're missing and which personas you failed to reach.
- **Stalled deals**: Prospects who started evaluating but never made a decision. Reveals organizational blockers, missing personas, and unresolved concerns.

### For market interviews

- **Current customers**: Different buyer roles within existing accounts — not just your primary contact, but the other people who were involved in the original purchase.
- **Prospects who never evaluated**: People in your target market who have the problems you solve but never entered a buying process. Reveals what triggers (or fails to trigger) active evaluation.
- **Non-customers in adjacent segments**: If you're expanding into new segments, interview buyers in those segments to understand how persona profiles shift.

### Identifying the right contacts

- Ask your sales team: "Who else was in the room during the evaluation? Who asked the tough questions? Who almost killed the deal?"
- Review deal records for all contacts associated with opportunities, not just the primary.
- At existing customers, ask your champion: "Who else was involved in the decision to bring us on board?"

## 4. Sample Interview Questions by Buyer Type

Adapt these to your specific product and market. These are starting points, not scripts.

### For all buyer types (opening questions)

- "Walk me through how the decision to evaluate [this type of solution] came about. What was happening in your organization?"
- "Who was involved in the evaluation process? What were their roles?"
- "How long did the process take from first conversation to final decision?"
- "What other options did you consider? How did you narrow them down?"

### Economic Buyer–focused questions

- "What business outcome were you trying to achieve with this purchase?"
- "How did you think about the budget for this? Was there an established budget, or did you need to build a case?"
- "What metrics or results would make you confident this was a good investment?"
- "Who else needed to approve the spending? What did they need to see?"
- "What was the biggest risk you saw in making this change?"

### Technical Buyer–focused questions

- "What were your non-negotiable technical requirements?"
- "How did you evaluate integration with your existing systems?"
- "What security, compliance, or data handling concerns did you have?"
- "Walk me through the technical evaluation — what did you test? What did you find?"
- "What would have been a dealbreaker from a technical standpoint?"

### User/Functional Buyer–focused questions

- "How does [this type of problem] show up in your day-to-day work?"
- "What tools or processes are you using today to handle this? What works and what doesn't?"
- "When you were evaluating solutions, what features or capabilities were most important to you?"
- "How did you imagine your workday changing if you had the right solution?"
- "Was there anything about [the product you chose / didn't choose] that surprised you during evaluation?"

### Champion/Lead Evaluator–focused questions

- "How did you end up leading this evaluation? Was it assigned to you or did you volunteer?"
- "How did you organize the evaluation process? What steps did you take?"
- "What resources or content were most helpful during your research?"
- "How did you build consensus among the other stakeholders? Were there disagreements?"
- "What would have made the evaluation process easier or faster?"

## 5. Synthesizing Research into Personas

After completing interviews and gathering secondary data, follow this process to synthesize findings into personas.

### Step 1: Identify patterns

Review interview transcripts and notes looking for clusters of similar behavior, concerns, and decision criteria. Group by:
- Role in the buying process (not job title — focus on what they *did* in the evaluation)
- Problems they described
- Decision criteria they prioritized
- Information sources they trusted
- Objections they raised

### Step 2: Define distinct personas

Each persona should represent a meaningfully different buying behavior pattern. If two groups have the same problems, criteria, and buying behavior, they're the same persona even if they have different job titles. If one group cares primarily about cost and another about integration, those are distinct personas.

A useful test: if you would craft a fundamentally different message for two groups, they're different personas. If the same message works for both, they may be one persona with title variations.

### Step 3: Populate the template

Using the patterns from Step 1, fill in the persona template (see `persona-template.md`). Use actual interview language wherever possible — the words buyers use to describe their problems are the words your messaging should use.

### Step 4: Validate with internal teams

Share draft personas with sales, customer success, and product teams for a reality check. Do they recognize these people? Do the personas match their experience? Are any key personas missing?

Internal validation is a gut check, not proof. Sales teams sometimes have blind spots (e.g., they may not see the technical blocker who killed deals they never heard about). Use internal input to refine, not to override interview data.

### Step 5: Mark confidence levels

Be honest about what's validated and what's hypothesis. Every persona field should be traceable to a data source. Fields based on a single interview or internal assumption should be flagged for further validation.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Confusing buyer personas with user personas
The Pragmatic Framework treats these as separate activities for a reason. A buyer persona describes someone involved in the *purchase decision*. A user persona describes someone who *uses the product*. Sometimes they're the same person; often they're not. Don't collapse them.

### Pitfall 2: Too many personas
If you have more than 5 buyer personas for a single market segment, you've probably sliced too thin. Consolidate personas that share the same buying behavior and decision criteria. More personas means more positioning documents, more content, more sales tools — and less focus.

### Pitfall 3: Too few personas (or just one)
Treating "the customer" as a monolith ignores the reality of B2B buying committees. Even in simpler sales, the person who signs the check has different concerns than the person who runs the trial.

### Pitfall 4: Building personas once and forgetting them
Markets shift, buying committees change, new competitors emerge. Personas should be reviewed at least quarterly and fully refreshed annually — or whenever you see a significant shift in win/loss patterns, content engagement, or sales cycle dynamics.

### Pitfall 5: Personas that live in a slide deck
If personas are only referenced during annual planning, they're not doing their job. Effective personas become a shared language across the organization — developers reference them, marketers build campaigns around them, sales reps identify them in deals. The Pragmatic Framework emphasizes making personas "come alive" to all parts of the organization.

### Pitfall 6: Stock photos and demographic filler
Adding a stock photo, hobbies, and personality traits to a persona doesn't make it more useful. It often introduces bias and distracts from the actionable content — buying behavior, decision criteria, and problems. Focus on the fields that change how you build, market, and sell.
