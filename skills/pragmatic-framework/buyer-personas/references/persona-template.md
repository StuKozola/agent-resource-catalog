# Buyer Persona Template — Field-by-Field Guide

This reference provides the full template structure for a Pragmatic Framework buyer persona document. Each persona should include the sections below. Not every field will be relevant for every product or market — use judgment and skip fields that don't apply.

## 1. Persona Identity

Give the persona a descriptive role-based label rather than (or in addition to) a fictional name. Some practitioners argue that fictional names create empathy; others argue they introduce demographic bias (race, gender, age assumptions). Present the trade-off to the user and let them decide. If using a name, pair it with the role label.

**Fields:**

- **Persona label**: e.g., "The IT Decision-Maker" or "The Operations Champion"
- **Fictional name** (optional): e.g., "David Chen" — only if the user wants one
- **Typical job titles**: List 2–4 titles this persona commonly holds (titles vary by company size and industry)
- **Seniority level**: e.g., Director, VP, C-suite, Manager, Individual Contributor
- **Department / function**: e.g., IT, Finance, Operations, Procurement
- **Market segment**: Which segment(s) this persona appears in
- **Education and background** (if relevant): Helps calibrate technical depth and communication style

## 2. Role in the Buying Process

This is the most important section for a *buyer* persona (as opposed to a user persona). It captures what this person does in the purchase decision.

**Fields:**

- **Buying role type**: Economic Buyer, Technical Buyer, User/Functional Buyer, Champion/Lead Evaluator, Procurement/Gatekeeper, Executive Sponsor, or Influencer
- **Decision authority**: Final decision maker, strong influencer, recommender, or blocker/gatekeeper
- **When they enter the process**: Early (triggers evaluation), mid (joins during shortlisting), or late (approves final decision)
- **Who they report to**: Their relationship to the economic decision-maker
- **Who they influence**: Other buying committee members they sway
- **Veto power**: Can this persona kill the deal? Under what circumstances?

## 3. Professional Context

This section makes the persona feel real and grounded in their working world.

**Fields:**

- **Company size and type**: Enterprise, mid-market, SMB; public/private; industry verticals
- **Team size they manage** (if applicable)
- **Key responsibilities**: 3–5 bullet points on what they're accountable for
- **KPIs they're measured on**: What metrics define success in their role
- **Tools and technologies they use daily**: Helps calibrate technical sophistication
- **Reporting structure**: Who they report to, who reports to them
- **Budget authority**: Do they own a budget? How large? What approval process do they follow?

## 4. Problems and Pain Points

The Pragmatic Framework is built around market problems. This section maps the persona's problems to what your product can solve.

**Fields:**

- **Top 3–5 urgent problems**: Stated in the persona's own language (from interviews, win/loss data, or support tickets). These should be problems they would describe themselves, not problems you think they have.
- **Underlying frustrations**: The emotional dimension — what makes their current situation painful or risky
- **Status quo approach**: How they solve (or cope with) these problems today without your product
- **Cost of inaction**: What happens if they do nothing — financial, operational, career risk

## 5. Goals and Success Criteria

What does this persona want to achieve, both with your product and in their career?

**Fields:**

- **Functional goals**: The practical tasks they want to accomplish (e.g., "reduce deployment time by 40%")
- **Emotional goals**: How they want to feel (e.g., "confident the system won't fail during peak load")
- **Social / career goals**: How they want to be perceived (e.g., "seen as the leader who modernized our infrastructure")
- **Success metrics**: How they will measure whether the purchase was a good decision. These are distinct from *your* product metrics — they're *their* metrics.
- **Timeline expectations**: When they expect to see results

## 6. Buying Behavior

How does this persona actually buy? This section supports the Buyer Experience activity in the Framework.

**Fields:**

- **Buying triggers**: What events push this persona from "aware" to "actively evaluating"? (e.g., budget cycle, new initiative, compliance deadline, leadership change, system failure)
- **Buying process stage involvement**: At which stages is this persona active — awareness, consideration, evaluation, decision, procurement?
- **Research behavior**: Do they google solutions? Ask peers? Attend conferences? Read analyst reports? Engage sales reps early or late?
- **Evaluation approach**: Do they run formal RFPs? Do POCs/trials? Require vendor presentations? Check reference customers?
- **Committee dynamics**: Do they lead the evaluation or participate in one? How many people are typically involved?
- **Typical sales cycle length**: From trigger to signed deal
- **Budget cycle**: When does budget get allocated? Is there a "use it or lose it" window?

## 7. Decision Criteria

What factors determine whether this persona will choose your solution over alternatives?

**Fields:**

- **Must-have requirements**: Non-negotiable criteria (table stakes)
- **Differentiating criteria**: What tips the decision in a close comparison
- **Weights / priorities**: Which criteria matter most to *this* persona (economic buyers weight cost; technical buyers weight integration)
- **Competitive alternatives**: Which competitors or alternative approaches does this persona consider? What do they say about them?
- **Risk tolerance**: Is this persona risk-averse (wants proven solutions, references, guarantees) or risk-tolerant (willing to bet on innovation)?

## 8. Information Sources and Preferences

Where and how does this persona consume information? This drives Content and Awareness strategies.

**Fields:**

- **Trusted sources**: Industry analysts, peer recommendations, review sites, professional associations, specific publications
- **Preferred content formats**: White papers, case studies, ROI calculators, demos, webinars, podcasts, short videos, technical documentation
- **Communication channels**: Email, LinkedIn, industry events, direct sales outreach, community forums
- **Vernacular and language**: What terminology does this persona use? (Use their words in messaging, not yours.) An economic buyer says "ROI" and "payback period"; a technical buyer says "API" and "latency."
- **Level of technical detail**: High (wants architecture diagrams), medium (wants feature comparisons), or low (wants business outcomes)

## 9. Objections and Concerns

What stops this persona from saying yes?

**Fields:**

- **Common objections**: The top 3–5 reasons this persona pushes back, stated in their own words
- **Hidden concerns**: Things they worry about but don't always say out loud (e.g., "this could fail and it would be my fault")
- **Risk factors**: What makes this purchase feel risky to them specifically
- **Previous bad experiences**: Have they been burned by similar products before? What went wrong?
- **Internal politics**: Are there organizational dynamics that make this purchase politically sensitive?

## 10. Day in the Life

A short narrative (3–5 sentences) that describes a typical workday for this persona. This brings the persona to life and helps product teams empathize with the person behind the profile.

Focus on: what their morning looks like, what meetings they attend, what frustrations they encounter, what tools they use, and what pressures they face. Include the problem your product solves showing up naturally in the narrative.

## 11. Key Quotes

Include 2–4 verbatim or representative quotes that capture this persona's voice. These can come from:

- Win/loss interviews
- Customer support interactions
- Sales call notes
- Survey open-text responses
- Market interviews

If real quotes aren't available yet, write representative quotes that capture the persona's likely perspective and mark them as hypothetical. Replace with real quotes as soon as research data is available.

## 12. Messaging Guidance

A bridge between the persona profile and the Positioning / Message Matrix activities.

**Fields:**

- **Value proposition for this persona**: One sentence that captures why your product matters to them specifically
- **Lead message**: The opening statement that would get this persona's attention
- **Proof points**: What evidence would be most persuasive to this persona (ROI data for economic buyers, integration specs for technical buyers, workflow demos for user buyers)
- **Language to use**: Words and phrases that resonate
- **Language to avoid**: Jargon or framing that would alienate this persona

## 13. Validation Status

Track whether this persona is based on real data or is still a hypothesis.

**Fields:**

- **Data sources**: What research inputs were used (e.g., "12 win/loss interviews, Q3 customer survey, 3 sales ride-alongs")
- **Confidence level**: High (validated with primary research), Medium (based on some interviews + internal knowledge), or Low (hypothesis only — needs validation)
- **Last validated**: Date of most recent review or refresh
- **Next review date**: When this persona should be revisited
- **Open questions**: What do we still need to learn about this persona?

## Template notes

- Not every field is needed for every persona. The economic buyer might not need a detailed "tools and technologies" section. The technical buyer might not need "budget authority." Use judgment.
- The most critical sections are: Role in the Buying Process, Problems and Pain Points, Decision Criteria, and Buying Behavior. If time is limited, prioritize these.
- When creating documents for the user, present the template fields as clean headings and content — don't include the instructional notes from this reference file in the output.
- If the user provides interview transcripts, survey data, or CRM exports, synthesize the data into the template rather than asking them to fill it in manually.

## Buying Committee Summary

In addition to individual persona documents, create a one-page **Buying Committee Map** that shows:

1. All the personas involved in a typical deal
2. Their buying role type
3. Their decision authority level
4. When they enter the buying process
5. Key concerns of each
6. Relationships and influence flows between them

This map is especially valuable for Sales Alignment — it shows reps who they need to engage, in what order, and with what message.
