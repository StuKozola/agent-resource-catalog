# Product Portfolio — Pragmatic Marketing Framework

## What This Skill Does

This skill helps you conduct the **Product Portfolio** activity as defined in the Pragmatic
Framework (Pragmatic Institute). It guides users through integrating their products, services,
and partners into a coherent portfolio focused on solving market problems — and then managing
that portfolio as if it were a product itself, complete with its own business plan, positioning,
buying process documentation, market requirements, and marketing plan.

## Context Within the Pragmatic Framework

Product Portfolio sits in the **Focus** category of the Pragmatic Framework, alongside Market
Definition, Distribution Strategy, and Product Roadmap. The Focus category is about deciding
*where to play* — which markets, channels, and product groupings will drive the business forward.

Product Portfolio specifically answers: **"How do we organize and manage our collection of
products so the whole is greater than the sum of its parts?"**

### Key Pragmatic Principle

> "Every 'product' needs product management and a business plan." — Pragmatic Rule #12

This rule applies to portfolios too. A portfolio without its own strategic management drifts into
an uncoordinated collection of individual products competing internally for resources and
confusing the market.

### Adjacent Framework Activities

Product Portfolio work draws on and feeds into several other Pragmatic Framework activities:

- **Market Definition** — Segments inform which products belong together in a portfolio
- **Distribution Strategy** — Channel alignment must work at the portfolio level, not just per-product
- **Product Roadmap** — Individual roadmaps must be coordinated across the portfolio
- **Business Plan** — The portfolio needs its own business case, separate from individual products
- **Positioning** — Portfolio-level positioning frames how the market perceives the whole offering
- **Pricing** — Cross-product pricing, bundles, and upsell paths are portfolio decisions
- **Buy, Build or Partner** — Gap analysis happens at the portfolio level to find missing pieces
- **Product Profitability** — Portfolio-level P&L ensures no single product cannibalizes the whole

## Responsibilities

The person responsible for Product Portfolio has two core duties:

1. **Integrate products, services, and partners into a coherent set (or portfolio) focused on
   solving a problem for the market.** This means the portfolio tells a unified story — it's not
   just "everything we sell" but a deliberate grouping organized around market problems.

2. **Manage the portfolio like a product** — with its own:
   - Business plan (investment thesis, financial model, go/no-go criteria)
   - Positioning (how the portfolio solves market problems as a whole)
   - Buying process documentation (how buyers evaluate and purchase across the portfolio)
   - Market requirements (what the market needs from the portfolio, not just individual products)
   - Marketing plan (awareness, demand gen, and programs at the portfolio level)

## Required Skills

Effective portfolio management requires:

- **Strategic thinking** — seeing the forest, not just the trees
- **Financial savviness** — portfolio-level P&L, resource allocation trade-offs, ROI analysis
- **Judgment capabilities** — deciding what belongs in the portfolio and what doesn't
- **Matrix/program management** — coordinating across multiple product teams
- **Previous experience managing multiple products or a product line** — pattern recognition matters

## How to Use This Skill

When a user needs help with Product Portfolio work, follow this workflow. The depth of each
step depends on how much context the user provides and where they are in their process.

### Step 1: Understand the Current State

Before doing anything strategic, get a clear picture of what exists. Ask about or help the user
document:

- What products, services, and partner offerings exist today?
- How are they currently grouped or organized? (By market? Technology? Historical accident?)
- Which market segments does each product serve?
- What market problems does each product solve?
- What's the revenue and profitability picture for each product?
- What lifecycle stage is each product in? (introduction, growth, maturity, decline)

If the user has this information, help them organize it. If they don't, help them build a
lightweight inventory. For detailed guidance, read `references/portfolio-inventory.md`.

### Step 2: Define the Portfolio Strategy

With the inventory in hand, help the user make strategic decisions:

- **Portfolio grouping logic** — Should products be grouped by market segment, problem domain,
  buyer persona, technology platform, or some combination?
- **Portfolio boundaries** — What's in and what's out? Not everything a company sells necessarily
  belongs in the same portfolio.
- **Strategic role of each product** — Use portfolio analysis frameworks (BCG matrix, GE-McKinsey,
  Three Horizons) to classify each product's role. See `references/portfolio-analysis.md`.
- **Gap analysis** — Where does the portfolio have holes? What market problems go unsolved? What
  buying process stages are unsupported?
- **Rationalization** — Which products should be sunset, merged, or spun off?

### Step 3: Build Portfolio-Level Artifacts

This is the "manage the portfolio like a product" part. Help the user create:

1. **Portfolio Business Plan** — Investment thesis, financial model, resource allocation, risk
   assessment, and go/no-go criteria for the portfolio as a whole
2. **Portfolio Positioning** — A positioning document that describes what the *portfolio* does for
   the market, not just what individual products do. Use the Pragmatic positioning format:
   For [target segment] who [have this problem], [portfolio name] is a [category] that
   [key benefit]. Unlike [alternatives], we [key differentiator].
3. **Portfolio Buying Process** — Document how buyers evaluate and purchase across the portfolio.
   Map the buyer journey at the portfolio level, including cross-sell and upsell paths.
4. **Portfolio Market Requirements** — Capture market needs that span multiple products. These
   are the requirements that no single product team owns but the portfolio must address.
5. **Portfolio Marketing Plan** — Awareness, demand generation, and programs at the portfolio
   level, with goals and measurements.

For templates and detailed guidance on each artifact, read `references/portfolio-artifacts.md`.

### Step 4: Establish Portfolio Governance

A portfolio needs ongoing management, not just initial setup. Help the user define:

- **Review cadence** — How often is the portfolio reviewed? (Quarterly is typical.)
- **Decision rights** — Who decides what enters or exits the portfolio? What's the escalation path?
- **Performance metrics** — What KPIs does the portfolio track? (Revenue mix, cross-sell rate,
  portfolio-level NPS, resource utilization across products, market share by segment)
- **Coordination mechanisms** — How do individual product teams stay aligned with portfolio
  strategy? (Portfolio councils, shared OKRs, joint roadmap reviews)
- **Lifecycle management** — What triggers move a product from one lifecycle stage to another?
  What are the criteria for sunsetting a product?

### Step 5: Output and Deliverables

Based on what the user needs, produce one or more of:

- **Portfolio inventory spreadsheet** — All products mapped to segments, problems, lifecycle stage,
  and financial performance
- **Portfolio strategy document** — The business plan, positioning, and marketing plan in one
  cohesive document (use docx skill if creating a Word document)
- **Portfolio analysis visualization** — BCG matrix, lifecycle map, or other visual framework
  (use the Visualizer or create as an artifact)
- **Portfolio review deck** — Presentation for leadership review (use pptx skill)
- **Action plan** — Specific next steps with owners and timelines

## Common Scenarios

### "We have too many products and need to simplify"
Focus on Step 1 (inventory) and Step 2 (rationalization). Help the user build criteria for
what stays, what goes, and what merges. The BCG matrix and lifecycle analysis are especially
useful here.

### "We're adding a new product and need to fit it into the portfolio"
Focus on Step 2 (gap analysis, strategic role) and Step 3 (update positioning and buying
process). Help the user assess whether the new product fills a genuine gap or creates overlap.

### "Leadership wants a portfolio strategy presentation"
Focus on Steps 1-3 and produce a portfolio review deck. Structure it as: current state →
strategic assessment → recommended portfolio composition → investment priorities → roadmap.

### "We need to align our product teams around a portfolio vision"
Focus on Step 3 (positioning, market requirements) and Step 4 (governance, coordination).
The portfolio positioning document becomes the north star that individual teams align to.

### "I'm new to portfolio management and need to understand the basics"
Walk through all five steps at a higher level. Explain the core Pragmatic principle: portfolios
should be managed like products. The biggest mindset shift is moving from "collection of things
we sell" to "integrated solution set organized around market problems."

## Key Principles to Reinforce

Throughout any portfolio conversation, return to these principles:

1. **Outside-in thinking** — Portfolio decisions start with market problems, not internal
   organizational structure. Don't group products by engineering team; group them by the
   problems they solve for the market.

2. **The portfolio is a product** — It needs its own business plan, positioning, and marketing
   plan. If you wouldn't run an individual product without these, don't run a portfolio without
   them either.

3. **Balance short-term and long-term** — A healthy portfolio has products at different lifecycle
   stages: mature cash generators funding growth investments, with experimental bets in early
   stages.

4. **Resource allocation is a portfolio decision** — Don't let individual product teams fight
   over resources in isolation. Allocate at the portfolio level based on strategic fit and
   market opportunity.

5. **Simplicity serves the market** — Buyers shouldn't need a decoder ring to understand your
   product lineup. If your sales team can't clearly explain what to sell to whom, the portfolio
   is too complex.
