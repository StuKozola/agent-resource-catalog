# Portfolio Analysis Frameworks

These frameworks help assess products within a portfolio and make strategic allocation decisions.
Use them during Step 2 (Define the Portfolio Strategy) of the main workflow.

## BCG Growth-Share Matrix

The Boston Consulting Group matrix plots products on two dimensions:
- **Y-axis: Market growth rate** — Is the market for this product expanding or contracting?
- **X-axis: Relative market share** — How does our share compare to the largest competitor?

This creates four quadrants:

### Stars (High Growth, High Share)
- Invest heavily to maintain position as the market grows
- These products are the future cash cows
- Portfolio implication: Prioritize resources and attention

### Cash Cows (Low Growth, High Share)
- Mature markets where the product dominates
- Generates cash with relatively low investment needed
- Portfolio implication: Harvest cash to fund Stars and Question Marks

### Question Marks (High Growth, Low Share)
- Growing markets where the product hasn't achieved leadership
- Requires significant investment to grow share, with uncertain outcome
- Portfolio implication: Make a deliberate invest-or-divest decision. Don't let these linger.

### Dogs (Low Growth, Low Share)
- Neither growing nor leading
- Often consuming more resources than they're worth
- Portfolio implication: Sunset, sell, or radically restructure. Every Dog should have an
  explicit plan.

### How to Use It
1. Plot each product in the portfolio on the matrix
2. Assess the overall distribution — a healthy portfolio has products in multiple quadrants
3. Define the action for each product based on its quadrant
4. Identify transitions — which Question Marks should become Stars? Which Stars are becoming
   Cash Cows?

### Limitations
- Market growth rate is only one dimension of attractiveness
- Relative market share doesn't capture all competitive dynamics
- Works best for product-level analysis in well-defined markets
- Less useful for platforms, ecosystems, or highly differentiated niche products

## GE-McKinsey Nine-Box Matrix

A more nuanced alternative to BCG that evaluates:
- **Y-axis: Market/Industry Attractiveness** — Composite score based on market size, growth
  rate, profitability, competitive intensity, technology requirements, regulatory environment
- **X-axis: Competitive Strength** — Composite score based on market share, brand strength,
  production capacity, profit margins, technology capability, management quality

### Scoring Approach
Rate each factor on a 1-5 scale, weight them by importance, and calculate a composite score
for each axis. This places each product in one of nine cells:

**High Attractiveness + Strong Position** → Invest and grow aggressively
**High Attractiveness + Medium Position** → Invest selectively to improve position
**High Attractiveness + Weak Position** → Invest to build or divest
**Medium Attractiveness + Strong Position** → Protect and maintain; invest selectively
**Medium Attractiveness + Medium Position** → Manage for earnings; selective investment
**Medium Attractiveness + Weak Position** → Limited investment; plan exit if no improvement
**Low Attractiveness + Strong Position** → Protect position; harvest cash
**Low Attractiveness + Medium Position** → Manage for cash; reduce investment
**Low Attractiveness + Weak Position** → Divest, harvest, or exit

### How to Use It
1. Define the factors for each axis relevant to your business
2. Weight the factors (they should sum to 100%)
3. Score each product on every factor
4. Plot on the matrix
5. Define strategic actions based on cell position

### When to Prefer GE-McKinsey over BCG
- When market growth rate alone doesn't capture attractiveness (e.g., a low-growth but highly
  profitable market)
- When competitive position is more nuanced than market share (e.g., strong brand but small share)
- When the portfolio includes products in very different types of markets

## Three Horizons Model

Originally from McKinsey, this framework categorizes products by their strategic time horizon:

### Horizon 1: Core Business
- Current products generating today's revenue and profit
- Managed for performance and efficiency
- Typical allocation: 70% of resources
- Risk profile: Low — these are proven products in known markets

### Horizon 2: Emerging Opportunities
- Products that are gaining traction but not yet at scale
- Growing revenue, significant investment needed
- Typical allocation: 20% of resources
- Risk profile: Medium — market fit is validated but scale is uncertain

### Horizon 3: Future Bets
- Early-stage products, experiments, or market explorations
- Little or no revenue yet; high uncertainty
- Typical allocation: 10% of resources
- Risk profile: High — these may or may not become real businesses

### How to Use It
1. Classify each portfolio product into a horizon
2. Calculate the resource allocation across horizons
3. Compare actual allocation to the target ratio (70/20/10 is a starting point, not a rule)
4. Identify imbalances — too little in H3 means no pipeline for future growth; too much in
   H3 means unstable current revenue
5. Define transition criteria — what must a product achieve to move from H3 → H2 → H1?

### Portfolio Health Check
A healthy portfolio using Three Horizons has:
- H1 products generating reliable cash flow
- H2 products showing market traction and path to profitability
- H3 products exploring adjacent or new market problems
- Clear criteria for graduating products between horizons
- A mechanism for killing H3 products that don't show promise

## Product Lifecycle Analysis

Map each product to its lifecycle stage and assess portfolio balance:

### Introduction Stage
- New to market, low revenue, negative or minimal profit
- High investment in development, launch, and market education
- Portfolio question: Is this filling a validated gap?

### Growth Stage
- Revenue accelerating, market adoption increasing
- Investment in scaling, market expansion, feature depth
- Portfolio question: Are we investing enough to capture the opportunity?

### Maturity Stage
- Revenue stable or growing slowly, strong profitability
- Investment in efficiency, retention, incremental improvements
- Portfolio question: Are we harvesting cash to fund growth elsewhere?

### Decline Stage
- Revenue declining, market shrinking or shifting
- Minimal investment, focus on cost reduction
- Portfolio question: When do we sunset, and how do we transition customers?

### Using Lifecycle for Portfolio Decisions
Plot all products on a lifecycle curve. Look for:
- **Pipeline health** — Are there enough products in Introduction/Growth to replace mature products?
- **Revenue risk** — If multiple products are in late Maturity/Decline simultaneously, revenue
  is at risk
- **Investment balance** — Resources should flow from Maturity cash to Introduction/Growth investment
- **Sunset planning** — Every Decline product should have an explicit sunset timeline and customer
  migration plan

## Choosing the Right Framework

| Scenario | Recommended Framework |
|----------|----------------------|
| Quick strategic overview | BCG Matrix |
| Detailed investment prioritization | GE-McKinsey Nine-Box |
| Innovation pipeline health check | Three Horizons |
| Revenue sustainability assessment | Product Lifecycle Analysis |
| Comprehensive portfolio review | Combine 2-3 frameworks for different lenses |

For most portfolio reviews, combining **BCG or GE-McKinsey** (for strategic positioning) with
**Three Horizons** (for innovation balance) and **Lifecycle Analysis** (for revenue sustainability)
gives the most complete picture.
