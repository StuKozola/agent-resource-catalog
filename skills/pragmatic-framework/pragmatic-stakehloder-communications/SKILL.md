# Pragmatic Framework: Stakeholder Communications

## Framework Context

**Official Definition**: Manage proactive communications with relevant stakeholders from strategy through execution.

**Framework Location**: PLANNING category, alongside Positioning, Buyer Experience, Buyer Personas, User Personas, Requirements, and Use Scenarios. Stakeholder Communications connects strategy outputs to every team that must act on them — it spans the full product lifecycle from strategic decisions through execution milestones.

**Key Principle**: Stakeholder Communications in the Pragmatic Framework is *proactive*, not reactive. Product teams do not wait for stakeholders to ask for status — they establish a regular cadence, define what success looks like, and deliver relevant information to the right audience at the right time.

**Covered in the Pragmatic Build course.** The activity lives at the intersection of market knowledge and execution — the product manager's responsibility is to translate what they know about the market into context that every internal and external audience can act on.

**Responsibilities** (from Pragmatic Framework Roles & Responsibilities):
- Manage proactive communications with relevant stakeholders from strategy through execution
- Provide relevant, timely, and easily consumable updates to all internal and external audiences
- Define key metrics and goals related to project progress and success
- Measure and report on progress toward metrics and goals

**Required Skills** (per the Framework):
- Understanding of roadmap, release plan, and project plan
- Written and verbal communications
- Ability to delegate and work well with project management resources
- Ability to manage resources up, down, and sideways

---

## When This Activity Applies

Stakeholder Communications is needed at every stage of the product lifecycle:
- **Strategy phase**: Communicating market problems, strategic direction, and positioning decisions
- **Planning phase**: Sharing roadmap, release plans, and priority rationale
- **Build phase**: Updating development progress, flagging scope changes, managing expectations
- **Launch phase**: Coordinating cross-functional readiness, pre-launch briefings
- **Post-launch phase**: Reporting on metrics, progress toward goals, and market response

## Core Workflow

### Step 1 — Identify Stakeholder Audiences

Not every stakeholder needs the same information. Begin by mapping the relevant audiences for the product or initiative at hand.

**Internal audiences** typically include:
- **Executives / Leadership** — Strategic direction, business impact, milestone status, risk
- **Development / Engineering** — Requirements clarity, priority changes, market context
- **Sales** — Roadmap direction, release timing, competitive implications, feature rationale
- **Marketing** — Launch readiness, positioning updates, campaign dependencies
- **Customer Success / Support** — Upcoming changes that affect customers, feature context
- **Product Operations** — Process changes, dependencies, workflow impacts

**External audiences** may include:
- **Key customers / design partners** — Strategic roadmap themes (never commitments), feedback loops
- **Partners / channels** — Integration timelines, enablement needs
- **Analysts / press** (at launch) — Coordinated through Marketing and PR

Ask the user: *Who are the relevant stakeholder groups for this product or initiative? Are there audiences currently receiving reactive communication rather than proactive updates?*

### Step 2 — Determine What Each Audience Needs

Apply outside-in thinking even internally. For each audience, answer:
1. **What decisions are they making** that your information should inform?
2. **What level of detail** do they need — strategic themes, milestone dates, or feature specifics?
3. **What concerns or objections** might they have?
4. **What format** works for them — executive summary, release notes, roadmap visual, Slack update?

> **Pragmatic principle**: The roadmap is a plan, not a commitment. Be transparent about what is known vs. what is planned vs. what is future. "Stick to the truth. It's easier to remember." — Steve Johnson, Pragmatic Institute founding instructor

**Translating market knowledge to internal audiences** is a core PM responsibility. When communicating about roadmap priorities or product decisions, always connect the "what" to the market problem it solves — not just the feature being built.

---

### Step 3 — Define Key Metrics and Goals

A strong stakeholder communication plan is grounded in measurable reality, not just narrative progress. For each communication workstream, define:
- **What progress metrics** will be reported (velocity, feature completion %, adoption rates, win rates)
- **What goal milestones** mark meaningful progress (beta, GA, adoption targets)
- **How often** metrics will be reported and to whom

This prevents communication from drifting into vague status ("we're making good progress") and builds PM credibility over time.

---

### Step 4 — Design the Communication Plan

For each audience, document a structured plan:

| Audience | Purpose | Key Content | Format | Frequency | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Executives | Strategic alignment | Progress vs. goals, risks, decisions needed | Briefing doc | Monthly | PM |
| Sales | Readiness | Upcoming releases, competitive context | Update email + Q&A | Bi-weekly | PMM |
| Engineering | Context | Market rationale for priorities | Sprint context notes | Per sprint | PM |
| Customers (key) | Relationship + feedback | Roadmap themes, not dates | Roadmap conversation | Quarterly | PM |
| Marketing | Launch readiness | Feature readiness, launch timing, positioning | Launch readiness review | Per release | PM + PMM |

### Step 5 — Choose the Right Roadmap Format

The product roadmap is among the most common forms of stakeholder communication. Two formats exist in the Pragmatic Framework:

**Date-driven roadmaps**
- Organized by calendar dates (Q1, Q2, specific months)
- Advantage: Familiar, concrete
- Risk: Creates commitment expectations; changes damage credibility

**Phase-driven (theme-driven) roadmaps**
- Organized by phases: Now / Next / Later (or similar buckets)
- Advantage: Communicates direction without locking to dates; resilient to change
- Best practice: Always include — *"This roadmap represents plans, not commitments."*

**Audience-specific roadmap views**: The same underlying data should be filtered differently per audience:
- **Executive view**: Strategic themes and business outcomes
- **Sales view**: Features, target personas, competitive impact
- **Engineering view**: Initiatives and sequencing
- **Customer view**: Problems being solved (not features being built)

> **Load reference**: `references/roadmap-formats.md` for format detail, audience-specific views, and date vs. phase guidance.

---

### Step 6 — Execute and Maintain Cadence

A communication plan only works if consistently executed. Establish:
- **Calendar invites and templates** so communication becomes routine, not heroic
- **Out-of-cycle triggers**: What events require unplanned communication? (Major scope change, competitive development, missed milestone, priority shift)
- **Feedback loops**: How do you know communications are landing? (Questions received, alignment in follow-up meetings)

---

### Step 7 — Measure and Report

Stakeholder Communications includes ongoing responsibility for defining and reporting on metrics:
- Regular reporting against pre-defined success metrics
- Making progress visible (dashboards, release trackers, milestone burn-downs)
- Proactively flagging when goals are at risk — *before* they miss, not after

## Common Pitfalls

- **Reactive communication**: Waiting for stakeholders to ask rather than proactively maintaining cadence
- **One-size-fits-all messaging**: Sending the same roadmap or update to executives, sales, and engineering
- **Feature-first framing**: Communicating what is being built without connecting to the market problem it solves
- **Dates as commitments**: Presenting roadmap dates without disclaimers that plans can change
- **No metrics**: Communicating progress narratively without grounding in measurable goals
- **Infrequent updates**: Communicating only at milestones rather than maintaining ongoing visibility
- **Surprise changes**: Failing to proactively communicate when priorities shift
- **Inside-out language**: Using internal jargon instead of market-problem framing that stakeholders can connect to
