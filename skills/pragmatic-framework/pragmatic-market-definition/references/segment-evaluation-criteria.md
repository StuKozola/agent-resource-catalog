# Segment Evaluation and Prioritization Framework

This reference provides a structured scoring rubric for evaluating and prioritizing candidate market segments as part of the Pragmatic Market Definition activity.

## Purpose

After identifying candidate segments and sizing them, you need a systematic way to decide which segments to actively pursue. This framework provides an objective scoring method that reduces bias and forces alignment across stakeholders.

## The Eight Evaluation Criteria

Score each candidate segment on these eight criteria using a 1-5 scale.

### 1. Problem Urgency (Weight: High)

How painful is the problem for buyers in this segment? Urgent problems drive faster sales cycles and higher willingness to pay.

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 5 | Mission-critical — buyers cannot function without solving this problem |
| 4 | High pain — significant productivity, revenue, or compliance impact |
| 3 | Moderate pain — a known problem that buyers want to solve but can live with |
| 2 | Low pain — a nice-to-have improvement |
| 1 | Latent — buyers don't yet recognize this as a problem |

**How to assess:** Reference Win/Loss data and Market Problems interviews. Look for language like "we need this yesterday" or "this is costing us $X per month."

### 2. Problem Pervasiveness (Weight: High)

What percentage of the segment experiences this problem? A problem that affects only 10% of a segment may not justify pursuing the entire segment.

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 5 | 80%+ of the segment experiences this problem |
| 4 | 60-80% of the segment experiences this problem |
| 3 | 40-60% of the segment experiences this problem |
| 2 | 20-40% of the segment experiences this problem |
| 1 | Less than 20% — a niche within a niche |

**How to assess:** Customer interview data, survey results, industry reports. Pragmatic guidance says to validate pervasiveness through direct market conversations (NIHITO — Nothing Important Happens In The Office).

### 3. Willingness to Pay (Weight: High)

Will buyers in this segment spend money to solve this problem? A large, painful market that won't pay is not viable.

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 5 | Budget already allocated for this problem category; precedent of paying for similar solutions |
| 4 | Buyers express strong willingness; budget can be justified easily |
| 3 | Buyers would pay but need internal justification; moderate budget hurdles |
| 2 | Budget is tight; would need strong ROI case to justify spend |
| 1 | Unlikely to pay; expects free solutions or workarounds |

**How to assess:** Win/Loss analysis, pricing research, competitive pricing benchmarks, direct buyer conversations.

### 4. Market Size — SOM (Weight: High)

Is the Serviceable Obtainable Market large enough to sustain and grow the business?

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 5 | SOM alone can support a standalone business line |
| 4 | SOM provides significant revenue contribution |
| 3 | SOM is meaningful but will need additional segments to hit targets |
| 2 | SOM is small; only viable as part of a multi-segment strategy |
| 1 | SOM is too small to justify dedicated investment |

**How to assess:** Use the TAM/SAM/SOM methodology from the market-sizing-guide. Compare SOM against revenue targets and growth goals.

### 5. Competitive Intensity (Weight: Medium)

How crowded is this segment? Can you differentiate meaningfully?

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 5 | Blue ocean — no direct competitors addressing this segment's specific problem |
| 4 | Few competitors; clear differentiation possible |
| 3 | Moderate competition; differentiation requires effort but is achievable |
| 2 | Crowded market; differentiation is difficult |
| 1 | Dominated by entrenched incumbents; very high switching costs |

**How to assess:** Reference Competitive Landscape analysis. Map competitors by segment and assess overlap.

### 6. Distinctive Competence Fit (Weight: High)

Does your organization's unique strength match what this segment values most?

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 5 | Our distinctive competence is exactly what this segment prioritizes |
| 4 | Strong alignment; our strengths are among their top buying criteria |
| 3 | Moderate alignment; our strengths matter but aren't the primary differentiator |
| 2 | Weak alignment; we'd need to develop new capabilities |
| 1 | Misalignment; this segment values things we don't do well |

**How to assess:** Cross-reference the organization's Distinctive Competence statement with the segment's prioritized buying criteria from Win/Loss analysis.

### 7. Ease of Acquisition (Weight: Medium)

Can you efficiently identify, reach, and convert buyers in this segment?

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 5 | Buyers are concentrated, easy to identify, and reachable through existing channels |
| 4 | Identifiable and reachable with modest investment in new channels |
| 3 | Can be reached but requires building new channel relationships or significant marketing effort |
| 2 | Fragmented and hard to reach; long sales cycles |
| 1 | Very difficult to identify or access; would require entirely new GTM motion |

**How to assess:** Consider: Can you build a prospect list? Do you have channel partners or communities that serve this segment? What is the expected sales cycle length?

### 8. Strategic Alignment (Weight: Medium)

Does pursuing this segment support the organization's long-term vision and product roadmap?

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 5 | Core to the company's long-term vision and strategy |
| 4 | Strong alignment; advances strategic goals |
| 3 | Neutral; neither advances nor conflicts with strategy |
| 2 | Tangential; could distract from core strategy |
| 1 | Conflicts with strategic direction or brand positioning |

**How to assess:** Compare against the product vision, company mission, and portfolio strategy. Consider: If you win in this segment, does it open doors to adjacent opportunities?

## Scoring and Prioritization Process

### Step 1: Build the Scoring Matrix

Create a table with candidate segments as rows and the eight criteria as columns. Score each cell 1-5.

### Step 2: Apply Weights

Not all criteria are equally important. Default weights:

| Criterion | Weight |
|-----------|--------|
| Problem Urgency | 3× |
| Problem Pervasiveness | 3× |
| Willingness to Pay | 3× |
| Market Size (SOM) | 3× |
| Competitive Intensity | 2× |
| Distinctive Competence Fit | 3× |
| Ease of Acquisition | 2× |
| Strategic Alignment | 2× |

Adjust weights based on the organization's priorities. For example, a startup might weight Ease of Acquisition higher; a mature company might weight Strategic Alignment higher.

**Weighted Score = Σ (Criterion Score × Weight)**
**Maximum possible = (5 × 3 × 4) + (5 × 2 × 4) = 60 + 40 = 100**

### Step 3: Rank and Categorize

Based on weighted scores, categorize segments:

- **Pursue (top tier)** — Actively invest in these segments. Build dedicated positioning, marketing programs, and sales enablement.
- **Monitor (middle tier)** — Keep these segments in view. They may become viable as the product or market evolves.
- **Deprioritize (lower tier)** — Don't allocate resources here now. Revisit during quarterly reviews.

A typical focused strategy targets 2-4 primary ("Pursue") segments.

### Step 4: Validate with Stakeholders

Share the scoring matrix with cross-functional partners (product, sales, marketing, leadership). The structured scoring creates a shared language for debate and alignment. Be open to adjusting scores based on stakeholder input — the goal is informed consensus, not a rigid formula.

| Segment | Urgency (3×) | Pervasive (3×) | WTP (3×) | Size (3×) | Competition (2×) | DC Fit (3×) | Acquisition (2×) | Strategic (2×) | Total |
|---------|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Mid-market SaaS | 4 (12) | 4 (12) | 4 (12) | 3 (9) | 3 (6) | 5 (15) | 4 (8) | 5 (10) | **84** |
| Enterprise Finance | 5 (15) | 3 (9) | 5 (15) | 4 (12) | 2 (4) | 3 (9) | 2 (4) | 4 (8) | **76** |
| SMB Retail | 3 (9) | 5 (15) | 2 (6) | 2 (6) | 4 (8) | 2 (6) | 5 (10) | 2 (4) | **64** |

In this example, Mid-market SaaS would be the top-priority segment, Enterprise Finance would be a secondary pursuit, and SMB Retail would be monitored but not actively targeted.

## When to Revisit

- Quarterly as a standard cadence
- Immediately after major competitive moves (new entrant, acquisition, price change)
- When upstream inputs change (new market problems discovered, distinctive competence shifts)
- When SOM assumptions prove wrong (actual win rates differ significantly from estimates)
- When entering a new product cycle or planning period
