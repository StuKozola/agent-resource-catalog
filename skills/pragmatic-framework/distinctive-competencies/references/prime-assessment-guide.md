## 1. Pre-Assessment Checklist

Before starting the PRIME assessment, ensure these inputs are available (or flag gaps to the user):

**Must-Have Inputs:**
- Access to 3-5 people from different functions (product, engineering, sales, customer success, leadership)
- At least some customer-facing evidence (win/loss data, NPS comments, testimonials, case studies, support tickets)

**Nice-to-Have Inputs:**
- Formal win/loss analysis report (Pragmatic Framework: Win/Loss Analysis box)
- Competitive landscape document (Pragmatic Framework: Competitive Landscape box)
- Asset inventory (Pragmatic Framework: Asset Assessment box)
- Recent analyst reports or industry benchmarks
- Employee engagement or culture survey data (can reveal embedded expertise and methods)

**If inputs are missing**, help the user identify quick ways to gather initial evidence. A lightweight version of the assessment can proceed with just internal stakeholder interviews and a few customer data points, but flag that market validation is essential before finalizing.

## 2. PRIME Dimension Deep Dives

### Purpose

**What to explore:**
- Company mission and vision statements (as written vs. as practiced)
- The founding story — what problem motivated the company's creation?
- What customer outcomes does the organization most care about enabling?
- How do employees describe what the company does when talking to friends or family?
- Where does the company invest disproportionately relative to competitors?

**Probing questions for workshops:**
- "If we could only solve one problem for our customers, what would it be?"
- "What would our most loyal customers say our purpose is?"
- "Where do we invest time and resources even when it's not the most profitable thing to do?"
- "What requests do we say no to because they don't align with who we are?"

**Watch for:** Companies often have a stated purpose that differs from their enacted purpose. The distinctive competency lives in the enacted version. If the company says its purpose is "empowering small businesses" but its biggest investments go into enterprise features, there's a disconnect worth surfacing.

### Reputation

**What to explore:**
- How customers describe you in reviews, case studies, and references
- What you're known for in the industry (analyst reports, press coverage, conference topics)
- What customers expect you to offer vs. what surprises them
- Brand associations — both positive and unintended
- History of successful product introductions — what patterns exist?

**Probing questions for workshops:**
- "What would customers say if asked 'What is [Company] best at?'"
- "What do prospects assume about us before the first sales call?"
- "What new product or service could we launch tomorrow that customers would find completely natural?"
- "What new product or service would surprise customers — 'That doesn't sound like them'?"

**Watch for:** Reputation can be a distinctive competency in itself (brand recognition, trust in a specific domain), but it can also mask declining distinctiveness. A company may be known for something it was great at five years ago. Validate current reputation with recent evidence.

### Innovation

**What to explore:**
- Products or features that were first-to-market or redefined a category
- Novel approaches to solving common industry problems
- Unique perspectives or mental models the company brings to its work
- Patents, proprietary algorithms, or unique technical approaches
- Creative business model innovations (pricing, delivery, packaging)

**Probing questions for workshops:**
- "What have we built that made competitors take notice?"
- "What unique insight about our market drives our product decisions?"
- "Where do we approach a problem differently from everyone else in our industry?"
- "What do we know about our customers' world that competitors don't seem to understand?"

**Watch for:** Innovation doesn't have to mean technology. A distinctive innovative approach to customer onboarding, a unique pricing model, or a novel way of combining existing technologies can all be genuine distinctive competencies. Don't let the conversation default to "we have cool tech."

### Methods

**What to explore:**
- Proprietary processes for product development, delivery, or customer support
- Operational systems that create consistency and scalability
- Quality assurance or testing methodologies unique to the company
- Supply chain or logistics advantages
- Thinking frameworks or decision-making systems embedded in the culture
- Certifications, standards, or compliance capabilities

**Probing questions for workshops:**
- "What systems do we have that we'd be afraid to lose?"
- "What processes do new employees find most surprising or impressive?"
- "What operational capability would be hardest for a competitor to build from scratch?"
- "Where does our way of doing things create measurably better outcomes?"

**Watch for:** Methods are often invisible to the people who use them every day. They're the "water we swim in." Cross-functional perspectives are especially valuable here — sales may not know about a manufacturing advantage, and engineering may not realize the support team has a proprietary triage system. Also look for methods that combine multiple elements in ways that are hard to decompose and copy.

### Expertise

**What to explore:**
- Deep domain knowledge in specific industries, technologies, or problem areas
- Key individuals whose expertise is recognized externally (speakers, authors, advisors)
- Organizational knowledge that's been codified and shared broadly
- Training programs or knowledge management systems
- Thought leadership presence (publications, conferences, advisory boards)
- Regulatory or compliance expertise

**Probing questions for workshops:**
- "What questions do customers, partners, or analysts come to us for that they wouldn't ask anyone else?"
- "Where is our expertise deepest — and is it in a few people or broadly distributed?"
- "What could we teach a masterclass on that competitors couldn't?"
- "If our top 3 domain experts left tomorrow, what would we lose that competitors couldn't offer?"

**Watch for:** Expertise concentrated in a few individuals is a risk, not just a competency. If the distinctive competency depends on specific people, the assessment should flag this and the strategy should include knowledge distribution plans. Also distinguish between expertise your market values and expertise that's technically impressive but not commercially relevant.

## 3. Validation Framework

After brainstorming, every candidate competency must be tested. Use this three-gate validation:

### Gate 1: Uniqueness Test
- Can any direct competitor claim the same thing credibly?
- If a prospect asked your top 3 competitors about this capability, what would they say?
- What specifically makes your version of this competency different?
- Source: Competitive Landscape analysis, analyst reports, competitor marketing

**Pass criteria:** Competitors cannot make a credible equivalent claim, OR your version is demonstrably superior in a way the market recognizes.

### Gate 2: Value Test
- Do customers mention this when explaining why they chose you?
- Does this capability show up in win/loss data as a decision factor?
- Would removing this capability cause customers to leave?
- Source: Win/loss interviews, customer satisfaction data, renewal/churn analysis

**Pass criteria:** Market evidence shows customers recognize, value, and factor this into purchase decisions.

### Gate 3: Durability Test
- How long would it take a well-funded competitor to replicate this?
- Is the competency rooted in things that are hard to copy (culture, accumulated expertise, network effects, proprietary data)?
- Could a startup with $50M in funding replicate this within 2 years?
- Source: Internal assessment, industry knowledge

**Pass criteria:** Replication would require years of investment, unique historical circumstances, or capabilities that can't simply be purchased.

### Scoring
For each candidate competency, rate each gate on a 1-3 scale:
- 3 = Strong pass (clear evidence)
- 2 = Moderate pass (some evidence, room for debate)
- 1 = Weak/fail (limited evidence or easily matched)

**Total 7-9:** Strong distinctive competency — build strategy around it
**Total 5-6:** Emerging distinctive competency — worth developing further
**Total 3-4:** Core competency or aspirational — don't position on this yet

## 4. Common Pitfalls

**Pitfall 1: The Echo Chamber**
Teams convince themselves they're distinctive without checking with the market. Counter this by requiring external evidence (win/loss, customer quotes) for every claimed competency.

**Pitfall 2: Confusing Features with Competencies**
"We have feature X" is not a distinctive competency. The competency is the underlying capability that enables the feature and makes it hard to replicate. A feature can be copied; the organizational capability that produced it often cannot.

**Pitfall 3: Historical Nostalgia**
"We were the first to..." is only relevant if you're still ahead. Markets evolve. Validate that past innovations still translate to current competitive advantage.

**Pitfall 4: Expertise Fragility**
Claiming expertise as a distinctive competency when it lives in 2-3 people's heads. If those people leave, the competency evaporates. Flag this as a risk and recommend knowledge distribution.

**Pitfall 5: Too Many Competencies**
If everything is distinctive, nothing is. Most companies have 2-4 genuine distinctive competencies. More than that usually means the bar is too low. Raise the validation standards.

**Pitfall 6: Ignoring the "So What?"**
A competency is only strategically valuable if it connects to a market problem customers care about. Being distinctively great at something nobody values isn't an advantage.

## 5. Example Assessment Questions by Role

**For Product Managers:**
- What product capabilities do customers cite most in renewals and expansions?
- Where do we win deals that we "shouldn't" win on paper?
- What do customers say they can't get anywhere else?

**For Sales:**
- What talking points consistently resonate in competitive deals?
- When we win against [top competitor], what's the most common reason?
- What objections do you never hear (suggesting an uncontested strength)?

**For Engineering/Technical:**
- What technical capabilities would take a competitor years to build?
- What's our most defensible technical advantage?
- Where does our architecture or approach fundamentally differ from competitors?

**For Customer Success:**
- What capabilities drive the highest customer satisfaction?
- Why do customers stay when competitors offer lower prices?
- What part of the product experience generates the most positive feedback?

**For Leadership:**
- What investments have we made that competitors haven't matched?
- Where would you bet the company's future?
- What organizational capability do you think is most underappreciated externally?

## 6. Connecting Results to Strategy

Once distinctive competencies are validated, map them forward to downstream Pragmatic Framework activities:

### → Innovation
For each distinctive competency, ask: "What market problems could we solve better than anyone else by extending this competency?" This directly feeds the Innovation box's mandate to focus creative effort on areas where you have a right to win.

### → Positioning
Each distinctive competency should map to one or more positioning statements. The positioning document should articulate how each competency translates into customer value. Pattern: "[Our distinctive competency] enables us to [solve specific market problem] in a way that [specific differentiator vs. alternatives]."

### → Market Definition
Target market segments where your distinctive competencies have the highest relevance. Pragmatic principle: "Use market segments that add value to your distinctive competence." If your competency is in regulated-industry compliance expertise, your primary segments should be in regulated industries.

### → Build/Buy/Partner
Distinctive competencies inform what you should build internally (where you're strong), what you should partner on (adjacent to your strengths), and what you should buy (outside your distinctive areas).

### → Product Roadmap
Roadmap items that leverage distinctive competencies should be prioritized over those that attempt to build capabilities from scratch in areas where competitors are already strong.
