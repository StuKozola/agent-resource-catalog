## The Problem → Solution → Results Structure

Every effective case study, regardless of length or format, follows a three-act narrative. This is not optional — it is the structural backbone that makes customer stories compelling and credible.

### Act 1: The Problem (Situation + Challenge)

This is the hook. The reader needs to recognize themselves in the customer's situation. Ground the problem in market reality, not product features.

**What to capture:**
- The customer's industry, size, and relevant context
- The specific business challenge or pain point they faced
- Why the problem mattered (financial impact, operational friction, competitive risk, compliance pressure)
- What they had tried before and why it wasn't working
- The urgency or trigger that drove them to seek a solution

**Writing principles:**
- Use the customer's own language, not vendor jargon
- Be specific about the problem ("manually reconciling 3,000 invoices per month took 120 staff-hours") not vague ("they had operational challenges")
- Connect the problem to a market problem your positioning addresses — this is where Advocacy and Positioning intersect
- The reader should think: "That sounds like my situation"

### Act 2: The Solution (Selection + Implementation)

This is the bridge. Show how the customer evaluated options, chose your product, and put it to work.

**What to capture:**
- How they found or learned about your product (this validates your awareness and nurturing programs)
- What criteria they used to evaluate solutions (this maps to your buyer experience and competitive positioning)
- Why they chose you over alternatives (this is gold for sales enablement)
- How implementation worked (timeline, key decisions, support received)
- Any challenges during implementation and how they were overcome (authenticity builds credibility)

**Writing principles:**
- Don't skip the selection process — prospects care deeply about why a peer chose you
- Be honest about implementation realities; a case study that glosses over challenges feels like marketing, not evidence
- Show the people involved — names, titles, roles — to humanize the story
- Keep this section proportional; it should be shorter than Problem and Results

### Act 3: The Results (Outcomes + Impact)

This is the payoff. Quantified results are the primary reason prospects read case studies.

**What to capture:**
- Measurable business outcomes (revenue increase, cost reduction, time saved, risk reduced, efficiency gained)
- Operational improvements (process changes, team productivity, scalability)
- Strategic impact (competitive advantage, market expansion, customer satisfaction)
- Unexpected benefits (secondary gains the customer didn't anticipate)
- Future plans (expansion, additional use cases — this signals ongoing satisfaction)

**Writing principles:**
- Lead with the strongest quantified result
- Use specific numbers whenever possible ("reduced processing time from 4 hours to 20 minutes" not "significantly reduced processing time")
- If the customer can't share exact numbers, use directional language with approval ("reduced costs by more than 30%")
- Include both hard metrics (dollars, percentages, time) and soft outcomes (team morale, customer satisfaction, strategic confidence)
- End with a forward-looking statement or customer quote that reinforces the ongoing value

## The Case Study Litmus Test

Pragmatic Institute's standard for whether a case study is effective. Before publishing, every case study should pass all five tests:

### Test 1: Real Customer, Real Problem
Does the case study feature a real (or credibly anonymized) customer facing a genuine market problem? Fictional composites fail this test. If the customer must be anonymous, provide enough context (industry, company size, region) for the reader to recognize the profile as credible.

### Test 2: Market Problem Alignment
Does the problem described align with the market problems your positioning addresses? If the case study tells a story about a problem your product doesn't centrally solve, it creates confusion. The case study should reinforce positioning, not dilute it.

### Test 3: Specific Solution Connection
Does the reader understand how your product specifically addressed the problem? Generic phrases like "leveraged the platform to transform their operations" fail this test. The reader needs to understand what the customer actually did with your product.

### Test 4: Quantified Results
Does the case study include at least one (preferably 2-3) quantifiable result? Results don't have to be financial — time saved, errors reduced, adoption rates, or satisfaction scores all count. But they must be specific numbers, not vague claims.

### Test 5: Persona Recognition
Would the target buyer persona reading this case study see themselves in the story? This means the customer's industry, company size, role, and challenge should map to one of your defined buyer personas. A case study aimed at IT Directors should feature an IT Director's perspective, language, and concerns.

## Interview Guide

### Before the Interview

**Preparation:**
- Review the customer's account history (product usage, support tickets, implementation notes, renewal history, any data from Customer Success)
- Identify the specific results or outcomes you believe they've achieved
- Research the customer's company (recent news, industry context, competitive landscape)
- Prepare 10-15 open-ended questions organized around Problem → Solution → Results
- Send the customer a brief overview of what the interview will cover so they can gather any data or metrics in advance
- Confirm recording permission before the interview

### The Interview Questions

**Opening / Context (5 minutes)**
1. Tell me about your role and responsibilities at [Company].
2. Give me a brief overview of [Company] — your industry, size, and who you serve.

**The Problem (10-15 minutes)**
3. What was the business challenge or pain point that led you to look for a solution?
4. How was this problem affecting your team or organization day-to-day?
5. Can you put a number on the impact? (Time spent, costs incurred, revenue at risk, errors encountered?)
6. What had you tried before, and why wasn't it working?
7. What was the tipping point that made you decide to act?

**The Solution (10 minutes)**
8. How did you first learn about [Product]?
9. What other solutions did you evaluate? What criteria were most important?
10. What ultimately made you choose [Product] over the alternatives?
11. Walk me through the implementation — how did it go? Were there any challenges?

**The Results (10-15 minutes)**
12. What results have you seen since implementing [Product]?
13. Can you share specific metrics? (Revenue impact, cost savings, time saved, efficiency gains?)
14. Were there any unexpected benefits — things you didn't anticipate?
15. How has this impacted your team's day-to-day work?
16. What would you say to someone in a similar role who is evaluating solutions like this?

**Closing (5 minutes)**
17. What are your plans going forward with [Product]?
18. Is there anything else you'd want a prospective buyer to know?

### During the Interview

- **Listen more than you talk.** Your job is to draw out the customer's story, not validate your product narrative.
- **Follow the energy.** When the customer gets animated about a specific point, go deeper. Those moments produce the best quotes.
- **Capture exact phrases.** When a customer says something vivid, specific, or emotionally resonant, note it verbatim. These become pull-quotes and headlines.
- **Probe for numbers.** If the customer says "it was much faster," ask "can you estimate how much faster? Hours? Days? Percentage?" Push gently for specificity.
- **Don't lead the witness.** Ask "what results did you see?" not "did you see a 30% improvement like other customers?" Let the story be theirs.

### After the Interview

- Transcribe key sections within 24 hours while context is fresh
- Identify the 2-3 strongest quantified results
- Select 3-5 candidate pull-quotes
- Draft a one-paragraph summary and share it with the customer for a quick accuracy check before full drafting

## Writing the Case Study

### Structural Template

**Title:** Results-led headline featuring the strongest metric
- Good: "[Company] Reduces Invoice Processing Time by 75% with [Product]"
- Weak: "[Company] Case Study" or "How [Company] Uses [Product]"

**Snapshot / Executive Summary (50-75 words):**
A brief overview of the customer, the problem, and the headline result. This is for the reader who only skims.

**At-a-Glance Sidebar:**
- Customer name and logo
- Industry
- Company size (employees, revenue, or other relevant metric)
- Products used
- Key results (3 bullet points with numbers)

**The Challenge (200-400 words):**
Act 1 of the narrative. Describe the customer's situation and the specific problem. Use the customer's language. Include a quote that captures the frustration or urgency.

**The Solution (200-400 words):**
Act 2. How they found you, why they chose you, and how implementation worked. Include a quote about the selection or implementation experience.

**The Results (300-500 words):**
Act 3. Lead with quantified outcomes. Layer in operational and strategic benefits. Include a quote that captures satisfaction and forward momentum.

**Looking Ahead (50-100 words):**
Brief closing on future plans and the ongoing relationship. End with a strong forward-looking customer quote.

### Writing Style

- **Voice:** Third-person narrative with embedded first-person customer quotes. The narrator (you) provides context and structure; the customer provides proof and emotion.
- **Tone:** Professional, confident, specific. Avoid superlatives and marketing hyperbole. Let the numbers speak.
- **Length:** 800-1,500 words for a standard written case study. Shorter is better if the story is clear.
- **Readability:** Use subheadings, pull-quotes, and visual call-outs to make the case study scannable. Many readers will skim before (if ever) reading in full.
- **Jargon:** Use the customer's industry language, not your product's internal terminology. If the customer calls it "inventory reconciliation," don't call it "asset synchronization."

## Format Variations

The same customer story can be repurposed into multiple formats:

**One-Page Summary:** Condensed version (300-400 words) for quick consumption in sales meetings, email attachments, or trade show handouts.

**Slide Format:** 4-6 slides (Problem, Solution, Results, Quote) for sales presentations and webinar decks.

**Video Script:** Adapted narrative for a 60-90 second testimonial or 3-5 minute case study video. Focus on the most visual and emotional elements.

**Blog Post:** More conversational retelling of the story for the company blog, incorporating SEO keywords and linking to the full case study.

**Social Media Series:** 3-5 posts extracting key quotes, stats, and insights from the case study for LinkedIn, Twitter/X, and other platforms.

**Sales One-Pager:** Highly compressed version with just the headline result, 2-3 bullet points, and a customer quote — designed to be included in proposal packages.

**Infographic:** Visual representation of the customer's journey from problem to results, using icons, data points, and pull-quotes.

## Approval and Publication

### Customer Review Process

1. **Draft Review:** Send the complete draft to the customer contact who was interviewed. Give them 5-7 business days to review.
2. **Feedback Incorporation:** Make requested changes promptly. Customers may want to soften language, adjust numbers, or remove specific details. Accommodate their requests — this is their story.
3. **Internal Review:** If the customer's organization requires legal, PR, or communications team approval, build this into the timeline (add 2-4 weeks for enterprise customers).
4. **Final Sign-Off:** Get written confirmation (email is fine) that the customer approves the final version for publication.
5. **Logo Usage:** If you plan to use the customer's company logo, get explicit permission for logo usage — this is often a separate approval from the content itself.

### Publication Checklist

Before publishing, confirm:
- [ ] Customer has approved the final version in writing
- [ ] Logo usage permission obtained (if applicable)
- [ ] All quoted metrics have been verified with the customer
- [ ] The case study has been reviewed by your own legal/compliance team (if required)
- [ ] The case study has been tagged with relevant metadata (industry, company size, product, persona, use case) for easy retrieval
- [ ] Distribution plan is in place (website, sales enablement, nurturing sequences, social media, events)
- [ ] Sales team has been notified that a new case study is available and briefed on when to use it
- [ ] The advocacy database has been updated with this activity
