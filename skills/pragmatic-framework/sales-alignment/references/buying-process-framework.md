## 1. Standard B2B Buying Stages

The following stages represent a generalized B2B buying process. Adapt terminology and boundaries to the user's specific market. The critical principle from Pragmatic is that the *market* defines these stages — not the selling organization.

### Stage 1: Problem Recognition

**What the buyer is doing:**
- Experiencing pain with the status quo — inefficiency, cost overruns, missed opportunities, compliance risk, or competitive pressure
- Discussing the problem informally with peers, colleagues, or industry contacts
- Beginning to quantify the cost of inaction (often roughly)

**What the buyer is thinking:**
- "Is this problem big enough to warrant action?"
- "Can we solve this ourselves or do we need outside help?"
- "Who else in the organization feels this pain?"

**Key personas active:** End users experiencing the pain; functional managers noticing impact on KPIs

**Typical buyer activities:**
- Internal conversations about frustrations
- Ad hoc research (Google searches, peer conversations, industry forums)
- Reading industry content that frames the problem
- Attending webinars or conferences where the problem category is discussed

**Barriers at this stage:**
- Status quo bias — "We've always done it this way"
- Lack of urgency — problem is known but not prioritized
- No internal champion willing to push for change
- Problem is distributed across the organization so no single owner emerges

**Information needs:**
- Content that helps the buyer articulate and quantify their problem
- Industry benchmarks showing the cost of the problem
- Peer stories ("companies like us faced this and here's what happened")

---

### Stage 2: Research & Education

**What the buyer is doing:**
- Actively researching the problem space (not yet shopping for solutions)
- Consuming content: analyst reports, blog posts, webinars, peer reviews
- Building internal awareness — socializing the problem with stakeholders
- Estimating the scope of the problem and potential budget range

**What the buyer is thinking:**
- "What are the different approaches to solving this?"
- "What have other companies in our situation done?"
- "How big is this problem really, and what would solving it be worth?"

**Key personas active:** Champion (often a functional manager); beginning to involve economic buyer for budget awareness

**Typical buyer activities:**
- Reading 3-7 pieces of content from various sources
- Attending industry webinars
- Consulting with peers in their network
- Engaging with early-stage vendor content (thought leadership, not product-focused)

**Barriers at this stage:**
- Information overload — too many sources, conflicting perspectives
- Difficulty quantifying the problem in terms that resonate with economic buyers
- Internal politics — other priorities competing for attention and budget

**Information needs:**
- Educational content about the problem category (not product pitches)
- Frameworks for evaluating the problem's severity
- ROI models or business case templates the buyer can adapt
- Case studies focused on the problem (not the solution vendor)

---

### Stage 3: Solution Exploration

**What the buyer is doing:**
- Identifying categories of solutions that could address the problem
- Building an initial awareness of vendors in each category
- Determining whether to build, buy, or partner
- Beginning to involve more stakeholders (IT, procurement, compliance)

**What the buyer is thinking:**
- "What types of solutions exist?"
- "Should we build this in-house, buy a product, or hire a service?"
- "Who are the players in this space?"
- "What's the general cost range?"

**Key personas active:** Champion driving research; technical buyer beginning evaluation; economic buyer monitoring

**Typical buyer activities:**
- Reviewing analyst reports (Gartner, Forrester, G2, etc.)
- Visiting vendor websites to understand product categories
- Asking peers for vendor recommendations
- Attending vendor-sponsored webinars (solution-oriented)

**Barriers at this stage:**
- Category confusion — similar-sounding solutions with different approaches
- Premature vendor selection based on brand awareness rather than fit
- Internal disagreement about whether to build vs. buy

**Information needs:**
- Category comparisons (approaches, not just vendors)
- Build vs. buy analysis frameworks
- High-level solution architecture or approach descriptions
- Analyst reports or market guides

---

### Stage 4: Requirements Definition

**What the buyer is doing:**
- Defining specific requirements based on their problem and organizational constraints
- Building a formal or informal shortlist of vendors (typically 3-5)
- Creating evaluation criteria (must-haves, nice-to-haves, deal-breakers)
- Engaging procurement or finance for budget approval
- Forming a buying committee or evaluation team

**What the buyer is thinking:**
- "What specific capabilities do we need?"
- "What are our technical, security, and compliance requirements?"
- "Which vendors should we evaluate more deeply?"
- "What's the total cost of ownership?"

**Key personas active:** Full buying committee forming — technical buyer, economic buyer, champion, end users, procurement, IT/security

**Typical buyer activities:**
- Writing RFP/RFI or informal requirements documents
- Initial vendor outreach for information (not yet for demos)
- Internal alignment meetings on priorities and criteria
- Budget discussions and preliminary approval

**Barriers at this stage:**
- Stakeholder misalignment on priorities
- Requirements bloat (including everything, prioritizing nothing)
- Budget constraints or competing budget requests
- Decision paralysis from too many options

**Information needs:**
- Requirements checklists or evaluation guides the buyer can customize
- Integration and architecture documentation
- Security, compliance, and data handling specifications
- Pricing models and TCO frameworks
- Reference customers in similar industries or use cases

---

### Stage 5: Evaluation & Validation

**What the buyer is doing:**
- Conducting deep evaluation of shortlisted vendors (demos, POCs, trials)
- Checking references and reading third-party reviews
- Testing specific use cases against product capabilities
- Comparing vendors on both functional and non-functional criteria

**What the buyer is thinking:**
- "Does this actually work for our specific situation?"
- "Can we trust this vendor long-term?"
- "What's the implementation risk?"
- "How does this compare to the other finalists?"
- "What will my team's experience be like day-to-day?"

**Key personas active:** Technical buyer leading evaluation; end users in trials; champion building internal consensus; economic buyer reviewing business case

**Typical buyer activities:**
- Attending customized (not generic) product demos
- Running proof-of-concept or pilot programs
- Conducting reference calls with existing customers
- Reading detailed case studies and implementation stories
- Negotiating preliminary terms

**Barriers at this stage:**
- Generic demos that don't address specific use cases
- Vendor claims that don't match reference feedback
- Implementation concerns (complexity, timeline, resource requirements)
- Internal champion unable to build consensus across the buying committee
- "No decision" risk — evaluation fatigue leading to abandonment

**Information needs:**
- Customized demo environments or sandbox access
- Detailed implementation plans and timelines
- Named reference customers willing to speak
- Competitive comparison content (honest, not dismissive)
- ROI calculators with customizable inputs
- Content the champion can use to sell internally (executive summaries, one-pagers)

---

### Stage 6: Decision & Negotiation

**What the buyer is doing:**
- Making final vendor selection
- Negotiating pricing, terms, and contract details
- Securing final budget approval and stakeholder sign-off
- Planning implementation logistics
- Managing internal change management (preparing the organization)

**What the buyer is thinking:**
- "Are we making the right choice?"
- "What's the risk if this doesn't work out?"
- "Can we negotiate better terms?"
- "How do I get final sign-off from leadership?"
- "What does the first 90 days look like?"

**Key personas active:** Economic buyer (final approval); procurement (contract terms); champion (internal advocacy); legal (contract review)

**Typical buyer activities:**
- Final vendor presentations to executive stakeholders
- Contract negotiation and legal review
- Internal business case presentation for final approval
- Implementation planning kickoff
- Change management communication planning

**Barriers at this stage:**
- Last-minute stakeholder objections
- Procurement delays or compliance review bottlenecks
- Budget reallocation or freeze
- Buyer's remorse or second-guessing
- Competitive displacement (late-arriving alternative)

**Information needs:**
- Executive-level business case materials
- Contract templates and negotiation flexibility guidance
- Implementation timeline and resource commitment details
- Customer success stories from similar organizations
- Risk mitigation documentation (SLAs, guarantees, exit clauses)

---

### Stage 7: Implementation & Adoption

**What the buyer is doing:**
- Onboarding with the selected vendor
- Deploying the solution within the organization
- Training users and managing change
- Measuring early results against expectations
- Identifying expansion or upsell opportunities (or problems)

**What the buyer is thinking:**
- "Is this living up to our expectations?"
- "Are our people actually using it?"
- "When will we see measurable results?"
- "Did we make the right decision?"

**Key personas active:** End users (adoption); technical buyer (integration); champion (internal advocacy for continued investment); economic buyer (ROI validation)

**Information needs:**
- Onboarding documentation and training materials
- Best practice guides and quick-start resources
- Success milestones and measurement frameworks
- Community access and peer learning opportunities
- Expansion and optimization guidance

## 2. Persona Roles in the Buying Process

Understanding persona roles is essential to Sales Alignment. Each persona has different information needs, decision criteria, and engagement patterns.

### Economic Buyer
- **Role:** Controls or approves budget; makes or ratifies final purchase decision
- **Primary concern:** Business impact, ROI, risk mitigation, strategic fit
- **Engagement pattern:** Less active in early stages; increasingly involved from Requirements onward; makes final decision
- **Content preferences:** Executive summaries, business case documents, ROI analyses, peer testimonials from other executives

### Technical Buyer
- **Role:** Evaluates technical fit, integration requirements, security, and compliance
- **Primary concern:** Architecture, integration, security, scalability, vendor stability
- **Engagement pattern:** Active from Solution Exploration through Evaluation; may gate the process with technical requirements
- **Content preferences:** Technical documentation, architecture diagrams, API references, security whitepapers, compliance certifications

### Champion
- **Role:** Internal advocate who drives the buying process forward; often the person who first identified the problem
- **Primary concern:** Solving the original problem; looking good internally for sponsoring a successful initiative
- **Engagement pattern:** Active throughout the entire process; the seller's primary contact and ally
- **Content preferences:** Materials they can share internally to build consensus — one-pagers, executive summaries, competitive comparisons, ROI snapshots

### End User
- **Role:** Will actually use the product day-to-day
- **Primary concern:** Ease of use, workflow fit, learning curve, day-to-day experience
- **Engagement pattern:** Active in Problem Recognition (experiencing the pain) and Evaluation (testing usability); may resist change
- **Content preferences:** Demo videos, trial access, user stories, implementation guides, training materials

### Procurement / Legal
- **Role:** Manages vendor qualification, contract negotiation, and compliance
- **Primary concern:** Terms, pricing, compliance, risk, vendor viability
- **Engagement pattern:** Active in Requirements (vendor qualification) and Decision (contract negotiation)
- **Content preferences:** Pricing documentation, compliance certifications, standard contract terms, vendor qualification questionnaires

## 4. Buying Process Diagnostic Questions

Use these questions to assess how well the user understands their market's buying process:

**Process Knowledge:**
- How do buyers typically first become aware of the problem your product solves?
- What research do buyers do before engaging with any vendor?
- At what point in their process do buyers first contact your sales team?
- How many people are typically involved in the buying decision?
- What is the average time from first contact to closed deal?

**Alignment Gaps:**
- Where in the buying process do deals most commonly stall?
- What is your "no decision" rate (deals lost to status quo, not competitors)?
- Do you know what information buyers need at each stage of their process?
- Can your sales team articulate the buyer's process, or only your selling process?
- Is your CRM structured around buyer milestones or seller activities?

**Content Readiness:**
- Does content exist for each stage of the buying process?
- Is content organized by buyer persona and buying stage, or by product feature?
- Can the champion easily find and share content to build internal consensus?
- Do sellers know which content to use at which stage?

## 5. Non-Linear Buying Patterns

Modern B2B buying is rarely a clean linear progression. The alignment map must account for common non-linear patterns:

**Loopbacks:** Buyers frequently revisit earlier stages. A failed evaluation may send the buyer back to Solution Exploration. A new stakeholder joining mid-process may require re-education. The selling process must accommodate re-engagement without frustration.

**Parallel Tracks:** Different personas may be in different stages simultaneously. The champion may be in Decision mode while the technical buyer is still in Evaluation. Alignment must address per-persona stage tracking.

**Stage Skipping:** Well-informed buyers may arrive at Evaluation having already completed Research, Education, and Requirements independently. The selling process must detect where the buyer actually is rather than forcing them through early stages.

**Consensus Stalls:** Even when the champion is ready to decide, the buying committee may not be aligned. The selling process needs content and tactics specifically designed to help the champion build internal consensus.

**Reactivation:** Deals that go dark often represent buyers who have paused, not abandoned, the process. The alignment map should include reactivation content and tactics for common pause points.
