## 1. Pre-Interview Preparation

Before the buyer interview, complete the internal sales debrief. Capture:

- **Deal metadata:** deal size, segment, industry, geography, sales cycle length,
  competitors identified, outcome (win/loss/no-decision), date of final decision
- **Sales team perspective:** their explanation for the outcome, the relationship
  history, key buyer contacts and roles, what products/solutions were proposed
- **CRM data:** stage progression, engagement metrics, content consumed, demo history

Use this context to customize your question selection. Not every question below applies
to every interview. Select 10–15 questions total for a 25–30 minute conversation.

---

## 2. Question Sequencing Strategy

Structure the interview as a funnel:

**Minutes 1–3: Opening.** Build rapport. Explain purpose. Get permission to record.

**Minutes 3–8: Context & trigger.** Broad, non-threatening questions about what prompted
the evaluation. These warm up the conversation and establish the business context that
frames everything else.

**Minutes 8–18: Evaluation & comparison.** The meat of the interview. Reconstruct the
evaluation process, explore competitive perceptions, assess product fit, and probe the
sales experience. This is where you spend the most time.

**Minutes 18–25: Decision drivers.** The most critical and sometimes uncomfortable
questions. By this point you've built enough rapport to ask directly why they chose
or didn't choose your solution.

**Minutes 25–30: Forward-looking & close.** What would change their mind? What advice
do they have? Thank them and close.

---

## 3. Opening & Context Questions

These set the tone. Keep it warm, brief, and clear about the purpose.

- "Thank you for taking the time. The goal of this conversation is to learn from your
  experience evaluating [solution category] so we can continuously improve. This isn't
  a sales conversation — there are no wrong answers, and candid feedback is the most
  helpful thing you can give us."

- "Before we dive in, can you give me a quick overview of your role and what you're
  responsible for?"

- "How familiar were you with [your company] before this evaluation began?"

---

## 4. Buying Trigger & Business Context

Understanding what initiated the evaluation grounds all subsequent answers in business
reality rather than abstract preference.

- "What was happening in your business that prompted you to look for a [solution
  category] at that time?"

- "Was there a specific event, pain point, or initiative that kicked off the
  evaluation?"

- "How long had this been a recognized need before you started actively looking?"

- "Who initiated the search — was it driven by a specific team, an executive mandate,
  or something else?"

- "Were you replacing an existing solution, or was this a net-new purchase?"

**Why these matter:** Marketing and sales programs are designed around repeatable buying
triggers. Understanding the conditions that push a prospect from passive awareness to
active evaluation helps you time outreach and craft content that meets buyers where
they are.

## 5. Evaluation Process & Criteria

Reconstruct how the buyer actually evaluated alternatives — the sequence of events,
conversations, and decisions that shaped their shortlist.

- "Walk me through how you went about evaluating your options. Where did you start?"

- "How did you build your initial shortlist of vendors to consider?"

- "What were the top 3–5 criteria that mattered most in your evaluation? How did you
  weight them?"

- "Did you issue a formal RFP, or was the process more informal?"

- "How many solutions did you seriously evaluate? Who were they?"

- "Which stakeholders were involved in the evaluation, and what were their respective
  priorities?"

- "At what point in your research did you first contact vendors directly? How much of
  your evaluation was self-directed?"

- "Were there any criteria that you expected to matter but ended up being less
  important than you thought?"

**Why these matter:** The stated criteria and the actual decision drivers are often
different. This section reveals the real evaluation framework — including who had
influence, what got weighted most, and where buyers did their own research vs. relying
on vendor input.

---

## 6. Competitive Comparison

How the buyer perceived your offering relative to alternatives. Handle with care —
frame questions neutrally to avoid leading the buyer toward a particular answer.

- "How would you describe the key differences between the solutions you evaluated?"

- "Were there areas where [your company] stood out — positively or negatively —
  compared to the alternatives?"

- "What did you think was [Competitor X]'s biggest strength? What about their biggest
  weakness?"

- "Was there a point during the evaluation where one vendor clearly pulled ahead? What
  drove that?"

- "Did any vendor surprise you — either positively or negatively — during the process?"

- "If you had to describe [your company]'s solution in one sentence to a colleague,
  what would you say?"

**Why these matter:** Competitive intelligence gathered through win/loss is uniquely
valuable because it comes from someone who directly compared you against alternatives
in a real buying scenario — not from an analyst report or your own assumptions.

## 7. Product & Solution Fit

Assess how well the product met the buyer's functional and technical requirements.

- "How well did [your product] meet your core requirements?"

- "Were there specific capabilities or features that were must-haves for you? Did we
  meet them?"

- "Were there any gaps in our product that concerned you during the evaluation?"

- "How important was [specific capability relevant to the deal] in your decision?"

- "Did you evaluate our product's ability to integrate with your existing systems?
  How did that go?"

- "How did you assess the long-term viability and roadmap of the solutions you
  evaluated?"

---

## 8. Sales & Marketing Experience

How the buyer experienced your go-to-market during the evaluation. This section often
surfaces actionable improvements for sales enablement and marketing content.

- "What was your overall impression of [your company] after going through the sales
  process?"

- "What, if anything, was difficult or confusing during the buying process?"

- "How helpful were the sales and marketing materials you received? Were there any
  that stood out — good or bad?"

- "How would you describe the quality of the demo you received? Did it address your
  specific use case?"

- "How responsive and knowledgeable was our team during the evaluation?"

- "Was there information you needed during the evaluation that was hard to find or
  that we didn't provide?"

- "How did you use [your company]'s website, case studies, or other content during
  your research?"

- "Did anything about the sales process make you more or less confident in [your
  company]?"

**Why these matter:** Buyers report that unresponsive sales teams, generic demos,
opaque pricing, and difficulty accessing knowledgeable staff are among the top sales
process complaints. These are fixable problems.

## 9. Pricing & Commercial Terms

Pricing is frequently cited as a win/loss factor but is almost never the root cause on
its own. Use these questions to uncover what the buyer actually means when they say
"price."

- "How did you evaluate the pricing of the solutions you considered?"

- "How transparent was our pricing model? Was it easy to understand how costs were
  structured?"

- "When you compared pricing across vendors, what stood out?"

- "Was there a point where pricing became a deciding factor? If so, what specifically
  about pricing drove that?"

- "If our solution had been priced the same as [competitor], would that have changed
  your decision? Why or why not?"

- "How did you think about the total cost of ownership — not just license fees, but
  implementation, training, and ongoing costs?"

**Why these matter:** When a buyer says "price," they often mean one of several things:
the value proposition didn't justify the premium, a competitor offered comparable
capabilities for less, or the pricing model itself (per-seat, usage-based, etc.) didn't
fit their budget structure. Laddering into the real driver is essential.

## 10. Decision Drivers (Win/Loss Core)

These are the most important questions in the interview. By this point, you should
have enough rapport to ask directly.

**For wins:**
- "What were the top 2–3 factors that led you to choose [your company]?"

- "Was there a single moment or factor that tipped the scales in our favor?"

- "Were there any concerns or reservations you had about choosing us? How did you
  resolve them?"

- "If you were advising a peer in a similar evaluation, what would you tell them about
  [your company]?"

**For losses:**
- "What were the main reasons [your company] was not selected?"

- "Was there a point during the evaluation where we fell behind? What happened?"

- "What would be the most important areas for us to focus on to win your business in
  the future?"

- "If you could change one thing about [your company] or our offering, what would it
  be?"

**For no-decision outcomes:**
- "What led you to decide not to move forward with any solution at this time?"

- "What would need to change — in your business or in the available solutions — for
  you to revisit this?"

- "Is this initiative still on your roadmap, or has the priority shifted?"

---

## 11. Forward-Looking Questions

These help you understand future opportunity and maintain the relationship.

- "Looking ahead, what are the biggest challenges you see in [relevant domain]?"

- "How do you see your needs in this area evolving over the next 12–24 months?"

- "Would you be open to hearing from us again if we made improvements in the areas
  you've mentioned?"

---

## 12. Closing

- "Is there anything else about your experience that we didn't cover that you think
  would be helpful for us to know?"

- "Thank you — this feedback is incredibly valuable. We'll use it to improve."

## 13. Laddering Technique

Laddering is the practice of following each answer 3–4 levels deep to move past surface
explanations to root causes. It's the single most important interviewing skill in
win/loss.

**How it works:**

Buyer: "We chose Competitor X."
→ "Can you tell me more about what made them the right choice?"
Buyer: "Their pricing was better."
→ "When you say better — was it the price level, the pricing model, or something else?"
Buyer: "The model. They offered usage-based pricing and yours was per-seat."
→ "What about usage-based pricing was important to you?"
Buyer: "Our team size varies a lot seasonally. Per-seat would've meant paying for
capacity we only use 4 months a year."
→ Now you've reached the real insight: your pricing model doesn't fit seasonal businesses.

**Rules of laddering:**
- Never accept the first answer as the complete answer
- Use "tell me more about that" and "what do you mean by..." as your workhorse probes
- When a buyer says "price," always ladder — it's rarely about the number alone
- When a buyer gives you a compliment, ladder there too — understand what specifically
  resonated and why
- Stop laddering when you've reached a specific, actionable insight or the buyer starts
  repeating themselves

## 14. Adapting Questions by Deal Type

Not every interview uses the same question set. Adapt based on context:

**Enterprise / complex sale:** Emphasize stakeholder mapping, committee dynamics,
procurement process, RFP questions, and implementation concerns. These deals have
6–10 people influencing the decision.

**SMB / velocity sale:** Emphasize buying trigger, self-service research, demo
experience, and speed of the sales process. These buyers do more independent research
and decide faster.

**Competitive displacement (replacing an incumbent):** Add questions about what
triggered dissatisfaction with the current vendor, switching costs, and change
management concerns.

**Greenfield (no prior solution):** Emphasize business case justification, internal
champions, and the "do nothing" alternative. Understand why they decided to invest now.

**Renewal / expansion loss:** Focus on the customer experience, product adoption,
support quality, and value realization since purchase. Different from net-new win/loss.
