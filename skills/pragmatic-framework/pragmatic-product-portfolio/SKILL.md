# Product Portfolio — Pragmatic Framework (2027)

## Where this fits in the 2027 Pragmatic Framework

- **Category:** Focus — *Where should we play, and where should we invest?*
- **Focus-area question:** *How do our offers fit together, and where should we invest?*
- **Position:** Above the bar (market-focused)
- **Other focus areas in this category:** Market Definition, Distribution Strategy, Product Roadmap

## What This Skill Does

Your product portfolio is every product, service and offer you have that solves market problems.
Managing it like a product, with its own plan and positioning, frees resources, reduces risk,
simplifies selling, and makes investment decisions clearer.

The core moves:

1. **Map each offer** by its lifecycle stage and the problems it solves.
2. **Take a horizon view** to balance short- and long-term goals: core products today, emerging
   products next, and new bets for the future.
3. **Allocate resources based on strategy**, not on which product team argues loudest.
4. **Manage the portfolio as a product**, with its own business plan, positioning, buying
   process, requirements and marketing plan.

### Key Pragmatic Principle

> "Every 'product' needs product management and a business plan."

This applies to portfolios too. A portfolio without its own management drifts into an
uncoordinated collection of products competing internally for resources and confusing the market.

### Connections to Other Focus Areas

- **Product Strategy** (Market) — the portfolio should express the product strategy: what each
  offer is known for, and what the portfolio says no to (`pragmatic-product-strategy`)
- **Market Problems** (Market) — the problems each offer solves (`pragmatic-market-problems`)
- **Market Definition** (Focus) — segments inform which offers belong together
- **Distribution Strategy** (Focus) — channels must work at the portfolio level, not just per product
- **Product Roadmap** (Focus) — individual roadmaps coordinated across the portfolio; a portfolio
  roadmap is one roadmap type (`pragmatic-product-roadmap`)
- **Business Plan** (Business) — the portfolio needs its own business case
- **Pricing** (Business) — bundles, cross-product pricing and upsell paths
- **Buy, Build or Partner** (Business) — portfolio gaps are where these decisions start
- **Business Outcomes** (Business) — portfolio-level results, so no single product's numbers hide
  a weak whole (`pragmatic-business-outcomes`)
- **Positioning** and **Buyer Journey** (Planning) — how the market perceives and buys the whole

## Responsibilities

1. **Integrate products, services and partner offerings into a coherent portfolio focused on
   market problems.** Not "everything we sell," but a deliberate grouping around problems.
2. **Decide where to invest**, using lifecycle stage, problems solved and the horizon view.
3. **Manage the portfolio like a product** — with its own business plan, positioning, buying
   process, market requirements and marketing plan.

## Required Skills

Strategic thinking; financial savvy (portfolio P&L, allocation trade-offs, ROI); judgment about
what belongs; coordination across multiple product teams; experience with multiple products or a
product line.

## How to Use This Skill

### Step 1: Map the Current Portfolio

Document what exists. For each offer:

- What is it (product, service, partner offering, add-on, platform)?
- Which segments does it serve, and **which market problems does it solve**?
- What **lifecycle stage** is it in (introduction, growth, maturity, decline)?
- Which **horizon** does it belong to (core today, emerging next, new bet)?
- What are its revenue, margin and trajectory?
- How does it connect to other offers (cross-sell, dependencies, overlap)?

If the user lacks this, help build a lightweight inventory. See `references/portfolio-inventory.md`.

### Step 2: Take the Horizon View

Classify every offer into one of three horizons:

| Horizon | What it is | Managed for | Typical share of resources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core today** | Proven products generating today's revenue | Performance, efficiency, retention | ~70% |
| **Emerging next** | Offers gaining traction, not yet at scale | Growth, path to profitability | ~20% |
| **New bets** | Experiments and explorations of new problems or markets | Learning, validation | ~10% |

The split is a starting point, not a rule. Look for imbalance: nothing in new bets means no future
pipeline; too much there means today's revenue is at risk. Set criteria for moving offers between
horizons and for stopping bets that don't show promise. See `references/portfolio-analysis.md`.

### Step 3: Decide Where to Invest

With the map and horizon view in hand:

- **Grouping logic** — by segment, problem domain, persona, platform, or a mix?
- **Boundaries** — what's in, what's out
- **Strategic role of each offer** — use BCG, GE-McKinsey, Three Horizons and lifecycle analysis
  (see `references/portfolio-analysis.md`)
- **Gap analysis** — which market problems go unsolved? Which buying stages are unsupported?
  Gaps feed Buy, Build or Partner.
- **Rationalization** — which offers to sunset, merge or spin off
- **Allocation** — shift resources to match the product strategy and the horizon targets

Check every decision against the product strategy. If an offer doesn't serve it, that's a
candidate to say no to.

### Step 4: Build Portfolio-Level Artifacts

This is "manage the portfolio like a product." Help the user create:

1. **Portfolio business plan** — investment thesis, financial model, allocation, risk, and
   fund/delay/stop criteria
2. **Portfolio positioning** — what the *portfolio* does for the market. Format: For [target
   segment] who [have this problem], [portfolio name] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike
   [alternatives], we [key differentiator].
3. **Portfolio buyer journey** — how buyers evaluate and purchase across the portfolio, including
   cross-sell and upsell paths
4. **Portfolio market requirements** — needs that span products and that no single team owns
5. **Portfolio marketing plan** — awareness, engagement and growth at the portfolio level

Templates and guidance: `references/portfolio-artifacts.md`.

### Step 5: Establish Portfolio Governance

- **Review cadence** — quarterly is typical; also review when the market or strategy changes
- **Decision rights** — who decides what enters or exits; escalation path
- **Performance metrics** — revenue mix by horizon, cross-sell rate, portfolio-level customer
  satisfaction, resource use across products, share by segment
- **Coordination** — portfolio councils, shared OKRs, joint roadmap reviews
- **Lifecycle and horizon management** — what triggers a move between stages or horizons; criteria
  for sunsetting

### Step 6: Deliverables

- **Portfolio map** — every offer by segment, problems solved, lifecycle stage, horizon and financials
- **Horizon chart** — offers and resource allocation by horizon, actual vs target
- **Portfolio strategy document** — business plan, positioning and marketing plan (docx skill)
- **Portfolio analysis visual** — BCG matrix, lifecycle map, horizon chart
- **Portfolio review deck** — for leadership (pptx skill)
- **Action plan** — next steps with owners and timelines

## Common Scenarios

**"We have too many products and need to simplify."** Steps 1 and 3 (rationalization). Build
criteria for what stays, goes or merges. Lifecycle and BCG views help most.

**"We're adding a new product."** Steps 2–3. Does it fill a real gap or create overlap? Which
horizon is it, and what will it take resources from?

**"Leadership wants a portfolio strategy presentation."** Steps 1–4 as a deck: current state →
horizon view → recommended composition → investment priorities → roadmap.

**"We need product teams aligned around a portfolio vision."** Steps 4–5. Portfolio positioning
becomes the reference point individual teams align to.

**"I'm new to portfolio management."** Walk all steps at a high level. The mindset shift: from
"collection of things we sell" to "a set of investments organized around market problems."

## Key Principles

1. **Outside-in** — group by the problems offers solve, not by org chart or engineering team.
2. **The portfolio is a product** — it needs its own plan and positioning.
3. **Balance the horizons** — core products fund emerging ones and new bets.
4. **Allocate at the portfolio level** based on strategy and market opportunity.
5. **Simplicity serves the market** — if sales can't explain what to sell to whom, the portfolio
   is too complex.

## Using AI in this focus area

- Consolidate scattered product data (revenue, margins, usage, problems solved) into a draft
  portfolio map.
- Draft horizon classifications and allocation scenarios for the team to challenge.
- Spot overlap by comparing product descriptions, requirements and customer problems across offers.
- Rules: check AI output against real market evidence and actual financials; NIHITO still applies.
  Keep revenue, margin and customer data out of tools not approved for them. The team, not the
  tool, decides where to invest and what to stop.
