# Buyer Experience Document Template

Use this template when creating a Buyer Experience document. Adapt section depth to the user's context — a startup with 10 interviews needs less formality than an enterprise team documenting a multi-segment analysis.

---

## [Product/Solution Name] — Buyer Experience Analysis

### 1. Executive Summary

Write 2–4 paragraphs covering:
- Which buyer persona(s) this document maps
- The market segment and product context
- How the data was gathered (number of interviews, timeframe)
- The single most important finding (lead with the insight that would surprise stakeholders)

**Example opener:**
> This document maps the buying journey of IT Directors at mid-market manufacturing companies evaluating [Product Category]. Based on 14 interviews with recent evaluators conducted in Q3 2025, the analysis reveals that the primary barrier to purchase is not price or feature gaps — it is the difficulty of building internal consensus across plant operations, IT, and finance stakeholders.

---

### 2. Buyer Persona Summary

Provide a compact reference to the persona(s) covered. This is not a full persona document — just enough context so readers understand who is being mapped.

For each persona referenced:
- **Role/Title**: Common job titles
- **Responsibilities**: What they own that makes this purchase relevant
- **Buying Role**: Economic buyer, technical buyer, functional buyer, champion, influencer, or gatekeeper
- **Key Concerns**: Top 2–3 things they care about in an evaluation
- **Success Metrics**: How they measure whether the purchase was a good decision

If the buying committee has multiple personas, list each one and note which persona is the primary focus of this journey map.

---

### 3. Buying Trigger

Document what initiates the buying process. This is the "before the journey" context.

**Common trigger categories:**
- Pain event (system failure, compliance violation, lost customer, security breach)
- Strategic initiative (digital transformation, market expansion, cost reduction mandate)
- Contract/lifecycle event (renewal, end-of-life, vendor dissatisfaction)
- External catalyst (regulation change, competitive pressure, market shift)
- Internal growth (scaling needs, team expansion, process bottleneck)

For each trigger identified:
- Describe the specific scenario
- Note how frequently it occurs based on interview data
- Identify who typically recognizes the trigger and who they escalate to
- Estimate how long between trigger event and active evaluation beginning

### 4. Journey Stages

This is the core of the document. For each stage in the buying journey, document the following:

#### Stage Template

**[Stage Name]**

| Dimension | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Duration** | Typical time spent in this stage |
| **Buyer's Goal** | What the buyer is trying to accomplish |
| **Key Questions** | The specific questions buyers need answered |
| **Activities** | What the buyer does (research, meetings, evaluations, etc.) |
| **Information Sources** | Where they look — websites, peers, analysts, communities, events |
| **Stakeholders Active** | Who is involved and what role they play |
| **Touchpoints with Vendor** | How/whether they interact with your company at this stage |
| **Barriers & Friction** | What slows them down, confuses them, or causes them to stall |
| **Emotional State** | Confidence level, anxiety, excitement, frustration |
| **Content That Helps** | Types of content/assets that move them forward |
| **Red Flags (Stall Indicators)** | Signs the buyer is stuck or about to abandon |

#### Recommended Stage Breakdown

Adapt these to your market. Not every market will have all stages, and some may have additional ones.

**Stage 1: Problem Recognition & Prioritization**
- Buyer realizes they have a problem worth solving
- Internal discussions about severity, urgency, and cost of inaction
- Often happens entirely without vendor involvement
- Key barrier: Competing priorities — the problem may be real but not urgent enough to act on

**Stage 2: Solution Exploration & Education**
- Buyer researches what categories of solutions exist
- Heavy content consumption: blogs, analyst reports, peer communities
- Forming a mental model of the solution landscape
- Key barrier: Information overload, difficulty distinguishing marketing from reality

**Stage 3: Requirements Definition**
- Cross-functional input gathering
- Building internal consensus on what "good" looks like
- May involve formal RFP/RFI or informal requirements list
- Key barrier: Stakeholder disagreement on priorities, scope creep

**Stage 4: Vendor Shortlisting & Evaluation**
- Active comparison of 2–5 vendors
- Demos, trials, proof of concept, reference calls
- Deepest engagement with sales teams
- Key barrier: Difficulty getting meaningful demos, vendor differentiation unclear

**Stage 5: Consensus Building & Internal Approval**
- Champion sells internally to economic buyer, legal, procurement
- Risk mitigation: security reviews, compliance checks, reference validation
- Key barrier: Decision by committee, fear of making wrong choice, budget justification

**Stage 6: Selection, Negotiation & Commitment**
- Final vendor selection
- Contract and pricing negotiation
- Implementation planning discussions
- Key barrier: Procurement bottlenecks, last-minute stakeholder objections, competing vendor counter-offers

### 5. Decision Criteria

Rank the criteria buyers use to compare and select vendors. Base this on interview data, not assumptions.

| Rank | Criterion | Description | Weight/Importance |
|------|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| 1 | | | Critical / High / Medium / Low |
| 2 | | | |
| 3 | | | |

**Common B2B decision criteria categories:**
- Functional fit (does it solve the core problem?)
- Ease of use / user experience
- Integration with existing systems
- Total cost of ownership (not just license price)
- Vendor viability and reputation
- Implementation timeline and complexity
- Customer support and success model
- Security and compliance posture
- Scalability and roadmap
- References and peer validation

Note which criteria matter to which buying role — economic buyers weight cost and ROI; technical buyers weight integration and security; end users weight usability.

### 6. Barriers and Friction Points

Consolidate all barriers identified across journey stages into a single view. Categorize and prioritize them.

**Barrier categories:**
- **Information barriers** — Can't find what they need, content is too generic, pricing not transparent
- **Process barriers** — Too many steps, slow response times, complex procurement requirements
- **People barriers** — Can't reach the right person, stakeholder misalignment, champion lacks authority
- **Risk barriers** — Fear of making wrong choice, switching costs, implementation risk
- **Competitive barriers** — Incumbent advantage, competitor FUD, alternative approaches (including "do nothing")

For each barrier:
- Describe the barrier
- Note which stage(s) it appears in
- Estimate how often it occurs (from interview data)
- Assess severity (deal-breaker vs. friction vs. annoyance)
- Suggest mitigation strategies

### 8. Implications and Recommendations

This section translates findings into action. Organize by function:

**For Positioning & Messaging:**
- Which buyer concerns should positioning prioritize?
- What language do buyers use (vs. internal jargon)?
- Where are messaging gaps relative to the buying journey?

**For Content & Marketing Programs:**
- What content is missing at each stage?
- Which channels and formats match buyer preferences?
- Where are the biggest awareness or education gaps?

**For Sales Process & Enablement:**
- Where should sales engage (and where should they hold back)?
- What tools do sellers need at each stage?
- How should demos and presentations be adapted?
- What objection-handling is needed based on common barriers?

**For Product:**
- Are there product gaps that consistently cause losses?
- Are there UX or trial experience improvements that would reduce evaluation friction?
- What integration or technical requirements keep coming up?

---

### 9. Methodology

Document how the research was conducted so stakeholders can assess credibility.

- Number of interviews conducted
- Breakdown by outcome (wins, losses, no-decisions)
- Breakdown by persona/buying role
- Breakdown by segment (company size, industry, geography)
- Interview timeframe
- Interview method (phone, video, in-person)
- Who conducted interviews (product management, third party, etc.)
- Any supplementary data sources used
- Known limitations or biases in the sample
