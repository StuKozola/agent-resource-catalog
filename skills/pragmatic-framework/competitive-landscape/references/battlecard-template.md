# Battlecard Template

A battlecard is a concise, actionable reference document designed for sales teams. It distills competitive intelligence into something a rep can scan in 60 seconds before or during a customer conversation. The best battlecards answer the question: "I'm going up against [Competitor X] in a deal right now — what do I need to know?"

## Design Principles

**One page, one competitor.** Each battlecard covers a single competitor. If it spills to a second page, cut ruthlessly. Sales reps won't read two pages.

**Action-oriented, not encyclopedic.** A battlecard is not a competitor profile. It's a cheat sheet for winning a deal. Every line should help the rep say or do something specific.

**Written for the field, not the boardroom.** Use direct language. Avoid marketing jargon. Write like you're coaching a peer, not presenting to executives.

**Updated regularly.** A stale battlecard is worse than no battlecard — it gives reps false confidence. Include a prominent "Last Updated" date and establish a quarterly refresh cadence at minimum.

**Tested with sales.** Before finalizing, review battlecards with 2-3 experienced reps. They'll tell you what's missing, what's wrong, and what they'd actually use.

## Battlecard Layout

### Header

```
COMPETITIVE BATTLECARD: [Competitor Name]
Last Updated: [Date]           Confidence: High / Medium / Low
Prepared by: [Product Marketing / Competitive Intel team]
```

### Section 1: Competitor at a Glance (2-3 lines)

A brief snapshot — who they are, who they sell to, and what they lead with. This is for reps who've never heard of this competitor.

Example:
> [Competitor] is a [company size] [type of company] focused on [primary market]. They position as [their key claim]. They're strongest with [buyer type / use case] and typically come up in deals involving [scenario].

### Section 2: When We Win / When We Lose

This is the most important section. Be direct and honest.

**We tend to win when:**
- [Specific scenario, buyer priority, or use case where you have an advantage]
- [Another scenario]
- [Another scenario]

**We tend to lose when:**
- [Specific scenario where the competitor has an advantage]
- [Another scenario]
- [What to watch out for]

Source these from win/loss data wherever possible. If sourced from anecdote or assumption, note it.

### Section 3: Key Differentiators

A quick-reference comparison on the 3-5 dimensions that most often decide competitive deals.

```
| Dimension                    | Us                          | Them                        |
|-----------------------------|-----------------------------|-----------------------------|
| [Key buying criterion 1]    | [Our position + evidence]   | [Their position + evidence] |
| [Key buying criterion 2]    | [Our position]              | [Their position]            |
| [Key buying criterion 3]    | [Our position]              | [Their position]            |
```

Be honest. If they're stronger on a dimension, say so and provide the reframe (see Section 5).

### Section 4: Landmines to Set

Questions or evaluation criteria to introduce early in the buyer's process that tilt the playing field in your favor. These work best when introduced before the buyer has seen the competitor, or before they've built their shortlist.

**Ask the buyer:**
- "[Question that highlights a capability where we excel and they don't]"
- "[Question that introduces a criterion the competitor can't meet]"
- "[Question that reframes the evaluation around our strengths]"

**Suggest the buyer include in their evaluation:**
- [Criterion or requirement that favors us]
- [Proof-of-concept scenario that showcases our advantage]

Important: Landmines must be grounded in genuine buyer value. If a question is transparently self-serving, it backfires. The best landmines are things the buyer should be asking anyway — you're just making sure they do.

### Section 5: Handling Objections & Competitor Claims

Address the specific things this competitor (or their champions inside the buyer's organization) are likely to say about you.

**If they say: "[Competitor's attack or claim]"**
Respond: "[Factual rebuttal or reframe]"
Evidence: [Customer example, data point, or proof point]

**If they say: "[Another common claim]"**
Respond: "[Response]"
Evidence: [Supporting evidence]

**If the buyer asks: "[Tough question competitors like to provoke]"**
Respond: "[Honest, credible answer]"

Rules for this section:
- Never trash-talk the competitor. Buyers hate it and it erodes trust.
- Acknowledge where the competitor is strong, then redirect to what matters more.
- Use customer proof points whenever possible — third-party credibility beats self-promotion.
- If you don't have a good answer to an objection, say so and recommend how to handle it (e.g., "Acknowledge this gap and pivot to [our strength]").

### Section 6: Trap Questions to Avoid

Things the competitor wants the buyer to ask you — questions designed to highlight your weaknesses or their strengths.

**Watch out for:**
- "[Question that exposes a known gap]" — If this comes up, [how to handle it]
- "[Evaluation criteria that's rigged in their favor]" — Reframe as [alternative framing]

### Section 7: Proof Points & References

Specific evidence a rep can cite in conversation:
- **Customer wins against this competitor**: [Company A switched from Competitor to us because...]
- **Analyst or review quotes**: [Brief reference — e.g., "G2 rates us higher on ease of use (4.5 vs 3.8)"]
- **Performance data**: [Any head-to-head benchmarks or metrics]
- **Case studies**: [Links to relevant case studies featuring competitive displacement]

### Footer

```
Questions? Contact: [Product Marketing / Competitive Intel contact]
Found this useful? Found an error? Let us know: [feedback channel]
Next scheduled update: [Date]
```

## Battlecard Maintenance

Battlecards go stale fast. Establish these practices:

- **Quarterly full review**: Revisit every section with updated win/loss data and market intelligence
- **Event-triggered updates**: Major competitor product launches, pricing changes, or funding rounds should trigger an immediate update
- **Sales feedback loop**: Create a simple mechanism (Slack channel, form, regular check-in) for reps to report what's working, what's not, and what competitors are saying in the field
- **Usage tracking**: If your battlecards live in a CRM or enablement platform, track which ones get viewed. Low-usage battlecards may need to be rewritten or may indicate the competitor isn't relevant

## Delivery Format

Battlecards should live where sales teams already work:
- **CRM integration** (Salesforce, HubSpot): Surfaced when a competitor is tagged on an opportunity
- **Sales enablement platform** (Highspot, Seismic, Showpad): Searchable and versioned
- **Internal wiki / Notion / Confluence**: Acceptable for smaller teams, but harder to keep current
- **PDF / one-pager**: Good for distribution, but creates version control problems. Always include "Last Updated" date.

For this skill, when creating battlecards as documents, produce them as clean single-page markdown or Word documents that can be easily imported into whatever system the team uses.
