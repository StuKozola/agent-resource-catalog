# Competitive Matrix Guidance

The competitive matrix is the central analytical artifact in a competitive landscape analysis. It provides a structured, at-a-glance comparison of how competitors stack up against each other on the dimensions that matter to buyers.

## Choosing the Right Dimensions

This is the single most important decision in building a matrix. The wrong dimensions produce a misleading analysis.

### Good Dimensions (Market-Problem-Driven)
Dimensions should reflect what buyers actually evaluate and care about. These typically come from:

- **Win/loss analysis**: What factors do buyers cite when explaining their decision?
- **Market interviews**: What problems keep your target personas up at night?
- **Buyer evaluation criteria**: What shows up in RFPs and vendor scorecards?
- **Industry analyst frameworks**: What dimensions do analysts like Gartner or Forrester use in their evaluations?

Example of market-problem-driven dimensions for a project management tool:
- Ease of onboarding for non-technical teams
- Cross-team visibility and reporting
- Integration with existing development workflows
- Scalability for enterprise (1000+ users)
- Total cost of ownership over 3 years

### Bad Dimensions (Feature-Driven)
Avoid dimensions that simply list product features without connecting them to buyer value:

- "Has Gantt charts" (feature, not problem)
- "API availability" (capability, not buyer outcome)
- "Number of integrations" (metric without context)

If a feature matters, reframe it as the buyer problem it solves: "API availability" becomes "Ability to integrate with existing tech stack" or "Extensibility for custom workflows."

### Recommended Number of Dimensions
- **For strategic planning**: 8-12 dimensions covering product, GTM, and company factors
- **For sales enablement**: 5-8 dimensions focused on the factors that decide deals
- **For executive summary**: 3-5 high-level dimensions with a simple visual

## Scoring Approaches

### Quantitative (1-5 Scale)
Best for detailed analysis and when you have enough data to justify precision.

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 5 | Industry-leading — widely recognized as best-in-class |
| 4 | Strong — above average, a competitive strength |
| 3 | Adequate — meets market expectations, not a differentiator |
| 2 | Below average — a known gap, loses deals on this dimension |
| 1 | Significant weakness — major gap or missing capability |

Rules:
- No competitor (including your own product) should score 5 on every dimension. If they do, your scoring is biased.
- If you can't justify why a score is different from 3, leave it at 3.
- Document the evidence behind each score in a separate notes column or appendix.

### Qualitative (Strong / Moderate / Weak)
Best for early-stage analysis when data is limited, or for executive audiences who don't want to debate specific numbers.

- **Strong**: This is a competitive advantage. Buyers notice and value it.
- **Moderate**: Meets expectations. Not a differentiator in either direction.
- **Weak**: A competitive liability. Buyers notice the gap.

### Directional (with Trend Arrows)
Add a trend indicator to show whether a competitor is improving or declining on a dimension:
- ↑ Improving (recent investment, new release, positive buzz)
- → Stable (no significant change)
- ↓ Declining (losing ground, talent departure, deprioritized)

## Matrix Layout

### Standard Format

```
| Dimension        | Your Product | Competitor A | Competitor B | Competitor C |
|-----------------|-------------|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| [Dimension 1]   | 4           | 3           | 5           | 2           |
| [Dimension 2]   | 3           | 4           | 2           | 4           |
| [Dimension 3]   | 5           | 2           | 3           | 3           |
| ...             | ...         | ...         | ...         | ...         |
| OVERALL         | [weighted]  | [weighted]  | [weighted]  | [weighted]  |
```

### Weighted Scoring
Not all dimensions matter equally. If you have data on relative importance (from win/loss, surveys, or buyer interviews), apply weights:

1. Assign each dimension a weight (must sum to 100% or use relative weights)
2. Multiply each score by the weight
3. Sum for overall weighted score
4. Present both raw and weighted views — the weighted view shows competitive position from the buyer's perspective

### Visual Formats

For presentations and executive audiences, consider visual formats in addition to (not instead of) the detailed table:

**Harvey Ball Chart**: Use filled circles (●, ◕, ◑, ◔, ○) for visual scanning. Works well in presentations.

**2x2 Perceptual Map**: Plot competitors on two key dimensions. Good for showing strategic positioning at a glance. Choose axes that represent the primary tradeoffs buyers face (e.g., "Ease of Use" vs "Enterprise Capability", or "Price" vs "Functionality Depth").

**Radar/Spider Chart**: Shows multi-dimensional comparison visually. Good for 5-8 dimensions, gets cluttered beyond that.

## What to Include Beyond the Matrix

The matrix alone isn't enough. Always accompany it with:

1. **Methodology note**: How scores were determined and what sources were used. This builds credibility.
2. **Key takeaways**: 3-5 bullet points summarizing the most important competitive insights.
3. **Strategic implications**: What the matrix means for product strategy, sales approach, or positioning.
4. **Confidence indicators**: Flag where data is strong vs. where you're making educated guesses.
5. **Date and review cadence**: When was this last updated? When will it be reviewed?

## Common Mistakes

- **Too many competitors**: A matrix with 10+ competitors is unreadable. Focus on the 3-5 that matter most. Put the rest in an appendix.
- **Too many dimensions**: More than 12 dimensions creates analysis paralysis. Prioritize the ones that actually decide deals.
- **Inflated self-scores**: The fastest way to lose credibility with sales and executives is to show your product winning on every dimension. Be honest.
- **Stale data**: A matrix from 6 months ago may be dangerously wrong in a fast-moving market. Note the date prominently and flag areas of uncertainty.
- **Missing the status quo**: For many categories, the biggest competitor is "do nothing" or "use a spreadsheet." Consider including this as a column.
