# Input Audit — Awareness Programs

Before designing awareness programs, validate that the upstream strategic artifacts are in place. Awareness programs built without these inputs default to inside-out guesswork — the exact opposite of the Pragmatic outside-in philosophy.

## Critical Inputs (Must Have)

### 1. Positioning Documents
**Source:** Planning → Positioning
**What you need:** Internal positioning documents by segment and persona that describe the product by its ability to solve market problems. Each positioning document should include the target persona, their top problems, your differentiated value, and the proof points that support it.

**Why it matters for Awareness:** Positioning provides the messaging pillars that awareness content must communicate. Without positioning, awareness programs devolve into feature-dump campaigns that fail to resonate with the market.

**Red flags:**
- Positioning documents are more than 12 months old
- Positioning is organized by product rather than by persona/segment
- No clear articulation of problems solved — only capabilities listed
- Positioning has not been validated with recent market interviews (NIHITO)

### 2. Buyer Personas
**Source:** Planning → Buyer Personas
**What you need:** Archetypical buyer profiles that define who is involved in purchasing decisions — their role, responsibilities, goals, challenges, information sources, and decision criteria.

**Why it matters for Awareness:** Personas determine where you invest awareness effort, what content you create, and which channels you use. A CTO consumes information differently than a line-of-business VP. Awareness programs must be persona-specific to be effective.

**Red flags:**
- Personas are demographic-only (title + company size) without behavioral and attitudinal dimensions
- Personas are based on internal assumptions rather than market interviews
- No documentation of information sources and watering holes
- Personas have not been refreshed in the last 12 months

### 3. Market Definition
**Source:** Focus → Market Definition
**What you need:** Clearly defined market segments with problem-based segmentation, size estimates, and strategic prioritization. You should know which segments to actively pursue and which to deprioritize.

**Why it matters for Awareness:** Awareness investment must concentrate on strategic segments large enough to support the business. Spreading awareness effort across every possible segment dilutes impact.

**Red flags:**
- Segments are defined by industry vertical alone without problem-based differentiation
- No prioritization — all segments treated equally
- Segment size has not been validated against TAM/SAM/SOM analysis

### 4. Competitive Landscape
**Source:** Market → Competitive Landscape
**What you need:** Identification of competitive and alternative offerings with assessed strengths and weaknesses, plus a strategy for differentiation.

**Why it matters for Awareness:** You cannot build effective awareness without understanding what your audience already knows about alternatives. Share of voice is relative — your awareness must be measured against competitors, not in isolation.

**Red flags:**
- Competitive analysis covers direct competitors only, ignoring alternatives and the status quo
- No assessment of competitor awareness tactics and share of voice
- Analysis is older than 6 months in fast-moving markets

## Important Inputs (Should Have)

### 5. Buyer Experience Map
**Source:** Planning → Buyer Experience
**What you need:** Documentation of the buyer's journey for key segments and personas, including the awareness and education stages where prospects first encounter problems and begin exploring solutions.

**Why it matters for Awareness:** The buyer experience map tells you exactly where awareness programs must operate — what triggers a buyer to start looking, where they go first, and what information they need at the earliest stages.

### 6. Marketing Plan
**Source:** Programs → Marketing Plan
**What you need:** The strategic marketing plan that articulates strategies and tactics for the fiscal period, including budget allocations, program calendar, and measurement goals.

**Why it matters for Awareness:** Awareness programs operate within the marketing plan's strategic framework. Budget, timing, and goals should already be established at the marketing plan level.

### 7. Win/Loss Analysis
**Source:** Market → Win/Loss Analysis
**What you need:** Patterns from recent evaluator interviews about why prospects did or did not buy, and what steps they took in the buying process.

**Why it matters for Awareness:** Win/loss data reveals whether awareness is a bottleneck — are you losing deals because prospects didn't know about you, or because they knew about you but didn't understand your differentiated value?

### 8. Distinctive Competencies
**Source:** Market → Distinctive Competencies
**What you need:** Articulation of the organization's unique abilities to deliver value.

**Why it matters for Awareness:** Distinctive competencies provide the authentic proof points that make awareness content credible rather than generic.

## Audit Decision Matrix

| Input Status | Action |
|---|---|
| All critical inputs current and complete | Proceed to Step 2 |
| Positioning missing or stale | STOP — Cannot build effective awareness without positioning. Complete Positioning first or document assumptions that must be validated. |
| Personas missing | STOP — Cannot target awareness without knowing who to reach. Build at minimum a lightweight persona profile per priority segment. |
| Market Definition missing | Proceed with caution — Document assumed segments and flag for validation. Risk: awareness investment may target wrong segments. |
| Competitive Landscape missing | Proceed with caution — Baseline share of voice will be unknown. Plan to include competitive research as part of the awareness measurement framework. |
| Important inputs missing | Proceed — Note gaps and build awareness program with explicit assumptions documented for later validation. |

## Working with Incomplete Inputs

The Pragmatic philosophy is outside-in — but in practice, teams often need to begin awareness programs before every upstream artifact is complete. When working with incomplete inputs:

1. **Document every assumption explicitly** — If you don't have a validated buyer persona, write down what you assume about the persona and flag it for NIHITO validation
2. **Start narrow** — Focus awareness effort on the one segment and persona where you have the most confidence
3. **Build feedback loops** — Design early awareness programs to generate the market intelligence you're missing (e.g., survey-based content that reveals persona preferences)
4. **Set review gates** — Schedule a 90-day check to reassess awareness strategy once upstream inputs are completed
