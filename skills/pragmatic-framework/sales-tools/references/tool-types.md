## 1. Competitive Battle Cards

**Selling step**: Competitive differentiation (mid-to-late sales cycle)

**Purpose**: Equip reps to respond confidently when a prospect mentions a specific competitor. Battle cards are the most widely used sales tool in competitive selling — research suggests that over 85% of competitive intelligence leaders at software companies provide them to sellers.

**Structure**:

- **Competitor overview** — Company snapshot (size, focus, recent news) in 2-3 sentences
- **Why we win** — 3-5 key differentiators with specific evidence, not vague claims
- **Why we lose / their strengths** — Honest assessment of where this competitor is strong; reps who acknowledge competitor strengths build more trust
- **Quick dismisses** — Short, confident statements (1-2 sentences each) to redirect early-stage competitor mentions without getting into a full comparison
- **Landmine questions** — Questions reps can plant that expose competitor weaknesses without directly attacking (e.g., "Have you asked them about their approach to X?")
- **Objection handlers** — The 4-6 most common objections when competing against this rival, with specific counter-responses
- **Pricing comparison** — Side-by-side pricing model comparison highlighting where your model delivers better value
- **Proof points** — 2-3 relevant customer wins, especially switchover stories from this competitor

**Design rules**:
- One card per competitor — never combine competitors on a single card
- Keep to one page (front and back maximum) for in-call usability
- Bold key phrases so reps can scan during live conversations
- Write counter-responses in natural conversational language, not marketing copy
- Include a "last updated" date and owner name

**Common mistakes**:
- Making the card too long (more than 2 pages defeats the purpose)
- Using internal jargon the buyer wouldn't recognize
- Claiming you win everywhere (dishonest cards get ignored by experienced reps)
- Forgetting to update after competitor product launches or pricing changes

## 2. Objection Handling Guides

**Selling step**: Any stage where buyer pushback occurs (spans the full cycle)

**Purpose**: Give reps pre-crafted, field-tested responses to the most common reasons prospects hesitate, delay, or reject.

**Structure**:

- **Objection category** — Group objections by theme: price/budget, timing, competition, status quo/inertia, technical concerns, organizational/political
- **The objection as stated** — Use the buyer's actual words, not a sanitized version
- **What it really means** — The underlying concern behind the stated objection
- **Recommended response** — A conversational response framework (not a script to read verbatim)
- **Proof point** — A specific story, data point, or reference that supports the response
- **Escalation path** — When this objection signals a deeper issue that requires different handling (e.g., involving an executive sponsor or technical specialist)

**Design rules**:
- Organize by sales stage so reps can find relevant objections quickly
- Include the 3-5 most common objections per persona type
- Write responses that acknowledge the concern before redirecting
- Test responses with top-performing reps before distributing widely
- Include "if/then" branching: if the buyer responds with X, then try Y

**Common mistakes**:
- Writing responses that dismiss the objection rather than addressing it
- Creating an exhaustive list of 50+ objections instead of focusing on the critical few
- Using combative or defensive language
- Not updating when new objections emerge from lost deals

## 3. ROI Calculators and Value Tools

**Selling step**: Business case justification (mid-to-late cycle, especially for economic decision-makers)

**Purpose**: Help prospects quantify the financial impact of their current problem and the value of your solution in their own numbers. This is particularly powerful for economic decision-makers who need to justify the investment internally.

**Structure**:

- **Input section** — Fields for the prospect's own data (headcount, current costs, time spent on manual processes, error rates, etc.)
- **Calculation logic** — Transparent formulas that show how inputs translate to outcomes
- **Output section** — Projected savings, revenue gains, productivity improvements, and time-to-value
- **Comparison view** — Current state vs. future state side by side
- **Sensitivity analysis** — Show conservative, moderate, and optimistic scenarios
- **Assumptions documentation** — Clearly state what assumptions underlie the calculations

**Formats**:
- Interactive spreadsheet (Excel/Google Sheets) for collaborative use with prospects
- Web-based calculator for self-service on your website
- Slide-based version for presentations to buying committees
- One-page summary for email follow-up after a demo

**Design rules**:
- Let the prospect's own numbers drive the output — never pre-fill with inflated assumptions
- Include industry benchmarks as reference points, not as defaults
- Make assumptions visible and adjustable so the tool builds trust, not skepticism
- Include a "cost of doing nothing" calculation — what it costs the prospect to maintain the status quo
- Design for the prospect to present internally (your tool becomes their justification document)

**Common mistakes**:
- Using unrealistic default values that undermine credibility
- Hiding the calculation methodology (this triggers skepticism)
- Focusing only on cost savings when revenue impact may be more compelling
- Making the tool so complex that reps can't walk a prospect through it in 10 minutes

## 4. Demo Scripts

**Selling step**: Solution presentation and demonstration

**Purpose**: Guide reps through product demonstrations that connect features to buyer problems rather than walking through a feature tour.

**Structure**:

- **Topic blocks** — Break the demo into 8-12 independent topic blocks (not a single linear script)
- **For each block**:
  - The market problem this feature addresses
  - Suggested transition language to introduce the topic
  - Key talking points (what to say while showing)
  - Proof point or customer story to reinforce
  - Questions to ask after demonstrating (to check resonance and surface concerns)
- **Persona routing guide** — Which blocks to prioritize for each buyer persona
- **Time management guide** — How to adjust for 15-minute, 30-minute, and 60-minute demo slots

**Design rules**:
- Modular structure is essential — reps must be able to jump between topics based on the conversation
- Lead with problems, not features — start each block with the pain point, then show the solution
- Include "reading the room" cues: signs the prospect is engaged vs. losing interest
- Provide a "discovery-first" opening section with questions to ask before showing anything
- Never script word-for-word; provide talking points and key phrases instead

**Common mistakes**:
- Creating a monolithic 60-minute walkthrough with no navigation flexibility
- Focusing on how the product works instead of why it matters
- Not providing guidance on how to handle the "can you just show me X?" interrupt
- Skipping the discovery questions that should precede any demonstration

## 5. Prospecting Scripts

**Selling step**: Initial outreach and qualification

**Purpose**: Help reps open conversations with new prospects using language tailored to each buyer segment's priorities.

**Structure**:

- **Segment routing** — Help the rep identify which buyer type they're calling and jump to the right section
- **Opening statements** — 2-3 variations per segment, each under 30 seconds
- **Value hooks** — Persona-specific statements that earn the right to continue the conversation
- **Qualifying questions** — Questions that simultaneously qualify the prospect and demonstrate expertise
- **Objection responses** — Handling the 3-4 most common early objections (not interested, already have a solution, send me information, bad timing)
- **Next step language** — How to advance to a discovery meeting or demo

**Design rules**:
- Write for the ear, not the eye — these will be spoken, so use natural language
- Keep individual blocks short (the rep may be reading while talking)
- Include email/voicemail versions alongside phone scripts
- Segment by buyer type, not by industry (unless industry-specific language is critical)

## 6. Qualification Checklists

**Selling step**: Discovery and qualification

**Purpose**: Give reps a structured framework to assess whether a prospect is a viable opportunity worth pursuing.

**Structure**:

- **Must-have criteria** — Non-negotiable qualifiers (budget authority, problem fit, timeline)
- **Scoring criteria** — Weighted factors that indicate deal quality
- **Red flags** — Warning signs that predict a low probability of closing
- **Questions to surface each criterion** — Specific questions reps can ask naturally
- **Stage-gate thresholds** — Minimum qualification scores required to advance a deal

**Common frameworks to adapt**: BANT (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline), MEDDIC (Metrics, Economic Buyer, Decision Criteria, Decision Process, Identify Pain, Champion), or CHAMP (Challenges, Authority, Money, Prioritization).

## 7. Reference and Proof Point Sheets

**Selling step**: Throughout the cycle (used as supporting ammunition)

**Purpose**: Provide quick-access facts, figures, and evidence reps can deploy when they need to substantiate a claim.

**Types**:
- **Product fact sheets** — Key capabilities, specifications, and differentiators
- **Case study summaries** — One-paragraph summaries with key metrics (full case studies are marketing content, not sales tools)
- **Customer reference lists** — Pre-approved references organized by industry, use case, and company size
- **Analyst/third-party validation** — Key findings from analyst reports, awards, and independent reviews
- **Pricing quick reference** — Current pricing tiers, common discount structures, and competitive positioning

---

## 8. Pain/Discovery Sheets

**Selling step**: Discovery and needs analysis

**Purpose**: Map the specific problems each buyer persona faces, with probing questions that help reps uncover urgency and connect to your solution.

**Structure**:

- **Persona identification** — Role, typical responsibilities, and reporting structure
- **Common pain points** — Organized by business impact (revenue, cost, risk, productivity)
- **Discovery questions** — Open-ended questions that surface each pain point naturally
- **Pain chain connections** — How one person's pain connects to another stakeholder's concern
- **Solution bridges** — How each pain point maps to a specific capability (for internal rep use, not to share with the prospect)
- **Urgency indicators** — Signs that suggest the pain is acute enough to drive action

---

## 9. Proposal Templates

**Selling step**: Proposal and negotiation

**Purpose**: Provide a structured framework for presenting the solution, pricing, and business case in a consistent, professional format.

**Structure**:

- **Executive summary section** — Template for restating the prospect's situation and desired outcomes
- **Recommended solution section** — How to present the solution mapped to stated needs
- **Value/ROI section** — Where to insert outputs from the ROI calculator
- **Investment section** — Pricing presentation with options and comparison frameworks
- **Implementation/timeline section** — What happens after the signature
- **Social proof section** — Where to place relevant case studies and references
- **Terms and next steps section** — Clear calls to action

## 10. Product Strategy Guides

**Selling step**: Account strategy and planning

**Purpose**: Help reps select the right selling strategy for different account types and map product capabilities to that strategy.

**Example strategies**:
- Low price, quick entry, expand later — emphasize modularity, user-based pricing, fast time-to-value
- Enterprise-wide initiative — emphasize integration, configurability, management dashboards, and total cost of ownership
- Displacement of incumbent — emphasize switching costs (low for you, high for them), migration support, and competitive proof points

---

## 11. Sales Playbooks

**Selling step**: Cross-stage (playbooks span multiple tools and steps)

**Purpose**: Orchestrate multiple tools into a cohesive strategy for a specific scenario (new market entry, competitive displacement, upsell/cross-sell, etc.).

A playbook is not a single tool but a curated collection of tools with guidance on when and how to use each one. It typically includes: the target scenario and buyer profile, recommended selling process with stage-specific tools, messaging frameworks, common objections and responses, and success metrics.
