# Positioning Document Template

Use this template for every positioning document. One document per buyer persona. All annotations (in parentheses) are guidance — remove them from the final output.

---

## [Product Name] — Positioning for [Buyer Persona Name]

**Version:** [Date]
**Owner:** [Product Manager / Product Marketing Manager name]
**Status:** [Draft | In Review | Approved]

---

### 1. Target Buyer Persona

**Role:** [Job title or functional role, e.g., "VP of Engineering," "IT Security Manager," "CFO"]

**Context:** [1–2 sentences describing this buyer's world — their responsibilities, what success looks like for them, and the environment they operate in. Write this as if you are describing a real person you have interviewed.]

**Role in Purchase Decision:** [Specify: Decision Maker, Influencer, Evaluator, or Champion. For complex B2B sales, also note whether this is a Financial Buyer, Technical Buyer, or User/Functional Buyer.]

(Guidance: This section should feel like a mini-biography, not a demographics sheet. The goal is that anyone reading this document can immediately picture who they are talking to and what that person cares about.)

---

### 2. Problem Statement

**The Problem:**
[State the overriding market problem this buyer faces. Write it in the buyer's own words — the way they would describe it to a colleague, not the way your engineering team describes it. 2–4 sentences.]

**Impact of the Problem:**
[What happens if this problem goes unsolved? Quantify where possible — lost revenue, wasted time, compliance risk, employee turnover, security exposure. 1–3 sentences.]

(Guidance: This is the most important section. If you cannot articulate the problem in the buyer's language, you have not done enough market discovery. Go back and interview customers, recent evaluators, and prospects. Reference your Win/Loss Analysis and Market Problems research from the Pragmatic Framework.)

---

### 3. Ideal Solution

[Describe what this buyer wishes existed to solve their problem. This is NOT your product description — it's the buyer's dream state. Write it as: "The ideal solution would..." 1–2 sentences.]

(Guidance: The ideal solution is aspirational. Your product should map closely to it, but framing it from the buyer's perspective ensures you stay problem-oriented. If your product only partially addresses the ideal, be honest — positioning that overpromises destroys credibility.)

---

### 4. Primary Message (25 Words or Less)

> [Your primary message here. This is the single sentence you want the buyer to remember. Target exactly 25 words or fewer.]

**Formula:** "[Product Name] helps [Buyer Persona] [solve Problem] by [providing Unique Value]."

(Guidance: This is the most-read sentence in the document. It will appear in sales decks, website copy, email subject lines, and elevator pitches. Iterate on it multiple times. Test: read it aloud — does it sound natural? Could you say it in a conversation? If it sounds like marketing copy, rewrite it.)

**Word count:** [Show the count — must be ≤25]

---

### 5. Product Description

[Expand on the primary message in 2–3 sentences. Explain HOW the product solves the problem stated above, in terms the buyer understands. Stay focused on outcomes, not architecture.]

(Guidance: This is your "elevator paragraph." It should flow naturally from the primary message. A reader should be able to read sections 4 and 5 together and have a clear, complete picture of what the product does for them.)

---

### 6. Key Capabilities (3–5)

List 3–5 capabilities that are relevant to THIS buyer persona. Each capability must be tied to a buyer outcome.

| Capability | Buyer Outcome |
|-----------|---------------|
| [Feature/Capability name in plain language] | [What this means for the buyer — the "so what?"] |
| [Feature/Capability name in plain language] | [What this means for the buyer — the "so what?"] |
| [Feature/Capability name in plain language] | [What this means for the buyer — the "so what?"] |

(Guidance: Choose capabilities that matter to THIS persona. A technical buyer wants to hear about API integrations and security certifications. A financial buyer wants to hear about automation that reduces headcount needs. A functional buyer wants to hear about time saved in their daily workflow. Do NOT list every feature your product has.)

---

### 7. Proof Points

Provide evidence that your claims are credible. Choose the strongest 2–4 proof points relevant to this buyer type.

- **[Proof Point Type]:** [Description]

Proof point types include:
- **Customer Result:** "[Company] achieved [measurable outcome] within [timeframe]."
- **Metric:** "[X]% improvement in [metric] based on [source]."
- **Analyst/Industry Recognition:** "[Analyst firm] recognized [Product] as [recognition] in [report]."
- **Third-Party Validation:** "[Certification/compliance standard] certified."

(Guidance: Proof points are what separate credible positioning from empty promises. If you don't have proof points yet, flag this as a gap and work with customer success and sales to collect them. "No proof points" is a signal that positioning may be premature or that the product needs more market validation.)

---

### 8. Competitive Context (Optional but Recommended)

**Primary Alternative:** [What the buyer is doing today instead of using your product — this might be a competitor, a manual process, or doing nothing.]

**Your Differentiation:** [1–2 sentences on what you do that the alternative cannot. Be specific and honest.]

(Guidance: In the Pragmatic Framework, this connects to your Competitive Landscape and Distinctive Competence analysis. The strongest positioning doesn't trash competitors — it makes your unique value so clear that the buyer draws their own conclusions.)
