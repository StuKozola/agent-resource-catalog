# Measurement — Pragmatic Framework (2027)

## Where this fits in the 2027 Pragmatic Framework

- **Category:** Business — *Is this a good investment, and is it paying off?*
- **Focus-area question:** *Which indicators tell us early whether this is working?*
- **Position:** Below the bar (product- and team-focused)
- **Other focus areas in this category:** Business Plan, Pricing, Buy, Build or Partner, Business Outcomes
- **Formerly:** Measurement (pre-2027 it measured product marketing programs; in 2027 it moved to the Business category and covers product, strategy and go-to-market)
- **Related Pragmatic Institute courses:** Insight (evidence and analysis practices); Market and Launch also emphasize Measurement

## Overview

Measurement defines the indicators that show whether your **product, strategy and go-to-market work** are producing the outcomes you intended. It decides what you will track and how, so decisions rest on evidence rather than opinion. It is no longer just about marketing programs: the same discipline applies to a product strategy, a roadmap bet, a launch, a pricing change or an onboarding flow.

Two kinds of indicator matter:
- **Leading indicators** predict results. Example: incoming leads, trial starts, activation rate, pipeline created.
- **Lagging indicators** confirm results. Example: product sales, renewals, revenue, churn.

Leading indicators give you time to adjust; lagging indicators tell you whether the adjustment worked. A measurement plan needs both, paired so each lagging result has one or more leading signals that move first.

Measurement is also about **tuning**: indicators exist to trigger decisions. Tracking without acting is just reporting.

## Boundary: Measurement vs. Business Outcomes vs. Customer Value Realization

| Focus area | Question | What it owns |
|---|---|---|
| **Measurement** (Business) — this skill | Which indicators tell us early whether this is working? | *Sets the indicators*, leading and lagging, with definitions, sources, owners, thresholds and the tune process |
| **Business Outcomes** (Business) — `pragmatic-business-outcomes` | Is the product delivering the results the business expected? | *Tracks results for your organization* — revenue, profit, margin, efficiency, cost reduction, ROI — against the business plan |
| **Customer Value Realization** (Operations) — `pragmatic-customer-value-realization` | Did customers get the outcome they bought the product for? | *Asks whether customers got their outcome* — agreed success criteria and evidence |

In practice: Measurement designs the dashboard and early-warning system; Business Outcomes reports the organization's results from it; Customer Value Realization checks the customer's side. Customer Satisfaction (Go-to-Market) is a separate signal — how customers judge their experience — and is not the same as either results or value realized.

## Key Responsibilities

- Translate corporate, product and go-to-market goals into a small set of indicators
- Pair leading and lagging indicators for each goal
- Define each indicator: formula, data source, owner, cadence, target, thresholds
- Establish the tune process — what triggers a review and who decides
- Supply indicators to Business Outcomes, Launch, Growth, Retention, Adoption and others; don't duplicate their result tracking

## Required Skills

- Strategic thinking and math acumen
- Data management and analysis — gather data, synthesize it, tell a story
- Written and verbal communications; presentation abilities

## Relationships to Other Focus Areas

- **Business Plan** (Business) → Sets the expected results; Measurement defines the indicators that show early whether they're on track
- **Business Outcomes** (Business) → Uses Measurement's lagging indicators to report results; Measurement's leading indicators give advance warning
- **Pricing** (Business) → Win rate by price point, discount depth, trial-to-paid conversion
- **Product Strategy** and **Product Roadmap** (Market, Focus) → Indicators that show whether strategic bets and roadmap outcomes are landing
- **Marketing Plan** (Go-to-Market) → Measurement validates whether the plan's goals and strategies are being achieved
- **Growth** and **Retention** (Go-to-Market) → Leading signals for new revenue/users and for renewal or churn risk
- **Launch** (Go-to-Market) → Launch goals and readiness measures
- **Awareness** and **Engagement** (Go-to-Market) → Funnel indicators from problem awareness through evaluation and trial
- **Customer Satisfaction** (Go-to-Market) → Satisfaction and relationship-health signals (NPS, CSAT, references) as indicators, not definitions
- **Adoption** (Enablement) → Activation and meaningful-use indicators, often the best leading indicators of retention
- **Customer Value Realization** (Operations) → Evidence of customer outcomes; complements, doesn't replace, business indicators

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Clarify Goals and Context
Understand what the organization is trying to achieve before choosing any metric:
- Company-level goals (revenue growth, profitability, market expansion)
- Product and strategy goals (adoption, retention, market share, strategic bets)
- Go-to-market goals (pipeline, launch targets, segment entry)
- The business plan's expected results and the product's stage
- The user's scope — a product, a strategy, a launch, a go-to-market motion, or all of these
- Current measurement maturity (nothing formal → basic reporting → data-driven tuning)

**Load reference file:** Read `references/measurement-framework.md` for the goal cascade, leading/lagging pairs and metric definitions.

### Step 2 — Map Work to Outcomes
Connect each initiative (product, strategy or go-to-market) to the outcome it is expected to influence. Measure results, not effort.

**Key principle:** Every indicator should answer "How does this show we're moving toward the goal?" If it doesn't, it's a diagnostic metric (useful for explaining) or a vanity metric (discard it).

### Step 3 — Pair Leading and Lagging Indicators
For each outcome, choose the lagging indicator that confirms it and 1–2 leading indicators that predict it. Examples:

| Lagging (confirms) | Leading (predicts) |
|---|---|
| Product sales / new revenue | Incoming qualified leads, pipeline created, trial starts |
| Renewal rate / churn | Active use, activation rate, support escalations, health score |
| Expansion revenue | Usage growth, seats added, feature adoption |
| Launch revenue target | Sales readiness, pipeline in launch window, demo requests |
| Market share in a segment | Win rate, share of evaluations, awareness in that segment |

Check that each leading indicator really moves first — validate the relationship with your own historical data before relying on it.

### Step 4 — Select and Prioritize
Keep it focused: more than 10 means none are truly key.
- 3–5 outcome indicators (a mix of leading and lagging) tied to goals
- 3–5 diagnostic metrics that explain movement
- Each indicator is aligned, measurable, actionable, timely, understandable and owned
- Confirm the data can actually be collected

### Step 5 — Build the Measurement Framework
**Load template:** Read `templates/measurement-framework-template.md`.

Include: goals → indicators cascade; each indicator with type (leading/lagging), definition, formula, source, owner, cadence, target and thresholds; dashboard design; review cadence (weekly operational, monthly strategic, quarterly executive).

### Step 6 — Establish the "Tune" Process
- What thresholds trigger a review? (e.g., a leading indicator below target for two consecutive periods)
- Who investigates root causes, and who decides on changes?
- How quickly can the work be adjusted? (days for digital campaigns, quarters for roadmap bets)
- How are changes communicated, and when are the indicators themselves revisited?

### Step 7 — Present and Align
- Translate indicators into business language executives understand
- Show the line from leading indicators → lagging results → corporate goals
- Address common objections (attribution complexity, lag between action and result)
- Hand lagging results to Business Outcomes for the organization's results review

## Pragmatic Principles to Reinforce

- **Outcomes over activities:** Measure results and the signals that predict them, not effort (emails sent, features shipped, events attended)
- **Early warning is the point:** Leading indicators exist so you can act before lagging results arrive
- **Alignment is non-negotiable:** Every indicator traces to a goal; if you can't draw the line, it doesn't belong on the executive dashboard
- **Metrics tell a story:** Present what changed, why, and what you're doing about it
- **Avoid vanity metrics:** Likes, impressions and page views are diagnostics at most
- **Measure and tune:** The feedback loop is the point

## Internal Products and Platforms

For internal products, leading indicators are usually onboarding completion, activation and active use by eligible teams; lagging indicators are efficiency gains, cost reduction and ROI (reported in Business Outcomes). Pair them the same way.

## Using AI in this focus area

AI can help propose candidate indicators and leading/lagging pairs, write metric definitions and SQL, test whether a leading indicator actually predicts a lagging one in historical data, flag anomalies, and draft narrative commentary for reviews. Rules:
- Check AI output against real data and market evidence. A plausible correlation is not proof, and dashboards don't explain *why* — talk to customers and non-buyers (NIHITO still applies).
- Keep sensitive customer, deal and company data out of tools not approved for it.
- The team, not the tool, owns which indicators matter and what to change.
