## Event Briefing Document

The single most important deliverable for event support. Ensures all participants share the same strategy, messaging, and preparation.

### Structure

1. **Event Overview** — Name, dates, location, format, expected attendance, our participation level (sponsor tier, speaking slots, booth size)
2. **Strategic Objectives** — Why are we at this event? What are the 2-3 measurable outcomes we're targeting? (e.g., generate 50 qualified leads matching Persona A; secure 5 analyst meetings; gather competitive intelligence on Competitor X's new messaging)
3. **Target Personas** — Which buyer personas will be present, their priorities, and what stage of the buying journey they're likely in
4. **Messaging Framework** — Headline message, supporting pillars, proof points (cascaded from positioning document)
5. **Competitive Landscape** — Which competitors are present, their likely messaging, our differentiation points
6. **Roles and Responsibilities** — Who's doing what: booth shifts, speaking sessions, prospect meetings, intelligence gathering
7. **Lead Qualification Criteria** — How to distinguish between a badge scan and a qualified lead; what information to capture and how
8. **Counterintelligence Reminders** — What's safe to share, what's off-limits
9. **Logistics** — Schedules, meeting locations, demo equipment, booth setup requirements
10. **Post-Event Plan** — Debrief schedule, lead follow-up SLA, intelligence sharing process

### Best Practices

- Distribute 5-7 business days before the event so participants have time to prepare
- Keep to 3-5 pages — anything longer won't be read
- Include a one-page "cheat sheet" with messaging, competitive quick-hits, and lead criteria for on-floor reference
- Review with sales/marketing stakeholders before distributing to align on objectives and follow-up

### Common Mistakes

- Creating the briefing document after the event strategy has been set by others (too late to influence)
- Including product feature lists instead of market-problem-based messaging
- Omitting competitive preparation (competitors will be there; prepare for it)
- No clear definition of what constitutes a "qualified lead" versus a contact

## Demo Scripts

Demo scripts for events are fundamentally different from sales demos or product tours. They must be short, problem-led, and visual.

### Structure

A well-structured event demo script follows the Problem-Solution-Value pattern:

1. **Problem Setup (30 seconds)** — Describe a specific, recognizable market problem that the target persona faces daily. Use buyer language, not technical jargon.
2. **Solution in Context (2-3 minutes)** — Show how the product addresses the problem. Keep the demo to 3-5 key screens or workflows. Show outcomes, not configuration.
3. **Differentiated Value (30 seconds)** — Articulate what makes this solution uniquely effective. Connect to positioning — why us versus alternatives?
4. **Engagement Hook (15 seconds)** — Ask a qualifying question or offer a next step: "Does this match the challenges you're seeing?" or "Would you like to see how this works with your specific [use case]?"

Total target length: 3-5 minutes for booth demos; 15-20 minutes for theater presentations.

### Best Practices

- Script multiple demo paths for different personas — the demo for a CTO is different from the demo for an end user
- Practice the "walk-up" version — what do you show when someone stops at your booth for 60 seconds?
- Build recovery points — if the demo breaks, know exactly where to pick up the narrative
- End every demo with a question, not a feature — get the prospect talking
- Prepare "day in the life" scenarios drawn from use scenarios and market problem documentation

### Common Mistakes

- Starting with a login screen and walking through menus (boring, product-centric)
- Showing too many features instead of solving one problem compellingly
- No narrative arc — just a random feature tour
- Ignoring the environment — booth demos must work with noise, interruptions, and small screens
- Not preparing for common prospect questions that arise during demos

## Booth Messaging and Conversation Guides

### Booth Messaging Hierarchy

Booth messaging operates at three distances:

**10+ feet (Banner/Header):**
- Purpose: Stop foot traffic; spark curiosity
- Format: 5-8 words maximum; problem question or bold claim
- Example: "What if [painful process] took 5 minutes, not 5 days?"

**5 feet (Supporting Panels/Screens):**
- Purpose: Elaborate on the headline; provide context
- Format: 2-3 supporting messages with brief proof points
- Map to the three messaging pillars from the positioning document

**Conversation Distance (Talking Points):**
- Purpose: Engage in personalized, persona-appropriate dialogue
- Format: Conversation starters, qualifying questions, and response frameworks

### Conversation Guide Structure

Create a conversation guide for booth staff with:

1. **Opening lines** — 3-4 conversation starters that lead with market problems:
   - "What brings you to [event name] this year?"
   - "Are you dealing with [common market problem]?"
   - "How is your team handling [industry trend/challenge]?"

2. **Qualifying questions** — Questions that reveal persona, buying stage, and fit:
   - Role and responsibility
   - Current approach to the problem
   - Timeline and urgency
   - Decision-making process and stakeholders involved
   - Budget context (if appropriate)

3. **Messaging blocks** — Per-persona responses to "What do you do?":
   - Lead with the problem you solve for their persona
   - Support with one proof point relevant to their role
   - Offer a specific next step appropriate to their buying stage

4. **Transition to demo** — How to move from conversation to demo naturally:
   - "Let me show you how that works in practice — it takes about 3 minutes"

5. **Objection responses** — Top 5 objections and market-driven responses

6. **Close and capture** — How to end the conversation productively:
   - Qualify the lead (hot/warm/cold based on persona fit and buying stage)
   - Capture specific needs and context (not just a badge scan)
   - Set a concrete next step with a timeframe

## Presentation and Speaking Materials

### Session Abstract/Proposal

Conferences select speakers based on abstracts that promise value to attendees — not vendor pitches.

**Structure for a winning abstract:**
- **Title:** Problem-focused, intriguing, no company name (e.g., "Why 73% of [Process X] Projects Fail — And What the Successful 27% Do Differently")
- **Description:** Frame the market problem → Promise actionable insights → Hint at the methodology or framework → Include a specific, tangible takeaway
- **Speaker bio:** Emphasize market expertise and practitioner credibility, not company title

### Speaker Preparation Kit

For each speaker or panelist, provide:

1. **Messaging guardrails** — The positioning messages to reinforce and the boundaries to stay within
2. **Market data package** — Statistics, research findings, and trends to reference
3. **Customer stories** — 3-5 approved stories with problem, approach, and outcome
4. **Competitive context** — What competitors might say on the same topic; how to differentiate without disparaging
5. **Q&A preparation** — Anticipated questions with suggested responses; topics to redirect or avoid
6. **Slide guidance** — Key slides to include, visual standards, and mandatory messaging elements

### Common Mistakes

- Submitting product-pitch abstracts that get rejected
- Speaker slides that are 80% product screenshots and 20% market insight (invert this ratio)
- Not preparing for Q&A — the audience often remembers the Q&A more than the presentation
- Using company jargon instead of audience language

## Pre-Event Promotional Content

Content that drives attendance and pre-qualifies the audience before the event.

### Types

1. **Invitation emails** — Persona-targeted; lead with the problem the event will address, not the product that will be shown
2. **Social media content** — Teasers that reference market problems and promise insights; speaker spotlights with problem-focused positioning
3. **Blog posts** — Pre-event thought leadership that establishes credibility on the event's topic
4. **Meeting scheduler** — For high-value prospects, offer pre-booked meeting slots grounded in "Let's discuss [specific problem]" not "Let us demo our product"

### Best Practices

- Start promotion 4-6 weeks before in-person events; 2-3 weeks for webinars
- Segment promotional content by persona — different problems for different roles
- Include social proof (customer speakers, attendee testimonials from past events)
- Create urgency through content value, not artificial scarcity

## Quick-Reference Cards

One-page cards for booth staff and presenters to carry during the event.

### Structure

- **Front:** Headline message, 3 supporting pillars, top 3 proof points
- **Back:** Competitive quick-reference (competitor → our differentiation), top 5 qualifying questions, lead capture checklist

### Format

- Print on card stock; laminated if possible
- Pocket-sized or badge-holder-sized
- Large enough text to read in a busy booth environment

## Leave-Behind and Handout Content

Physical or digital content that extends the event conversation.

### Principles

1. **Align with the buying stage** — Awareness-stage attendees get thought leadership; evaluators get solution comparisons; deciders get ROI frameworks
2. **Problem-led, not product-led** — Lead with the market problem on the cover; solution details inside
3. **Include a clear next step** — Every leave-behind should have one specific call to action
4. **Make it memorable** — Generic data sheets get thrown away; insightful research or useful frameworks get kept
5. **Track distribution** — If possible, use QR codes or unique URLs to track which content drives follow-up engagement

### Content Types by Buying Stage

| Buying Stage | Effective Leave-Behind Content |
|-------------|-------------------------------|
| Unaware / Problem Aware | Industry research reports; market trend summaries; problem framework guides |
| Solution Aware | Solution approach guides; comparison frameworks; buyer's checklists |
| Evaluating | Technical briefs; customer case studies; ROI calculators |
| Deciding | Implementation guides; reference customer lists; business case templates |
