## Persona-Message Mapping

Every event has a mix of attendee types. Effective event support maps the right messages to the right personas at the right level.

### Step 1: Identify Personas Present

For each event, determine which buyer personas are likely to attend. Sources include:

- Event registration data and attendee demographics
- Past attendance records from previous years
- Event organizer's published audience profile
- Industry knowledge about who attends this type of event

### Step 2: Map Personas to Messages

For each persona expected at the event, document:

| Element | What to Capture |
|---------|----------------|
| **Persona name** | The archetype from your buyer persona documents |
| **Role/title patterns** | Typical titles this persona holds |
| **Primary market problem** | The #1 problem they need solved |
| **Positioning message** | The 25-word (or shorter) message from your positioning document aimed at this persona |
| **Proof points** | Specific evidence — customer stories, data, analyst validation — that supports the message |
| **Competitive differentiation** | What to say if they ask "How are you different from [competitor]?" |
| **Objection patterns** | Common pushback and market-driven responses |
| **Call to action** | Desired next step in the buying journey for this persona |

### Step 3: Prioritize by Event

Not all personas are equally important at every event. Rank personas for each specific event based on:

1. Expected attendance volume for this persona
2. Strategic priority in the marketing plan
3. Buying stage most attendees are in
4. Competitive intensity for this persona at this event

Focus the majority of event content and preparation on the top 1-2 priority personas for that event.

## Positioning Cascade for Events

Event content should cascade directly from the positioning document, not be invented from scratch.

### The Cascade

```
Positioning Document (source of truth)
  → Event Theme / Headline Message
    → Session/Presentation Titles and Abstracts
      → Demo Scripts and Booth Messaging
        → Individual Talking Points and Conversation Starters
          → Follow-up Content and Nurture Messages
```

### Rules for the Cascade

1. **Every event message must trace back to the positioning document.** If it doesn't, it either doesn't belong or the positioning document needs updating.

2. **Adapt the message to the format, not the strategy.** A 25-word positioning statement becomes a 10-word booth banner, a 90-second elevator pitch, a 30-minute presentation, or a 500-word abstract — but the core message stays consistent.

3. **Lead with the problem, not the product.** At every level of the cascade, the market problem comes before the solution. Booth banners ask the problem question. Presentations open with the problem. Demos start with a problem scenario.

4. **Use buyer language, not company language.** Draw vocabulary from market interviews, win/loss analysis, and persona documents — not from engineering specs or internal roadmap documents.

5. **Test the "So what?" at every level.** For each piece of event content, ask: "Would our target persona care about this? Would they stop walking, click this webinar link, or stay in this session?"

## Competitive Preparation Framework

When competitors are present at the same event (common at conferences and trade shows), prepare specifically for competitive encounters.

### Pre-Event Competitive Preparation

1. **Identify which competitors will be present** — Check exhibitor lists, speaker lineups, and sponsor rosters

2. **Review current competitive positioning** — What are they likely to say? What messaging have they used recently?

3. **Prepare differentiation talking points** — For each major competitor:
   - Where you win (strengths to emphasize)
   - Where they claim to win (how to reframe)
   - Where you're comparable (neutralize, don't concede)
   - Landmine questions to plant if prospects are also evaluating them

4. **Create competitive quick-reference cards** — One-page cards for booth staff with:
   - Competitor name and booth location
   - Their likely messaging
   - Your differentiation points
   - Questions to ask prospects who mention them
   - What NOT to say (avoid disparagement; focus on your strengths)

### During-Event Competitive Intelligence

- Walk competitor booths and note messaging, demos, and traffic levels
- Attend competitor sessions and capture positioning, claims, and audience reactions
- Ask prospects what they've heard from competitors and what resonated
- Document competitive intelligence in a shared, timestamped format for the team

### Post-Event Competitive Debrief

- What new messaging or positioning did competitors introduce?
- What claims did they make that we need to validate or counter?
- Which competitor attracted the most attention and why?
- Did any prospect comparisons reveal gaps in our positioning?

## Buyer Experience Stage Mapping

Event content should map to where attendees are in their buying journey.

### Stage-Appropriate Event Content

| Buying Stage | Attendee Mindset | Appropriate Event Content |
|-------------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| **Unaware** | "I don't know I have this problem" | Thought leadership presentations on market trends; educational webinars; industry research findings |
| **Problem Aware** | "I know I have this problem but haven't started looking for solutions" | Problem-focused sessions; best-practice workshops; customer panels on challenges; market data presentations |
| **Solution Aware** | "I'm exploring solution approaches" | Solution category overviews; approach comparisons; use case demonstrations; analyst sessions |
| **Evaluating** | "I'm comparing specific products" | Product demonstrations; competitive differentiation sessions; technical deep-dives; customer case studies |
| **Deciding** | "I'm close to a decision" | Executive roundtables; reference customer conversations; ROI/business case workshops; proof-of-concept discussions |

### Matching Event Type to Buying Stage

| Event Type | Primary Buying Stage Served |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| Industry conferences (keynotes) | Unaware → Problem Aware |
| Trade show booth conversations | Solution Aware → Evaluating |
| Webinars (educational) | Problem Aware → Solution Aware |
| Webinars (product-focused) | Solution Aware → Evaluating |
| Executive roundtables | Evaluating → Deciding |
| User conferences | Post-purchase (retention, expansion) |
| Workshops/seminars | Problem Aware → Solution Aware |

## Messaging Hierarchy

For each event, establish a clear messaging hierarchy so all content and conversations align.

### Three-Level Hierarchy

**Level 1: Event Headline (The "One Thing")**
A single statement that captures the core message for this event. This should be the one idea an attendee remembers after leaving your booth, session, or webinar.

- Format: Problem → Solution framing in 15 words or fewer
- Example: "Your customers expect [outcome X] — legacy approaches can't deliver it."

**Level 2: Supporting Messages (The "Three Pillars")**
Three supporting messages that elaborate on the headline. These form the structure of presentations, the sections of a booth, or the talking points in a demo.

- Each pillar addresses a specific aspect of the market problem
- Each pillar has proof points (data, customer stories, analyst quotes)
- Each pillar maps to a capability or differentiator

**Level 3: Proof Points and Stories (The Evidence)**
Specific evidence that makes the messages credible:

- Customer success stories (with names where possible, anonymized where necessary)
- Market data and research findings
- Analyst endorsements or report citations
- Product-specific differentiators with measurable outcomes
- Competitive win examples (without naming competitors publicly)

## Counterintelligence Guidelines

Events are public. Product team members must know what can and cannot be shared.

### Safe to Share

- Published product capabilities and released features
- Publicly available customer stories and case studies
- Market data from published research
- General industry perspectives and thought leadership
- Approved positioning messages and differentiators

### Handle with Care

- Product roadmap — share direction and vision, not dates or specifics
- Pricing — refer to published pricing or direct to sales for custom quotes
- Customer names — only share approved reference customers
- Win/loss patterns — discuss trends, not specific deal details
- Partnership discussions — do not disclose pending or exploratory partnerships

### Never Share at Events

- Unreleased product features or internal development timelines
- Financial details (revenue, margins, customer count — unless publicly disclosed)
- Internal strategy documents or competitive analysis details
- Specific deal details, prospect names, or pipeline information
- Negative characterizations of competitors (focus on your strengths)

### Best Practice

Brief all event participants on counterintelligence guidelines before the event. Product experts are often the most vulnerable because they're passionate about what they're building and eager to share — which makes them the most likely to inadvertently disclose sensitive information in casual conversation.
