# Channel Training — Pragmatic Framework (Enablement Category)

## Overview

**Official Pragmatic Definition:** Design and deliver training programs to help the sales channels focus on how to sell the product, not how to use it.

**Category:** Enablement (alongside Sales Alignment, Content, and Sales Tools)

**Key Insight:** The critical distinction in Channel Training is the emphasis on *selling* over *using*. Product training teaches features and functionality — Channel Training teaches the market problems your product solves, who experiences those problems, and how to guide a buyer through their purchasing decision. As Pragmatic Rule #20 states: "Product management should help sales channels, not individual salespeople."

**Why It Matters:** Sales channels — whether internal field sales, resellers, distributors, VARs, or strategic partners — are the last mile between your positioning and the market. If they revert to feature-focused pitches instead of problem-focused conversations, your entire go-to-market strategy breaks down. Channel Training ensures every selling conversation reflects your positioning and aligns with the buyer's journey.

## Prerequisites — What to Have Ready

Before building a Channel Training program, the following Pragmatic Framework activities should be substantially complete:

- **Positioning** — The foundation. Channel Training teaches reps to articulate positioning, not invent their own messaging.
- **Buyer Personas** — Training must be organized around who the channel is selling to.
- **Buyer Experience** — Understanding the buying process so training maps to how customers actually buy.
- **Sales Alignment** — The selling process must be mapped to the buying process before training can teach it.
- **Sales Tools** — Tools created for specific steps in the selling process are the artifacts reps use after training.
- **Competitive Landscape** — Reps need to know alternatives and how to win against them.

> **If these are incomplete:** Read `references/prerequisites-check.md` for guidance on what minimum inputs are needed to begin Channel Training design.

## Six-Step Workflow

### Step 1: Assess Current Channel Training State

Before designing new training, audit what exists:

- **Inventory current training** — What programs, materials, and sessions are already in place?
- **Identify the gap** — Is existing training focused on *how to use* (features, demos, specs) or *how to sell* (problems, personas, buying process)?
- **Survey the channel** — What do reps say they need? What are they struggling with in the field?
- **Review win/loss data** — Where are deals being lost? At what stage? These patterns reveal training gaps.
- **Evaluate channel types** — Direct sales, resellers, distributors, VARs, MSPs, OEMs, and strategic partners all have different training needs.

> Read `references/channel-training-guide.md` → Section: "Audit and Gap Analysis" for a detailed assessment framework.

### Step 2: Define Training Objectives and Audience Segments

Training must be designed with clear, measurable objectives tied to selling outcomes:

- **Define what "trained" means** — Reps can articulate the top 3 problems by persona, handle the top 5 objections, and navigate the buying process.
- **Segment your audience** — New partner onboarding vs. existing partner updates, technical pre-sales vs. quota-carrying reps, direct vs. indirect channels.
- **Set success metrics** — Training completion rates are insufficient. Measure time-to-first-deal, win rate changes, deal size changes, and selling behavior adoption.
- **Align with product introduction cycles** — Channel Training should be tightly coupled with launches and major releases.

> Read `references/channel-training-guide.md` → Section: "Objectives and Segmentation" for audience segmentation models and objective-setting templates.

### Step 3: Design the Curriculum Around Selling, Not Using

This is where the Pragmatic distinction is most critical. Structure the curriculum around:

**Market Problems (not features)**
- What urgent, pervasive problems does each persona face?
- Why do these problems matter to the buyer's business?
- How does our product solve these problems differently?

**Buyer Personas (not user personas)**
- Who is involved in the buying decision?
- What does each buyer care about? What are their objections?
- What language do they use to describe their problems?

**Buying Process (not product walkthroughs)**
- What steps does the buyer take to evaluate and select a solution?
- What information does the buyer need at each step?
- How should the rep support each stage of the buyer's journey?

**Competitive Differentiation (not feature comparisons)**
- What alternatives exist? How do buyers compare them?
- What are the competitive traps to avoid?
- When to fight and when to walk away?

**Positioning and Messaging (not datasheets)**
- How to open a conversation around problems
- How to articulate value in the buyer's language
- How to connect product capabilities to business outcomes

> Read `references/channel-training-guide.md` → Section: "Curriculum Design" for a complete curriculum framework with module templates.

### Step 4: Select Delivery Methods and Build Content

Choose formats based on channel type, geography, and learning preferences:

- **Live instructor-led training** — Best for initial onboarding, complex selling motions, and relationship building with key partners.
- **Virtual/webinar training** — Scalable for distributed channels and ongoing updates.
- **Self-paced e-learning** — On-demand modules for product updates, new hire onboarding, and certification paths.
- **Microlearning** — Short, focused content (2–5 minutes) for reinforcement and just-in-time learning.
- **Role-play and simulation** — Practice handling objections, discovery questions, and competitive scenarios.
- **Field ride-alongs** — Pairing channel reps with your best internal sellers for real-world coaching.
- **Certification programs** — Structured progressions that validate selling competency and motivate ongoing learning.

**Content development principles:**
- Every module should connect back to a market problem and buyer persona
- Use real customer stories and anonymized win/loss examples
- Include practice exercises, not just information delivery
- Create quick-reference job aids reps can use during actual selling conversations

> Use `templates/channel-training-plan.md` as the starting template for building your training plan.

### Step 5: Launch, Deliver, and Reinforce

Training is not a one-time event — it requires a launch strategy and ongoing reinforcement:

- **Pilot with a small group** — Test with trusted partners before full rollout. Gather feedback and iterate.
- **Align with launches** — Every product launch should include updated Channel Training aligned with the launch messaging.
- **Create a training cadence** — Quarterly updates, monthly reinforcement, and ad-hoc sessions for competitive changes or major wins.
- **Build a feedback loop** — Regularly survey channel reps on what's working and what's not. Feed insights back to Product Marketing.
- **Incentivize participation** — Certification badges, tiered partner benefits, or SPIFFs tied to training completion.
- **Enable the trainers** — If using train-the-trainer models, ensure your channel managers and partner success teams are equipped to deliver consistent messaging.

> Read `references/channel-training-guide.md` → Section: "Delivery and Reinforcement" for rollout checklists and reinforcement cadence models.

### Step 6: Measure Effectiveness and Iterate

Measure whether Channel Training is changing selling behavior and improving outcomes:

**Leading Indicators (activity-based):**
- Training completion and certification rates
- Content engagement and usage metrics
- Rep confidence scores (self-assessed readiness)
- Quality of discovery questions being asked in the field

**Lagging Indicators (outcome-based):**
- Win rate changes (overall and by channel segment)
- Time-to-first-deal for new partners
- Average deal size and sales cycle length
- Pipeline coverage ratio by channel
- Competitive win rates
- Partner-sourced revenue growth

**Qualitative Indicators:**
- Are reps leading with problems or features?
- Are reps using positioning language or making up their own?
- Is the channel asking for feature training or selling training?
- What do customers say about the sales experience?

> Read `references/channel-training-guide.md` → Section: "Measurement Framework" for a complete metrics dashboard template.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Training on features, not selling** — The #1 mistake. If your training agenda reads like a product spec, it's product training, not Channel Training.
2. **One-and-done delivery** — Training without reinforcement decays rapidly. Plan for ongoing engagement.
3. **Ignoring channel diversity** — A VAR selling into SMB has very different needs than a systems integrator selling into enterprise. Don't use one-size-fits-all.
4. **No connection to the buying process** — Training that doesn't map to how buyers actually buy produces reps who pitch instead of consult.
5. **Measuring attendance, not outcomes** — Completion certificates don't close deals. Measure behavior change and revenue impact.
6. **Product marketing abdication** — Channel Training is a product marketing responsibility, not a sales ops or L&D function. Product marketing owns the message; Channel Training delivers it.

## Key Pragmatic Principles for Channel Training

- **"Your opinion, although interesting, is irrelevant."** — Train on validated market problems, not internal assumptions about what reps need.
- **"Positioning focuses on the problems you solve."** — Every training module should anchor to a market problem, not a product capability.
- **"Product management should help sales channels, not individual salespeople."** — Design scalable programs, not one-off coaching for individual reps.
- **"An outside-in approach increases the likelihood of product success."** — Training should reflect how buyers think and buy, not how your organization is structured.
