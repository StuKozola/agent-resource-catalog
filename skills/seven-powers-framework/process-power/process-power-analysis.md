## 1. Process Advantage Mapping

Before assessing whether Process Power exists, you need a clear understanding of what the company actually does differently and what outcomes those differences produce. This mapping must be concrete and specific — vague claims like "we have a culture of excellence" are not sufficient.

### Process Advantage Categories

**Operational Process Advantages** — How the company produces and delivers:
- Manufacturing methods and production flow
- Supply chain management and logistics
- Quality control and defect prevention
- Inventory management and just-in-time practices
- Maintenance and asset utilization

**Organizational Process Advantages** — How the company organizes and decides:
- Decision-making speed and quality
- Cross-functional coordination mechanisms
- Employee empowerment and problem-solving authority
- Information flow and transparency practices
- Talent development and knowledge transfer

**Ecosystem Process Advantages** — How the company works with external partners:
- Supplier relationship management and co-development
- Channel partner integration
- Customer feedback loops and co-creation
- Standard-setting within the value chain

### Process Mapping Template

For each claimed advantage, document:

| Dimension | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Process Name** | What is the specific process or practice? |
| **Scope** | How many parts of the organization does it touch? |
| **Origin** | How and when did it develop? (Bottom-up evolution vs. top-down design) |
| **Codification** | Is it documented in manuals/SOPs, or primarily tacit? |
| **Outcome** | What measurable result does it produce? (Cost, quality, speed, etc.) |
| **Competitor Gap** | How does this compare to what competitors achieve? |
| **Development Time** | How long did it take to reach current state? |

Map 3-7 key processes. If fewer than 3 processes contribute to the advantage, the complexity barrier is likely too thin for Process Power. If the advantages are in a single domain (e.g., only manufacturing), the interdependency that creates true complexity may be absent.

## 2. Hysteresis Assessment Framework

Hysteresis is the core barrier in Process Power. This assessment determines whether the barrier is real and strong enough to constitute a Power.

### The Fundamental Question

> If a well-funded, well-managed competitor dedicated itself to replicating your process advantages starting today, how long would it take them to achieve comparable outcomes — and what would prevent them from succeeding?

If the answer is "3-5 years with sufficient investment," you do not have Process Power. If the answer is "10+ years, and even then it's uncertain," you may have Process Power.

### Hysteresis Assessment Matrix

| Factor | Score (1-5) | Evidence Required |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Time to develop** | | How many years did the current process state take to evolve? |
| **Interdependency depth** | | How many organizational domains are connected? |
| **Tacit knowledge ratio** | | What fraction of the process knowledge is undocumented? |
| **Failed replication attempts** | | Have competitors tried and failed to copy? |
| **Transfer difficulty (internal)** | | Can the company itself transfer the process to new units? |
| **Evolutionary path dependence** | | Were intermediate steps necessary that couldn't be skipped? |

### Scoring Guide

- **5 — Extreme hysteresis**: 15+ years to develop; deeply interdependent across 5+ domains; mostly tacit; multiple competitors have tried and failed; even internal transfer takes years; strong path dependence
- **4 — Strong hysteresis**: 10-15 years to develop; interdependent across 3-5 domains; primarily tacit; at least one competitor has tried and struggled; internal transfer is difficult
- **3 — Moderate hysteresis**: 5-10 years to develop; touches multiple domains with some interdependency; mix of tacit and explicit; competitors haven't seriously tried; internal transfer achievable with effort
- **2 — Weak hysteresis**: 3-5 years to develop; limited interdependency; mostly codified; competitors could likely replicate with investment; internal transfer straightforward
- **1 — No hysteresis**: < 3 years to develop; isolated process; fully documented; standard industry practice; easily taught

**Threshold**: A combined average score below 3.0 almost certainly does not constitute Process Power. Scores above 4.0 suggest genuine Process Power. The range between 3.0-4.0 requires careful judgment and additional evidence.

## 3. Complexity Scoring

Complexity is the first driver of hysteresis. It refers to the number of interconnected process improvements that must be achieved *together* — not in isolation — for the advantage to materialize.

### Complexity Dimensions

**Breadth**: How many distinct organizational functions or domains does the process advantage span?

| Breadth | Score | Description |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| Single function | 1 | Advantage is contained within one department (e.g., only manufacturing) |
| Two functions | 2 | Spans two connected functions (e.g., manufacturing + quality) |
| Cross-functional | 3 | Spans 3-4 functions with meaningful interdependencies |
| Enterprise-wide | 4 | Touches most functions including external partners |
| Full ecosystem | 5 | Extends to suppliers, customers, and partners as integral participants |

**Depth**: How many layers of interdependent improvements exist within each domain?

| Depth | Score | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| Surface practices | 1 | Simple practices that could be adopted independently |
| Integrated practices | 2 | Practices that depend on 2-3 other practices to work |
| Systemic practices | 3 | Practices deeply woven into organizational routines and incentives |
| Cultural embedding | 4 | Practices inseparable from organizational culture and values |
| Organic evolution | 5 | Practices that emerged through decades of co-evolution and cannot be separated from organizational history |

**Interconnection density**: How tightly coupled are the process elements?

| Coupling | Score | Description |
|----------|-------|-------------|
| Loosely coupled | 1 | Each process element works independently; could be adopted à la carte |
| Moderately coupled | 2 | Some dependencies, but elements could be partially adopted |
| Tightly coupled | 3 | Removing one element degrades several others |
| Deeply coupled | 4 | Elements are so interconnected that they can only be understood as a system |
| Inseparable | 5 | The system is greater than the sum of parts; individual elements cannot be meaningfully isolated |

**Overall Complexity Score** = Average of Breadth, Depth, and Interconnection Density

## 4. Opacity Scoring

Opacity is the second driver of hysteresis. It refers to the extent to which the process knowledge is tacit — embedded in organizational routines, habits, culture, and institutional memory — rather than explicit and transferable.

### Opacity Assessment

| Factor | Score (1-5) | Guiding Question |
|--------|-------------|------------------|
| **Documentation ratio** | | What fraction of the process knowledge is formally documented? (5 = almost nothing; 1 = comprehensive manuals) |
| **Explainability** | | Can practitioners articulate *why* things work, or only *that* they work? (5 = can't explain; 1 = fully articulable) |
| **Transfer test** | | When the company tries to teach the process (to new employees, new sites, new partners), how well does it transfer? (5 = poorly, takes years; 1 = transfers easily) |
| **Observation test** | | If a competitor observed the process in action (like GM at NUMMI), could they reproduce it? (5 = definitely not; 1 = yes, easily) |
| **Key person dependency** | | If key architects of the process left, would the capability persist? (5 = yes, it's distributed; 1 = no, it's concentrated in individuals) *Note: For opacity to support Process Power, knowledge should be distributed and tacit, not concentrated in key individuals* |
| **Reverse engineering resistance** | | Can the process be reverse-engineered from its outputs? (5 = not at all; 1 = outputs reveal the process) |

### Important Nuance on Key Person Dependency

Counter-intuitively, Process Power is *stronger* when key person dependency is *low*. If the process capability lives in 3-5 key individuals, a competitor could hire them away. True Process Power exists when the knowledge is so distributed across the organization, embedded in hundreds of routines and relationships, that no individual or small group carries it. Toyota's TPS persisted through countless personnel changes because it was organizational, not individual.

If key person dependency is high, the advantage may be better analyzed as **Cornered Resource** (talent) rather than Process Power.

**Overall Opacity Score** = Average of the six factors

## 5. The Replication Test

The Replication Test is the acid test for Process Power. It systematically evaluates whether a determined competitor could close the gap.

### Competitor Replication Scenario

Construct a realistic scenario in which the strongest plausible competitor attempts to replicate the process advantage:

**Step 1: Identify the strongest replication candidate**
- Which competitor has the most resources, motivation, and baseline capability?
- What is their current process maturity?

**Step 2: Map the replication path**
- What specific investments and changes would the competitor need to make?
- In what sequence would they need to occur?
- Are there prerequisite capabilities that must be built before others can begin?

**Step 3: Estimate replication timeline**

| Phase | Duration | Activities |
|-------|----------|------------|
| Recognition & commitment | 6-12 months | Competitor recognizes the gap and commits to closing it |
| Foundation building | 1-3 years | Basic practices adopted; initial training and culture change |
| Integration | 2-5 years | Practices begin to interconnect; cross-functional coordination improves |
| Embedding | 3-7 years | Practices become routine; tacit knowledge develops |
| Optimization | 5-10+ years | System-level improvements emerge from compounding incremental gains |

**Step 4: Identify showstoppers**
- Are there steps that simply cannot be compressed?
- Is there path-dependent knowledge (things that could only be learned by doing them in a specific historical sequence)?
- Is there a "NUMMI problem" — even with full transparency, would the competitor struggle?

### Replication Test Verdict

| Estimated Total Replication Time | Verdict |
|----------------------------------|---------|
| > 10 years with high uncertainty | **Strong Process Power** — Barrier is durable |
| 7-10 years with moderate uncertainty | **Probable Process Power** — Barrier exists but may not be permanent |
| 4-7 years with reasonable investment | **Weak/No Process Power** — Advantage exists but is replicable |
| < 4 years | **No Process Power** — This is operational excellence, not a Power |

## 6. Benefit Magnitude Assessment

The Benefit side of Process Power must be material — it must move the profit needle. Small process advantages, even if truly inimitable, don't constitute a meaningful Power.

### Benefit Measurement

| Metric | Measurement | Company | Best Competitor | Gap |
|--------|-------------|---------|-----------------|-----|
| Unit cost | $/unit | | | |
| Defect rate | % or ppm | | | |
| Cycle time | Hours/days | | | |
| Yield rate | % | | | |
| On-time delivery | % | | | |
| Customer satisfaction | NPS or score | | | |
| Employee productivity | Output/FTE | | | |
| Margin differential | % | | | |

### Benefit Significance Thresholds

| Margin Differential | Assessment |
|--------------------|------------|
| > 10 percentage points | **Highly significant** — Process advantage is a major profit driver |
| 5-10 percentage points | **Significant** — Meaningful impact on competitive position |
| 2-5 percentage points | **Moderate** — Contributes to advantage but isn't decisive alone |
| < 2 percentage points | **Marginal** — Unlikely to constitute meaningful Power |

Note: Margin differential should be measured against the *closest* competitor, not the industry average. Process Power is about differential advantage over specific rivals.

## 7. Durability and Risk Analysis

Process Power, once established, is generally durable — but it's not invulnerable. Assess the following threats:

### Durability Threats

**Technology disruption**
- Could new technology (automation, AI, software platforms) codify the tacit knowledge and make it transferable?
- Could a fundamentally different technology render the process advantage irrelevant?
- Rating: High / Medium / Low risk

**Industry standardization**
- Are industry bodies, consultants, or academic researchers working to codify and standardize what was previously tacit?
- Has the company's process approach become "best practice" that's now widely taught?
- Rating: High / Medium / Low risk

**Organizational dilution**
- Is the company growing rapidly through M&A, which could dilute the process culture?
- Are key process leaders retiring or departing faster than knowledge is embedded?
- Is organizational complexity increasing in ways that degrade process performance?
- Rating: High / Medium / Low risk

**Competitive leapfrogging**
- Could a competitor bypass the current process paradigm entirely with a fundamentally different approach?
- Is the process advantage tied to a technology generation that may become obsolete?
- Rating: High / Medium / Low risk

### Durability Score

| # High Risks | # Medium Risks | Assessment |
|--------------|----------------|------------|
| 0 | 0-1 | **Highly durable** — Process Power likely to persist for 10+ years |
| 0-1 | 1-2 | **Durable** — Process Power likely to persist for 5-10 years |
| 1-2 | 2-3 | **Moderately durable** — Process Power under some threat |
| 2+ | Any | **Fragile** — Process Power may be eroding |

## 8. Power Scoring Matrix

Use this scoring framework to produce an overall assessment of Process Power.

| Dimension | Weight | Score (1-10) | Weighted Score |
|-----------|--------|--------------|----------------|
| Benefit Magnitude (margin differential) | 20% | | |
| Complexity Score | 20% | | |
| Opacity Score | 20% | | |
| Replication Test (time estimate) | 20% | | |
| Durability Score | 10% | | |
| Evidence of Failed Replication | 10% | | |
| **Total** | **100%** | | |

### Interpretation

| Total Weighted Score | Assessment |
|---------------------|------------|
| 8.0–10.0 | **Dominant Process Power** — Extremely rare. This is a powerful, durable moat rooted in organizational capability that competitors cannot replicate. (Toyota-level) |
| 6.0–7.9 | **Strong Process Power** — Meaningful Power exists. The company has deeply embedded processes that will take competitors many years to approach. |
| 4.0–5.9 | **Possible Process Power** — Some evidence of inimitable process advantage, but the barrier may not be strong enough to qualify as a true Power. Warrants further investigation. |
| 2.0–3.9 | **Operational Excellence, Not Power** — The company has good processes, but they are replicable. This is a competitive advantage, not a Power. |
| < 2.0 | **No Process Power** — No meaningful process advantage, or the advantage is easily copied. Look for Power elsewhere. |

## 9. Deliverable Template

When producing a Process Power assessment, structure it as follows:

### Process Power Assessment: [Company/Product Name]

**Executive Summary** (2-3 sentences)
State whether Process Power exists, how strong it is, and the key source of the advantage.

**Business Context**
- Company/product being analyzed
- Primary competitors
- Industry and lifecycle phase (confirm Stability phase)
- Duration of process evolution

**Process Advantage Mapping**
- Key processes that produce the claimed advantage
- Scope and interdependencies
- Measured outcomes vs. competitors

**Benefit Assessment**
- Specific metrics showing superior outcomes
- Margin differential vs. closest competitor
- Significance rating

**Hysteresis Assessment**
- Complexity analysis (breadth, depth, coupling)
- Opacity analysis (tacit vs. explicit knowledge)
- Overall hysteresis score

**Replication Test**
- Identified strongest replication candidate
- Estimated replication timeline
- Key showstoppers or bottlenecks
- Evidence of failed replication attempts (if any)

**Durability and Risk Analysis**
- Technology disruption risk
- Standardization risk
- Organizational dilution risk
- Competitive leapfrogging risk
- Overall durability assessment

**Power Score**
- Completed scoring matrix
- Overall assessment rating

**Strategic Implications**
- What this means for the company's competitive strategy
- Recommendations for deepening the advantage (if it exists)
- Recommendations for alternative strategy (if Process Power is weak)
- Risks to monitor over time
