Each section gives writing rules, a quality test, and weak vs. strong examples. The worked example at the end uses a fictional company.

## 1. Before scenarios

**Rules:** One business issue per scenario. Write it as the buyer would describe it to a peer. Describe the situation, not the absence of your product.

**Test:** Would the buyer nod and say "that's us"?

- Weak: "Customers lack an AI-powered forecasting tool."
- Strong: "Sales leaders build the forecast from rep spreadsheets and gut feel, and find out about slipped deals in the last two weeks of the quarter."

## 2. Negative consequences

**Rules:** State the cost of the before scenario and the risk of doing nothing. Quantify where possible: money, time, risk, people, reputation. Include consequences for the business and for the persona personally.

**Test:** Does it create urgency to act now rather than next year?

- Weak: "This is inefficient."
- Strong: "The company has missed its forecast by more than 10% in three of the last four quarters, eroding board confidence and forcing reactive hiring freezes."

## 3. Positive business outcomes (PBOs)

**Rules:** Mirror a before scenario. Describe the business result, not the product activity. Make it measurable. Write it from the buyer's point of view.

**Test:** Remove the product name — is it still a result someone would pay for?

- Weak: "Customers get real-time pipeline dashboards."
- Strong: "Sales leaders call the quarter within 5% by week six, so hiring and spending decisions are made with confidence."

## 4. Required capabilities

**Rules:** Start with "the ability to…". Write neutrally, so the buyer can agree before you claim it. Each capability should be necessary for at least one PBO. Then rate your delivery: unique, better, or parity.

**Test:** Would a neutral analyst agree this is required to achieve the outcome?

- Weak: "Our patented SignalGraph engine."
- Strong: "The ability to detect deal risk from activity data (emails, meetings, stakeholder engagement) without relying on rep updates."

## 5. Metrics

**Rules:** Use measures the buyer already tracks or will accept. Include a baseline and a target where possible. Split leading indicators (early signs) from lagging (final results).

**Test:** Could the buyer's finance team verify it?

- Weak: "Better visibility."
- Strong: "Forecast accuracy (actual vs. week-six call), percentage of deals slipping out of quarter, time spent on forecast preparation."

## 6. Differentiators

**Rules:** Each one must be unique, better, or defensible, and relevant to a PBO. Explain how it is different, not just that it is. Never disparage competitors; let the contrast emerge from the required capabilities.

**Test:** The competitor swap — replace your company name with a competitor's. If the sentence is still true, it's not a differentiator.

- Weak: "Best-in-class, easy-to-use platform."
- Strong: "Risk signals are captured automatically from email and calendar activity, so the forecast doesn't depend on reps updating the CRM — alternatives rely on manual stage and probability inputs."

## 7. Proof points

**Rules:** Specific customer, specific result, specific timeframe. Prefer quantified, referenceable outcomes. Include third-party validation where available. Map each proof point to the differentiator and PBO it supports.

**Test:** Would a skeptical buyer find it credible, and could they verify it?

- Weak: "Customers love us."
- Strong: "A 400-rep software company improved forecast accuracy from ±18% to ±4% within two quarters (referenceable)."

## 8. Worked example (fictional)

**Company:** Northbeam, a revenue forecasting platform. **Persona:** CRO (economic buyer).

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Before scenario | The forecast is built from rep spreadsheets and judgment; slipped deals surface late in the quarter. |
| Negative consequences | Missed forecast in three of four quarters; board confidence falling; reactive hiring freezes. |
| PBO | Call the quarter within 5% by week six; make hiring and spending decisions with confidence. |
| Required capabilities | Detect deal risk from activity data; roll up a forecast without manual inputs; show what changed week over week. |
| Metrics | Forecast accuracy at week six; percentage of deals slipping; hours spent on forecast prep. |
| Differentiator | Automatic risk capture from email and calendar activity, independent of CRM hygiene. |
| Proof point | 400-rep software company: accuracy improved from ±18% to ±4% in two quarters. |

**Three essential questions (CRO):**

1. *What problems do we solve?* Forecasts built on rep judgment that miss by double digits and erode board confidence.
2. *How do we solve them differently?* We read deal risk from what's actually happening in emails and meetings, so accuracy doesn't depend on reps updating the CRM.
3. *Where have we done it before?* A 400-rep software company went from ±18% to ±4% accuracy in two quarters.
