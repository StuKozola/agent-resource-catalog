# Win/Loss Analysis — Pragmatic Framework (2027)

## Where this fits in the 2027 Pragmatic Framework

- **Category:** Market — *What problems does the market have, and where can we win?*
- **Focus-area question:** *Why did recent evaluators buy from us, or not?*
- **Position:** Below the bar (product- and team-focused)
- **Other focus areas in this category:** Market Problems, Product Strategy, Distinctive Competencies, Competitive Landscape

Win/loss analysis is how you learn why recent evaluators did or didn't buy from you. Done
well, it shows who your real competitors are, how buyers judge your pricing and features,
how they actually make decisions, and how well sales and marketing are working.

It needs a methodical approach. Three rules from the Framework anchor this skill:

1. **Run it from outside sales.** Evaluators give honest feedback to someone who didn't
   work the deal. Product management, product marketing, a research function or a third
   party should own the interviews.
2. **Interview two to four weeks after the decision.** Soon enough that details are fresh,
   late enough that the decision has settled and the buyer is willing to reflect.
3. **Look for patterns only after at least 10 deals.** Single interviews are anecdotes.
   Repeat the process across 10 or more deals before calling something a pattern.

Prospects rarely share the complete truth with the sales rep who worked the deal. A
structured, neutral interview process gets at the rest.

## The Win/Loss Analysis Lifecycle

### Phase 1: Program Design

Before conducting a single interview, establish the operational foundation. Most win/loss
programs fail not from bad questions but from poor organizational design.

**1. Secure executive sponsorship.** Win/loss findings touch sales, marketing, product,
pricing, and customer success. Without leadership backing the program and committing to
act on findings, insights gather dust. Frame the program as a continuous competitive
advantage engine, not a one-time study.

**2. Define learning objectives.** Work with stakeholders to identify 3–5 specific
questions the business needs answered. Examples:
- "Why are we losing to [Competitor X] in the mid-market segment?"
- "How does our pricing model affect purchase decisions?"
- "What role does our demo experience play in the buyer's final choice?"

Learning objectives should be specific and actionable — they guide everything from
interview question design to how you'll report findings.

**3. Establish sampling criteria.** Decide which deals to analyze:
- Equal balance of wins and losses (this is critical for statistical validity)
- Timing: interview two to four weeks after the decision. Treat 90 days as the outer limit.
- Mix of deal sizes, segments, and competitor matchups
- Include "no decision" outcomes — deals where the prospect chose to do nothing
- Don't report patterns until you have at least 10 deals. Mature programs run 10–15
  interviews per month; a starting program can batch 15–20 per quarter

**4. Assign ownership outside sales.** Designate a program owner — typically product
marketing or product management, not the sales team. This person coordinates interviews, maintains the interview guide,
routes findings, and tracks whether actions are taken. Without clear ownership, the
program will die within two quarters.

**5. Choose your interview model.** Three options, each with trade-offs:
- *Internal, product-led:* Product managers or product marketers conduct interviews.
  Lowest cost, fastest to launch. Risk: buyer may filter feedback.
- *Internal, dedicated:* A competitive intelligence or market research function runs
  the program. More rigor, but requires headcount.
- *Third-party outsourced:* An independent firm conducts interviews. Highest candor
  from buyers (they speak more freely to neutrals). Higher cost.

Regardless of model, the interviewer must NOT be someone from the sales team who
worked the deal. Sales involvement biases responses and suppresses honest feedback.

**6. Set the cadence.** Continuous monthly programs produce the freshest signal.
Quarterly batch programs are common for teams just starting. Compile findings and
present them quarterly at a minimum, with monthly flash reports on emerging themes.

### Phase 2: Preparing for Interviews

**Pre-interview internal briefing.** Before every buyer interview, debrief with the
sales team involved in the deal:
- How did the company get involved in the opportunity?
- What was the existing relationship with the prospect?
- Which products/solutions were proposed?
- Who were the key stakeholders on the buyer's side?
- Who were the competitors in the evaluation?
- What does the sales team believe drove the outcome?
- Was the outcome expected or a surprise?

This internal context helps the interviewer ask sharper follow-up questions during the
buyer conversation. It also reveals where the sales team's perception diverges from
the buyer's reality — that gap is often where the most valuable insights live.

**Scheduling the buyer interview.** Best practices:
- Aim to hold the interview two to four weeks after the decision (no later than 90 days)
- Let them know in advance what topics you'll cover
- Position it as a learning conversation, NOT a sales call
- Offer a small incentive ($25–$100 gift card) to encourage participation
- Plan for 20–30 minutes; rarely go beyond 45
- Record the conversation (with permission) so the interviewer can focus on listening
- Use video conferencing or phone; avoid email-only surveys (they miss nuance)

For the complete question bank and sequencing guidance, read
`https://raw.githubusercontent.com/StuKozola/agent-resource-catalog/main/skills/pragmatic-framework/pragmatic-win-loss-analysis/references/interview-guide.md`.

### Phase 3: Conducting the Interview

The interview follows a funnel: start broad and open-ended, then narrow as rapport builds.
Opening (purpose: learning, not selling; recording permission) → buying trigger and
context → evaluation process → competitive comparison → sales and marketing experience →
decision drivers → close. Plan 20–30 minutes.

Two habits matter most. When a buyer cites "price," dig deeper: it usually means the rest
of the value proposition didn't justify the difference. And ladder every decision driver
3–4 levels deep to get past surface explanations. Timings, question bank and laddering
technique are in `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/StuKozola/agent-resource-catalog/main/skills/pragmatic-framework/pragmatic-win-loss-analysis/references/interview-guide.md`.

### Phase 4: Analysis & Reporting

After collecting interviews, the work shifts to pattern recognition and actionable
synthesis. For the complete analysis framework, metrics formulas, coding taxonomy,
and report templates, read `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/StuKozola/agent-resource-catalog/main/skills/pragmatic-framework/pragmatic-win-loss-analysis/references/analysis-and-reporting.md`.

Key principles:
- Code each interview using a consistent taxonomy (product, pricing, sales experience,
  competitive positioning, brand/reputation, implementation, support)
- Look for patterns across interviews, not just individual stories — a collection of
  conversations is more meaningful than any single anecdote
- Calculate quantitative metrics: win rate, win/loss ratio, loss rate by reason
- Cross-reference buyer perceptions with the sales team's internal assessment to
  identify perception gaps
- Hold pattern claims until you have at least 10 deals; label smaller samples as early signals
- Route findings to specific owners: product issues → product team, messaging gaps →
  marketing, sales process problems → sales leadership, pricing concerns → pricing team,
  unaddressed problems → Market Problems (see below)

---

### Phase 5: Action & Continuous Improvement

The program only creates value when findings drive change.

**Route insights to owners.** Every finding should have a named owner and a proposed
action. Don't dump a 40-page report on the organization and hope people act.

**Track actions.** Maintain a simple tracker: finding → owner → action → status → impact.
Review it quarterly.

**Close the loop.** Report back to the organization on what changed as a result of
win/loss findings. This builds credibility for the program and sustains executive support.

**Feed the loop.** Win/loss is one of the standing inputs to Market Problems. Problems
that decided deals, and problems buyers had that you didn't address, go into the market
problems inventory as leads for discovery and validation (`pragmatic-market-problems`).
Competitor findings go to Competitive Landscape (`pragmatic-competitive-landscape`), and
what buyers say sets you apart goes to Distinctive Competencies
(`pragmatic-distinctive-competencies`).

**Evolve the program.** Update your interview guide quarterly based on what you're
learning. As business priorities shift, update your learning objectives. Expand
sampling to new segments or geographies. Increase interview volume as the program
matures.

## Common Pitfalls to Flag

When working with the user, proactively flag these common mistakes:

- **Interviewing only wins.** Skews findings and creates a false sense of confidence.
  Always balance wins and losses equally.
- **Letting sales lead interviews.** Buyers filter their feedback when speaking to
  someone they have a commercial relationship with. Run the program from outside sales.
- **Calling patterns too early.** Three losses to the same competitor is a hunch, not a
  pattern. Wait for at least 10 deals.
- **Accepting "price" at face value.** Price is usually a proxy. Dig into what the
  buyer actually means — was the value proposition insufficient? Was a competitor
  offering more for less? Was budget genuinely constrained?
- **Treating it as a one-time study.** Win/loss is a continuous process, like a
  wellness program. One-time snapshots lose their value quickly as markets shift.
- **Not acting on findings.** The fastest way to kill a win/loss program is to produce
  reports that no one reads and no one acts on. Route findings to specific people with
  clear asks.
- **Waiting too long to interview.** Aim for two to four weeks after the decision. After
  90 days, buyer memories degrade significantly, and the emotional texture of the
  decision, where the real insights live, fades fastest.
- **Using surveys instead of interviews.** Surveys capture what buyers think they should
  say. Interviews, especially with skilled follow-up questions, capture what actually
  happened. Surveys can supplement interviews for quantitative trending, but they should
  never replace them.

## Using AI in this focus area

- Transcribe recorded interviews and apply your coding taxonomy consistently across the batch.
- Compare buyer interviews with sales debriefs to surface perception gaps.
- Draft quarterly readouts and per-audience summaries from coded findings.
- Rules: check AI-coded themes against the transcripts and real market evidence; AI can't
  replace the evaluator interview itself, and NIHITO still applies. Keep buyer names, deal
  values and CRM data out of tools not approved for them. The team, not the tool, decides
  what the findings mean and who acts on them.
- Watch for AI inflating a handful of interviews into confident "patterns"; the 10-deal
  rule still applies.
