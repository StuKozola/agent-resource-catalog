# Pragmatic Framework: Market Problems

## Overview

Market Problems is a foundational activity in the Pragmatic Framework (formerly the Pragmatic Marketing Framework) developed by Pragmatic Institute. It sits in the **Market** category of the framework's 37 boxes and represents one of the most critical activities a product team can perform.

The core mandate: **Discover problems in the market by interviewing customers, recent evaluators, and untapped potential customers. Validate urgent problems to show their pervasiveness in the market.**

This is the "outside-in" approach that Pragmatic Institute champions — the belief that product decisions should be driven by market evidence, not internal opinions. As Pragmatic Rule #6 states: "Your opinion, although interesting, is irrelevant." And Rule #7: "Build solutions for problems that are urgent, pervasive, and that the market will pay to solve."

## Why Market Problems Matter

Market Problems is the starting point for nearly everything downstream in the Pragmatic Framework. Problems you uncover here feed directly into:

- **Positioning** — describing the product by the problems it solves
- **Requirements** — market problems drive requirements that work
- **Use Scenarios** — illustrating problems as stories that provide context
- **Business Plan** — quantifying the opportunity
- **Pricing** — reflecting the value of making the problem go away, not what it cost to build
- **Innovation** — focusing creative energy on solving real market problems

A poorly defined market problem cascades through the entire framework. The individual factors don't add — they multiply. A weak understanding of market problems results in weak positioning, weak requirements, and ultimately a product that doesn't sell.

## The NIHITO Principle

NIHITO stands for **"Nothing Important Happens In The Office."** It is the foundational philosophy behind Market Problems. The core idea: product teams must get out of the building and talk directly to the market — regularly, systematically, and with discipline.

NIHITO interviews are the primary discovery tool for Market Problems. They are qualitative, open-ended conversations designed to uncover:

- What problems customers face in the context of your product's domain
- Why those problems matter (urgency, cost, impact)
- How customers currently cope with or work around those problems
- What the buying process looks like
- What trends are shaping the market

NIHITO interviews are excellent for **discovery** — understanding the "why" behind behaviors. However, they are not sufficient for **validation**. You cannot talk to enough people in interviews alone to reach statistical relevance. Validation requires quantitative methods (surveys, usage data, sales data) layered on top of qualitative discovery.

## The Market Problems Workflow

The complete Market Problems activity follows this sequence:

### Phase 1: Plan the Research
Before scheduling a single interview, establish a clear, specific research objective. Avoid overly broad goals like "understand the market." Instead, frame a focused hypothesis or question.

Read `references/research-planning.md` for detailed guidance on:
- Setting research objectives
- Identifying interview targets (customers, recent evaluators, non-customers)
- Securing operational buy-in from sales, development, and leadership
- Building a research timeline

### Phase 2: Conduct NIHITO Interviews
Interviews are the heart of Market Problems. They should be qualitative, open-ended, and focused on listening rather than pitching.

Read `references/nihito-interviews.md` for detailed guidance on:
- Structuring interviews (open-ended + draw-out questions)
- Who to interview and how to recruit them
- Interview best practices (dos and don'ts)
- Recording, documenting, and sharing findings
- Sample question library

### Phase 3: Synthesize and Analyze Findings
After conducting interviews, identify patterns across conversations. Individual interviews are data points; patterns across interviews are insights.

Read `references/synthesis-analysis.md` for detailed guidance on:
- Identifying recurring themes and patterns
- Categorizing problems by domain, persona, and severity
- Distinguishing symptoms from root causes
- Building a problem inventory

### Phase 4: Validate Problems
Discovery tells you what problems exist. Validation confirms whether those problems are worth solving at scale.

Read `references/problem-validation.md` for detailed guidance on:
- The three validation criteria: Urgency, Pervasiveness, Willingness to Pay
- Quantitative validation methods (surveys, data analysis)
- The Satisfaction vs. Importance matrix
- When a problem is "appropriately served" vs. underserved

### Phase 5: Document and Communicate
Package findings into deliverables that the organization can act on.

Read `templates/` directory for:
- `market-problem-statement.md` — Template for a single problem statement
- `market-problems-report.md` — Template for a comprehensive Market Problems report
- `interview-guide.md` — Template for structuring NIHITO interview sessions

## Key Concepts Quick Reference

**Three Filters for Problem Worth Solving:**
1. **Urgent** — The problem causes real pain, right now. Customers feel the cost of not solving it.
2. **Pervasive** — The problem affects a large enough group to support a business. It's not limited to one or two vocal customers.
3. **Willingness to Pay** — The problem is painful enough that customers will spend money to make it go away.

A problem should satisfy at least two of three filters. If it's urgent and pervasive but no one will pay, consider alternative monetization. If it's urgent and they'll pay but it's not pervasive, you may be looking at a luxury niche.

**Market Problem vs. Feature Request:**
A market problem is a pain point expressed in the customer's language. A feature request is a customer's proposed solution. Always dig beneath feature requests to find the underlying problem — often the customer's proposed solution isn't the best one, but their problem is real.

**Interview Target Types:**
- **Current customers** — understand how they use your product, what frustrations remain, where they see gaps
- **Recent evaluators (wins)** — understand why they chose you, what problems drove their purchase
- **Recent evaluators (losses)** — understand why they chose a competitor or decided not to buy
- **Non-customers / untapped prospects** — understand problems in segments you haven't reached yet

## Skills Required for Market Problems

From the Pragmatic Framework roles documentation, the Market Problems activity requires:
- Written and verbal communications
- Facilitating productive interviews, focus groups, and meetings
- Presentations
- Primary research
- Analytical thinking
- Data synthesis

## Relationship to Other Framework Activities

Market Problems directly feeds:
- **Win/Loss Analysis** — understanding why recent evaluators bought or didn't buy
- **Competitive Landscape** — problems your competitors solve (or don't)
- **Positioning** — messaging built on problems you solve
- **Requirements** — problems translated into product capabilities
- **Use Scenarios** — problems illustrated in context as stories
- **Business Plan** — quantified opportunity based on validated problems
- **Market Definition** — segments defined by shared problems

## How to Use This Skill

When a user asks for help with Market Problems, determine where they are in the workflow:

1. **Planning stage** → Help them define research objectives, identify interview targets, and build a plan. Load `references/research-planning.md`.
2. **Building interview guides** → Help create question sets tailored to their domain. Load `references/nihito-interviews.md` and `templates/interview-guide.md`.
3. **Analyzing interview data** → Help identify patterns, categorize problems, separate symptoms from causes. Load `references/synthesis-analysis.md`.
4. **Validating problems** → Help design validation surveys, apply the urgency/pervasiveness/willingness-to-pay filters. Load `references/problem-validation.md`.
5. **Creating deliverables** → Help write problem statements, reports, or presentations. Load relevant templates from `templates/`.
6. **General education** → Explain the Market Problems concept, NIHITO philosophy, or how it connects to the broader Pragmatic Framework. Use the SKILL.md content directly.

Always orient deliverables around the customer's language and perspective, not internal jargon. The outside-in approach means that everything produced should reflect what was heard in the market, supported by evidence.
