# Switching Costs — 7 Powers Framework

## What Switching Costs Is

Hamilton Helmer defines Switching Costs as:

> **The value loss expected by a customer that would be incurred from switching to an alternate supplier for additional purchases.**

In the 7 Powers framework, Switching Costs is the Power where customers who have adopted a product face meaningful penalties — financial, procedural, or relational — if they attempt to move to a competitor's offering. It is one of three Powers (along with Scale Economies and Network Economies) that typically originates during the **Takeoff phase** of a business — the period of rapid growth after product-market fit.

Switching Costs arise when a consumer values compatibility across multiple purchases from a specific firm over time. These purchases can be repeat purchases of the same product, upgrades, or purchases of complementary goods and add-on services.

Critically, Switching Costs Power has two properties that distinguish it from most other Powers:

1. **It only benefits the holder with existing customers.** You hold no advantage over competitors when pursuing new customers. The benefit accrues exclusively in selling follow-on products to your current installed base.
2. **It is non-exclusive.** Unlike Scale Economies or Network Economies where typically one leader benefits most, Switching Costs Power can be enjoyed by all players simultaneously — every firm benefits from its own installed base of locked-in customers.

For Switching Costs to constitute a true Power, two conditions must hold:

1. **Benefit**: The firm can charge higher prices (or sell more products) to its existing customers than competitors could, because customers face a net value loss from switching.
2. **Barrier**: Competitors must compensate customers for the switching costs to win them away, making the economics of customer acquisition unattractive. The challenger faces a structurally worse cost/benefit ratio for share gains.

## Two Critical Preconditions

Switching Costs Power generates no value without both of these preconditions:

### 1. Follow-On Revenue Must Exist
If there are no additional sales to the customer after the initial purchase, Switching Costs are irrelevant — there is nothing to monetize. The Power lies in the stream of future revenue from maintenance, upgrades, add-ons, expansions, and complementary products. This is why SAP and Oracle aggressively acquire adjacent software products: each acquisition extends the revenue surface over which their switching costs operate.

### 2. The Value Must Be Captured Before Pricing Arbitrage
The major value contribution comes from acquiring customers before the market fully prices in the long-term switching cost premium. If the market already expects and discounts the lock-in premium into acquisition pricing (e.g., customers demand deep discounts on the initial sale knowing they'll be locked in), the economic benefit of Switching Costs is reduced. The strategic imperative is to build the installed base while the switching cost advantage is not yet fully reflected in competitive pricing.

## Three Types of Switching Costs

Helmer identifies three categories. Most businesses with strong Switching Costs Power exhibit more than one simultaneously, and they compound each other.

### 1. Financial Switching Costs
These are the transparently monetary costs of switching. They include the purchase price of a replacement product, data migration costs, contract termination penalties, sunk investment in complementary applications, and the direct financial impact of downtime during transition.

**Example**: An enterprise running SAP's ERP system faces the cost of purchasing a new database, recreating customizations, buying replacement applications from SAP's ecosystem, and absorbing the financial impact of disruption — potentially hundreds of millions of dollars.

### 2. Procedural Switching Costs
These stem from the loss of familiarity with the current product and the risk and uncertainty associated with adopting a new one. When employees across an organization have invested significant time learning a system, retraining them introduces cost, productivity loss, and the risk of errors. Procedural switching costs are often underestimated because they are less visible than financial costs, but they can be equally or more powerful — they create organizational resistance to change even when the financial case for switching is neutral.

**Example**: When employees in HR, sales, procurement, accounting, and management have all learned to create reports and workflows in SAP, a system switch forces changes to daily routines across the entire organization. These procedural changes open the door for errors, which in database-dependent systems can be catastrophic. Even when a competitor provides migration tools, the transition is costly and imperfect.

### 3. Relational Switching Costs
These arise from the breaking of emotional bonds built through use of the product and interactions with other users and service providers. Customers often establish close, beneficial relationships with a provider's sales and service teams. Familiarity, ease of communication, and mutual trust create resistance to severing those ties. Community membership, identity association, and social connections built around a product all contribute.

**Example**: Enterprise customers develop deep relationships with their vendor's account managers, implementation consultants, and support teams. These relationships represent accumulated context, trust, and communication efficiency that would be lost in a switch. In consumer contexts, community membership (Nike Running Club, Peloton communities) creates relational bonds that discourage switching.

## The Surplus Leader Margin for Switching Costs

The intensity formula for Switching Costs is simpler than for Scale Economies:

```
SLM = Δ (switching cost per unit)
```

Where Δ represents the aggregate switching cost the customer faces per unit of ongoing purchase. The advantage accruing to the incumbent is simply the total switching cost multiplied across the locked-in customer base.

Unlike Scale Economies, where both fixed cost intensity and the scale gap must be large, Switching Costs intensity depends on:

- **Industry Economics (Δ)**: How large are the switching costs inherent to this type of product/service? This is the same for all players.
- **Competitive Position**: Binary — you either have the customer, or you don't. There is no "partial" switching cost advantage.

The non-exclusive nature means every competitor with an installed base benefits equally from the industry-level switching costs. The competitive battle shifts to acquiring new customers (where switching costs provide no advantage) and expanding revenue within the existing base (where switching costs are decisive).

## How to Use This Skill

When a user asks for help analyzing Switching Costs, follow this process:

### Step 1: Identify the Business and Customer Relationship
Understand the product, who the customers are, and the nature of the ongoing relationship. Switching Costs analysis requires clarity on the customer lifecycle and what "follow-on" purchases look like.

Questions to explore:
- What is the product or service being analyzed?
- Who are the customers (enterprise, SMB, consumer)?
- What does the ongoing revenue relationship look like — subscriptions, maintenance, add-ons, upgrades, complementary purchases?
- What phase is the business in — origination, takeoff, or stability?

### Step 2: Map the Three Types of Switching Costs
Assess which of the three switching cost categories are present and how intense each one is. For detailed frameworks and scoring templates, read `references/switching-costs-analysis.md`.

### Step 3: Evaluate the Preconditions
Confirm that follow-on revenue exists and assess whether the switching cost premium has been priced into the market. If either precondition fails, Switching Costs Power may be weak or absent.

### Step 4: Estimate the Switching Cost Magnitude
Quantify or estimate the total cost a customer would bear to switch. Even rough estimates help — if the switching cost is clearly large relative to the annual value of the relationship, the Power is likely strong.

For worked examples and estimation frameworks, read `references/switching-costs-analysis.md`.

### Step 5: Assess Durability and Threats
Switching Costs can be swept away by technology shifts, standard-setting, or aggressive competitor migration programs. Evaluate the threats to current switching cost levels.

### Step 6: Test for Common Pitfalls
Apply the pitfall checklist below to ensure you're not mistaking a fragile advantage for a durable Power.

### Step 7: Deliver the Assessment
Produce a clear, structured analysis. For output templates, read `references/switching-costs-analysis.md`.

## Common Pitfalls

**Confusing dissatisfaction-driven retention with Power.** Customers who stay because switching is painful — not because the product is good — represent a vulnerability, not strength. Competitors will specifically target this gap by offering migration assistance, and customers who feel trapped will switch at the first viable opportunity. SAP's paradox of high retention and low satisfaction illustrates this tension.

**Ignoring the follow-on requirement.** Switching Costs produce zero economic value if there are no additional purchases. A one-time purchase business (e.g., a wedding photographer) may have high theoretical switching costs but no way to monetize them. The Power lives in the stream of future transactions.

**Assuming new customers are affected.** Switching Costs provide no advantage in acquiring net-new customers. Competition for new customers can be fierce precisely because all players recognize the lifetime value of a locked-in customer. This often leads to aggressive, even unprofitable, customer acquisition — which can destroy value if the expected switching cost revenue doesn't materialize.

**Overestimating durability against technology shifts.** Technology disruptions can collapse switching costs overnight. Cloud computing reduced the switching costs for many on-premise enterprise software categories. API standardization, open data formats, and interoperability mandates can all erode switching costs that seemed permanent. Always ask: what technology shift could make switching easy?

**Conflating switching costs with product superiority.** Customers who stay because your product is genuinely better don't represent Switching Costs Power — they represent good execution. Switching Costs Power specifically refers to customers who would face a net value loss from switching even if the competitor's product were equivalent or somewhat better.

**Forgetting the win-lose dynamic with customers.** As Helmer notes, Switching Costs create a win-lose situation with customers. Customers who feel exploited by lock-in will actively seek alternatives, demand regulatory intervention, or choose competitors for new purchases. The management challenge is extracting value from switching costs without triggering a backlash that erodes the installed base.

**Neglecting competitor migration strategies.** Competitors facing a switching cost barrier will invest specifically in reducing it. "Translator" tools, migration services, data portability features, and compatibility layers are all competitive responses designed to lower the switching cost barrier. A strong Switching Costs position requires ongoing investment in maintaining the barrier, not just initial creation.

## Relationship to Other Powers

Switching Costs interacts with the other Powers in important ways:

- **Scale Economies** — Switching Costs can reinforce Scale Economies by protecting the customer base that generates the scale advantage. Together, they create a compound moat: customers can't leave (switching costs) and the scale leader has lower costs.
- **Network Economies** — Switching Costs and Network Economies frequently co-exist (e.g., Slack, LinkedIn). Connecting users and building complementary goods via switching costs may generate Network Effects. However, they are distinct: Network Economies increase value for all users as the network grows; Switching Costs increase the cost of leaving.
- **Counter-Positioning** — Challengers using Counter-Positioning can sometimes overcome switching costs by offering a fundamentally different model that reframes the switching decision (Salesforce vs. SAP's on-premise model).
- **Branding** — Switching Costs can feed into Branding when marquee locked-in customers influence potential customers' purchasing decisions. Having prestigious customers who are publicly committed to your product strengthens brand perception.
- **Cornered Resource** — Proprietary data, unique integrations, or exclusive content accumulated through an installed base can become a Cornered Resource that reinforces switching costs.
- **Process Power** — Superior customer success processes can amplify switching costs by deepening product embedding and relationship quality.

## When Switching Costs Is NOT the Right Power

Guide the user toward a different Power analysis if:
- The advantage comes from lower costs due to size → **Scale Economies**
- The advantage is demand-side (more users make the product more valuable for all users) → **Network Economies**
- The advantage comes from a proprietary asset that can't be replicated → **Cornered Resource**
- The advantage comes from brand affinity that goes beyond lock-in → **Branding**
- Customers stay because the product is genuinely better, not because leaving is costly → Not a Power issue; it's operational excellence
