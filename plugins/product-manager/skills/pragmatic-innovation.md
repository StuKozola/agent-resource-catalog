# Pragmatic Framework: Innovation Activity

## What This Skill Does

This skill helps product teams execute the **Innovation** activity from the Pragmatic Institute's Framework. Innovation sits in the **Business** category of the framework, alongside Business Plan, Pricing, Buy/Build/Partner, and Product Profitability.

The official definition:

> **Innovation**: Focus your teams' creative spirit on solving market problems by leveraging your organization's distinctive competencies.

This is not about brainstorming in a vacuum. Pragmatic innovation is *market-driven* — it channels creativity toward problems that are urgent, pervasive, and that the market will pay to solve, using what your organization is uniquely good at.

## Core Philosophy

The Pragmatic approach to innovation rests on a few principles that distinguish it from generic "ideation" or "design thinking" exercises:

1. **Market problems are the starting point.** Every innovation effort should trace back to a validated market problem. If you can't name the problem and who has it, you're not ready to innovate — you're guessing.

2. **Distinctive competencies are the constraint.** Innovation should leverage what your organization does uniquely well (your PRIME attributes: Purpose, Reputation, Innovation history, Methods, Expertise). This prevents chasing shiny ideas your org can't actually deliver on.

3. **Urgency and pervasiveness matter.** Not all problems are worth solving. Pragmatic innovation prioritizes problems that are urgent (people feel the pain now), pervasive (many people in the target segment share it), and that the market will pay to have solved.

4. **Innovation ≠ invention.** A valuable innovation delivers an improved customer experience through a better way of doing things. It might be a new technology, a new delivery model, new pricing, new packaging, a new methodology, or a new service. It doesn't have to be revolutionary to be successful.

5. **Outside-in, not inside-out.** The answer is not in the building. Smart R&D teams left alone will come up with clever innovations, but there's no guarantee customers will buy them. The Pragmatic approach insists on external validation.

## Prerequisites from Other Framework Activities

Innovation doesn't happen in isolation. Before running innovation work, you need outputs from these upstream activities:

- **Market Problems** — Validated, prioritized list of urgent and pervasive market problems discovered through customer interviews
- **Distinctive Competencies** — Articulated unique abilities your org has (using the PRIME method: Purpose, Reputation, Innovation, Methods, Expertise)
- **Competitive Landscape** — Understanding of what alternatives exist and where competitors are weak
- **Asset Assessment** — Inventory of technical assets, skills, services, patents, and other leverage points
- **Win/Loss Analysis** — Patterns in why evaluators bought or didn't buy

If these inputs are missing or incomplete, the first step is to go gather them — not to start generating ideas.

## How to Use This Skill

When helping someone with the Innovation activity, follow this sequence:

### Step 1: Assess Readiness

Before diving into innovation work, check whether the prerequisites are in place. Ask about:
- What validated market problems are you working from?
- What are your organization's distinctive competencies?
- What does your competitive landscape look like?
- What assets can you leverage?

If the user doesn't have clear answers, help them think through these first. Read `references/readiness-checklist.md` for a structured assessment.

### Step 2: Choose the Right Deliverable

Based on the user's situation, determine which output they need. Read the appropriate template:

- **Innovation Brief** — `templates/innovation-brief.md` — A structured document that connects a specific market problem to a proposed innovation approach, grounded in distinctive competencies. This is the core deliverable of the Innovation activity.

- **Innovation Portfolio Assessment** — `templates/innovation-portfolio.md` — For teams managing multiple innovation initiatives, a framework for categorizing and prioritizing them.

- **Innovation Workshop Plan** — `templates/workshop-plan.md` — A facilitation guide for running a team session that generates market-driven innovation ideas.

- **Innovation Opportunity Scorecard** — `templates/opportunity-scorecard.md` — A scoring rubric for evaluating whether a potential innovation is worth pursuing.

### Step 3: Guide the Work

Walk the user through creating their deliverable. The key is always maintaining the connection between:

**Market Problem → Distinctive Competency → Innovation Approach → Expected Value**

Never let the conversation drift into pure brainstorming disconnected from validated market needs. If someone says "we should build X because it would be cool," redirect to "what market problem does X solve, and why are we uniquely positioned to solve it?"

### Step 4: Connect Downstream

Innovation outputs feed into several downstream Framework activities:
- **Product Roadmap** — Innovations that pass evaluation get placed on the roadmap
- **Business Plan** — Significant innovations need financial modeling and risk quantification
- **Requirements** — Approved innovations translate into prioritized persona problems for development
- **Positioning** — Innovations should map to the product's ability to solve market problems

## Roles and Responsibilities

The Innovation activity typically involves:

**Primary Owner**: Senior Product Manager or Product Line Manager

**Required Skills**:
- Analytical thinking — gathering data, synthesizing it, and telling a story
- Strategic thinking — connecting market needs to organizational capabilities
- Technical acumen — understanding what's feasible and what's not
- Communication skills — articulating innovation rationale to stakeholders
- Leadership and influence — getting teams aligned without necessarily having authority

**Key Collaborators**: Engineering/R&D leads, Design, Sales (for market signal), Executive sponsors

## Types of Innovation

When working on innovation, help the user understand which type they're pursuing:

- **Incremental**: Small improvements to existing products (new features, UX refinements, performance gains). Lower risk, faster to deliver, maintains competitive parity.

- **Adjacent**: Extending existing capabilities into new markets or applying new approaches to existing markets. Moderate risk, leverages existing strengths in new ways.

- **Transformational**: Creating entirely new products, technologies, or business models. Highest risk and reward, requires strong executive sponsorship.

A healthy innovation portfolio usually contains work across all three types, weighted toward incremental and adjacent.

## Common Anti-Patterns

Watch for these mistakes and help the user avoid them:

1. **Technology-push innovation** — "We built this cool thing, now let's find a market for it." Redirect to market problems first.

2. **Single-customer innovation** — Building something because one loud customer asked for it, without validating pervasiveness.

3. **Competitive reaction** — Copying a competitor's feature without understanding if your market actually needs it.

4. **Innovation theater** — Running hackathons and ideation sessions that produce exciting ideas but no shipped products. Make sure there's a path from idea to roadmap.

5. **Ignoring distinctive competencies** — Pursuing innovations that require capabilities your org doesn't have and can't realistically develop.

6. **Skipping validation** — Going from idea to build without checking whether real customers would pay for the solution.
