# Market Problems — Pragmatic Framework (2027)

## Where this fits in the 2027 Pragmatic Framework

- **Category:** Market — *What problems does the market have, and where can we win?*
- **Focus-area question:** *What problem does the market have, and how do we know?*
- **Position:** Above the bar (market-focused)
- **Other focus areas in this category:** Product Strategy, Distinctive Competencies, Win/Loss Analysis, Competitive Landscape

## Overview

Market problems are the pains, needs and desired outcomes that lead people to buy or use a product. They can be observed and measured, qualitatively or quantitatively. Finding them is the foundation for the rest of the Framework, and the Foundations and Design courses both emphasize it.

Data and personas tell you *who* your potential customers are. They don't tell you *why* people buy. To learn that, talk with evaluators: people who decided to buy and people who decided not to. Then **validate, prioritize and share** the problems that matter most to the market you serve.

The "how do we know?" half of the question matters as much as the first half. Every problem you put forward should carry its evidence: who said it, how many, how often, and what it costs them.

This is the outside-in approach Pragmatic Institute champions. Pragmatic Rule #6: "Your opinion, although interesting, is irrelevant." Rule #7: build solutions for problems that are urgent, pervasive and that the market will pay to solve.

## Why Market Problems Matter

Problems uncovered here feed directly into:

- **Product Strategy** (Market) — what the product will be known for and what it will say no to. See `pragmatic-product-strategy`.
- **Market Definition** (Focus) — segments defined by shared problems
- **Positioning** (Planning) — describing the product by the problems it solves
- **Requirements** (Planning) — problem, who has it, how often it happens
- **Ideation** (Planning) — testing concepts against real problems with real users
- **Business Plan** (Business) — quantifying the opportunity
- **Pricing** (Business) — reflecting the value of making the problem go away, not what it cost to build

A weak understanding of market problems cascades: weak positioning, weak requirements, and a product that doesn't sell.

## Problems, needs and desired outcomes

Capture all three for each problem:

- **Pain** — what goes wrong today, and what it costs
- **Need** — what the customer must be able to do
- **Desired outcome** — what they want to be true once it's solved, in their own words ("close the books in two days")

Desired outcomes matter downstream. They become the success criteria that Customer Value Realization (Operations) later checks: did customers get the outcome they bought for?

## The NIHITO Principle

NIHITO stands for **"Nothing Important Happens In The Office."** Product teams must get out of the building and talk directly to the market, regularly and systematically.

NIHITO interviews are qualitative, open-ended conversations designed to uncover:

- What problems people face in your product's domain
- Why those problems matter (urgency, cost, impact)
- How they cope with or work around them today
- What outcome they want
- How they buy, and what trends are shaping the market

Interviews are excellent for **discovery**. They are not enough for **validation**: you can't talk to enough people to reach statistical relevance. Layer quantitative methods (surveys, usage data, sales data) on top.

## The loop: evidence that flows back into Market Problems

The 2027 Framework is a loop, not a line. What teams learn after launch becomes the next round of market problems. Treat these as standing inputs alongside new interviews:

| Source | Focus area (category) | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Customer value reviews | Customer Value Realization (Operations) | Outcomes customers expected but didn't get; new outcomes they now want |
| Exception log | Exceptions (Operations) | Repeated one-off requests that point to a missing capability |
| Post-escalation reviews | Escalations (Operations) | Critical failures that reveal unmet needs or broken workflows |
| Win/loss findings | Win/Loss Analysis (Market) | Problems that decided deals, and problems you didn't address |

Inbound evidence is a lead, not a conclusion. A pattern from exceptions or escalations still needs discovery interviews to find the underlying problem and validation to show it's pervasive. See `pragmatic-customer-value-realization`, `pragmatic-exceptions`, `pragmatic-escalations` and `pragmatic-win-loss-analysis`.

## Key Concepts Quick Reference

**Three filters for a problem worth solving:**
1. **Urgent** — causes real pain now
2. **Pervasive** — affects a group large enough to support a business
3. **Willingness to pay** — painful enough that people will spend money to fix it

A problem should pass at least two of three. Urgent and pervasive but no one pays: consider other monetization. Urgent and paid for but not pervasive: you may be looking at a niche.

**Market problem vs. feature request:** a feature request is a customer's proposed solution. Dig beneath it to the problem and the outcome they want.

**Interview targets:**
- **Current customers** — how they use the product, remaining frustrations, gaps
- **Recent evaluators who bought** — what problem drove the purchase
- **Recent evaluators who didn't buy** — why they chose an alternative or nothing
- **Non-customers** — problems in segments you haven't reached

## Using AI in this focus area

- Transcribe and code interview notes, then cluster recurring themes across dozens of conversations.
- Draft interview guides and survey questions from your research objective, then edit them for bias.
- Scan support tickets, exception logs and escalation reviews for repeated problems worth investigating.
- Rules: check every AI-found theme against real market evidence; NIHITO still applies, and AI can't replace talking to buyers and non-buyers. Keep customer names, recordings and deal data out of tools not approved for them. The team, not the tool, decides which problems to pursue.
- Watch for AI summaries that smooth away the customer's own language or merge distinct problems; keep verbatim quotes.
