# Business Plan — Pragmatic Framework (2027)

## Where this fits in the 2027 Pragmatic Framework

- **Category:** Business — *Is this a good investment, and is it paying off?*
- **Focus-area question:** *Why is this worth funding?*
- **Position:** Above the bar (market-focused)
- **Other focus areas in this category:** Pricing, Buy, Build or Partner, Business Outcomes, Measurement

## What This Skill Does

This skill guides the creation of a **product business plan**: a product-level case for investing in a new product or release. It lays out the purpose, the potential and the risks so leadership can decide which projects to **fund, delay or stop**. Formats vary by organization, but every plan should document the market problems the product addresses and explain why your organization is well suited to solve them.

The work runs in a clear order: confirm the idea supports company strategy; define the vision, target market and problem; then validate the opportunity by analyzing market needs, estimating costs and assessing value. **Present findings early and keep the plan current** — a business plan is a living reference, not a one-time funding artifact.

The business plan is NOT a traditional corporate business plan or a startup pitch deck. It is a **product-level investment justification** that turns market evidence into a clear recommendation.

## Key Responsibilities

- Confirm the opportunity supports company strategy and the Product Strategy (`pragmatic-product-strategy`, Market)
- Analyze the market opportunity objectively, using evidence from the market
- Estimate costs, assess value, and quantify risk with a financial model
- Recommend **fund, delay or stop** (or a conditional fund) based on the evidence
- Share findings early with leadership, and keep the plan updated as evidence changes
- Hand the plan's expected results to Business Outcomes so they can be tracked

## Required Skills

- Written and verbal communications; presentation abilities
- Analytical capability — gathering data, synthesizing it, and telling a story
- Strategic thinking and internal influencing
- Financial acumen (forecasting, market share, top and bottom line)

## Pragmatic Principles That Guide the Business Plan

1. **Outside-in approach** — The plan is built from market evidence, not internal opinions. As Pragmatic teaches: "Your opinion, although interesting, is irrelevant."
2. **The answer is not in the building** — Data comes from interviews with customers, recent evaluators, and untapped potential customers (NIHITO).
3. **Market-driven, not sales-driven** — The plan addresses market segments, not individual deals.
4. **Problem-oriented** — The plan is anchored to validated, urgent market problems — not features or technology.
5. **Distinctive competence matters** — The plan must articulate why *this* organization is well suited to solve the problem.
6. **Strategy first** — If the idea doesn't support company strategy, stop before building the financial model.

## When to Create a Product Business Plan

- Evaluating a new product or major release before committing development resources
- Justifying continued, increased, or reduced investment in an existing product
- Deciding whether to enter a new market segment
- Presenting an opportunity to leadership or investors for funding
- Comparing multiple opportunities so leadership can fund some, delay others and stop the rest

All business plans within an organization should follow the same format so they can be compared on equal footing.

### Internal products and platforms

For internal products (platforms, tools, shared services), the plan answers the same question for internal funders. The "market" is the internal user and stakeholder groups; revenue is replaced by measurable value such as cost reduction, time saved, risk reduced, or revenue enabled elsewhere in the business. Cost allocation and funding model belong in the financial section (see `pragmatic-pricing`).

## Document Structure

Read `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/StuKozola/agent-resource-catalog/main/skills/pragmatic-framework/pragmatic-business-plan/references/business-plan-template.md` for the full template with section-by-section guidance. Below is a summary.

A product business plan should be **concise and action-oriented** — typically 4–8 pages or a focused presentation deck, or a one-page canvas for early screening. Keep it manageable, comparable and current.

### Required Sections

1. **Executive Summary** — One-page overview with the recommendation (fund / delay / stop, or conditional), the opportunity in brief, and key financial highlights.
2. **Strategic Fit & Market Opportunity** — How the idea supports company strategy; which market problems you found, how pervasive and urgent they are. Draws on Market Problems and Win/Loss Analysis (Market).
3. **Target Market & Segmentation** — Which segments you will pursue and whether they are large enough. Draws on Market Definition (Focus).
4. **Distinctive Competence & Competitive Position** — Why your organization is well suited to solve this problem, what you already have, and what else buyers could use (including doing nothing). Draws on Distinctive Competencies, Competitive Landscape and Product Strategy (Market), including the asset inventory in `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/StuKozola/agent-resource-catalog/main/skills/pragmatic-framework/pragmatic-product-strategy/references/asset-registry-template.md`.
5. **Solution Overview** — High-level description of the proposed solution tied to market problems (not a feature list). References the Product Roadmap (Focus) and Buy, Build or Partner (Business).
6. **Financial Model & Risk Quantification** — Revenue (or value) projections, cost estimates, key assumptions, break-even, and an honest quantification of risks.
7. **Recommendation & Expected Outcomes** — Fund, delay or stop, with conditions, next steps, decision gates, and the results the business should expect (the baseline for Business Outcomes).

### Optional Sections

- Distribution strategy alignment
- Pricing strategy summary (see `pragmatic-pricing`)
- Resource requirements and timeline
- Key milestones and decision gates

## How to Use This Skill

### Step 1: Understand Context
Ask what stage they're at:
- Starting from scratch with a new product idea?
- Documenting an opportunity they've already researched?
- Reviewing or updating an existing plan (new evidence, changed market)?
- Need the template to fill in themselves?

### Step 2: Confirm Strategic Fit
Before any modeling, check the idea against company strategy and the Product Strategy. If it doesn't fit, say so — "stop" or "delay" is a valid early answer.

### Step 3: Gather Inputs
A business plan synthesizes outputs from other focus areas. Ask what the user already has:
- Market Problems documentation (interview findings, validated problems) — Market
- Win/Loss Analysis data — Market
- Product Strategy and Distinctive Competencies assessment — Market
- Competitive Landscape analysis — Market
- Asset inventory ("what do we already have?") — input to Product Strategy
- Market Definition / segmentation work — Focus
- Product Roadmap or vision — Focus
- Any existing financial data or projections

If the user doesn't have these, help them think through each area with guided questions rather than skipping them.

### Step 4: Build the Document
Read `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/StuKozola/agent-resource-catalog/main/skills/pragmatic-framework/pragmatic-business-plan/references/business-plan-template.md`, then create the business plan. Use the docx skill if the user wants a Word document, or create a markdown document for simpler needs.

### Step 5: Present Early, Keep Current
Encourage sharing a draft with leadership before it's "finished" — early feedback surfaces objections and missing evidence while they're cheap to fix. Set a review cadence (e.g., each funding cycle, or when a key assumption changes) and record what changed.

### Step 6: Review Against Pragmatic Principles
Before finalizing, validate the plan:
- Does the idea clearly support company strategy?
- Is every claim backed by market evidence, not internal opinion?
- Are market problems described from the customer's perspective?
- Is the financial model built on stated, testable assumptions?
- Does the distinctive competence argument hold up against alternatives?
- Is the recommendation (fund / delay / stop) clear and actionable?
- Are the expected outcomes specific enough for Business Outcomes to track later?
- Could leadership compare this plan against other opportunities?

## Link Forward to Business Outcomes

The business plan sets expectations; Business Outcomes (`pragmatic-business-outcomes`, Business) checks them. Business Outcomes asks *"Is the product doing what the business plan said it would?"* — so the plan should state its expected results (revenue, profit, margin, efficiency, cost reduction, ROI, or stage-appropriate milestones like validation and adoption) and the assumptions behind them. Measurement (`pragmatic-measurement`, Business) then defines the leading and lagging indicators that show early whether those results are on track.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Leading with the solution** — Lead with the market problem, not the product idea.
- **Skipping market evidence** — A business plan without customer interview data is a guess dressed up in a spreadsheet.
- **Overly optimistic financials** — Quantify risk, not just upside. Include realistic scenarios and clearly state assumptions.
- **Presenting late** — Waiting until the plan is "perfect" means leadership sees it after decisions are already forming.
- **Write once, never update** — A stale plan can't guide fund/delay/stop decisions or serve as a baseline for Business Outcomes.
- **Too long or too detailed** — Keep it focused on what leadership needs to decide.
- **Confusing features with value** — Describe the product's ability to solve market problems, not its specifications.
- **Ignoring the competition** — There are always alternatives, including doing nothing.

## Using AI in this focus area

AI can help draft sections from your research notes, build first-pass financial models and sensitivity scenarios, stress-test assumptions by generating counterarguments and risks, and summarize market sizing sources. Rules:
- Check AI output against real market evidence. It can't replace interviews with customers and non-buyers (NIHITO still applies), and it will happily produce confident market-size numbers with no basis — trace every figure to a source.
- Keep sensitive customer, deal, financial and company data out of tools not approved for it.
- The team, not the tool, owns the fund/delay/stop recommendation.
