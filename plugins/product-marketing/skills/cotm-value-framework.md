# The Value Framework — Command of the Message, Step 3

The Value Framework is the single source of truth for how the company describes its value. Every talk track, discovery question, deck, campaign and coaching conversation is built from it. Get it right and specific; everything downstream inherits its quality.

**Output:** a Value Framework document covering each in-scope buyer persona and use case, with every differentiator linked to proof.

## Structure

The framework flows in buyer order:

1. **Before scenarios** — the buyer's current state and business issues, in their words.
2. **Negative consequences** — what the current state costs and what happens if nothing changes.
3. **Positive business outcomes (PBOs)** — the tangible business results once the problem is solved.
4. **Required capabilities** — what any solution must be able to do to deliver those outcomes.
5. **Metrics** — how the buyer will measure success.
6. **Differentiators** — how your approach is unique, better and defensible.
7. **Proof points** — evidence you've delivered before.

Plus two summary elements: the **three essential questions** answered in a paragraph each, and a short **value statement** (developed further into the mantra in `cotm-field-tools`).

## Workflow

### 1. Gather inputs

Ask for, or confirm, the following. If something is missing, note it as an assumption to validate rather than inventing it:

- Buyer personas in scope (economic, functional/user, technical) and the use cases or segments that matter
- Market problems, ideally with buyer quotes (`pragmatic-market-problems`)
- Competitive alternatives, including do-nothing and build-it-yourself (`pragmatic-competitive-landscape`)
- Customer results with numbers, plus win/loss findings (`pragmatic-win-loss-analysis`)
- Existing positioning (`pragmatic-positioning`) and workshop output (`cotm-executive-alignment`)

### 2. Write the problem side first

Draft before scenarios and negative consequences for each persona. Use the buyer's language. Keep each scenario to one business issue. Quantify consequences wherever possible (cost, risk, time, revenue).

### 3. Write the outcome side

Draft positive business outcomes that mirror the before scenarios — each PBO should resolve a specific issue. Tie each PBO to one or more metrics the buyer already tracks or would accept.

### 4. Define required capabilities

For each PBO, write the capabilities any solution would need, in neutral language ("the ability to…"). Then mark which ones you deliver uniquely, better, or at parity. Uniquely-delivered capabilities are candidates for trap-setting questions in `cotm-field-tools`.

### 5. Define differentiators and attach proof

Test each candidate differentiator:

- **Unique:** competitors can't credibly claim it.
- **Better:** where others claim it, you do it measurably better.
- **Defensible:** you can prove it.
- **Relevant:** it connects to a PBO this buyer cares about.

Attach at least one proof point to each. A differentiator with no proof becomes an open item, not a claim.

### 6. Answer the three essential questions

Summarize the framework in three short paragraphs per persona. If these are hard to write, the framework isn't finished.

### 7. Validate

Test the draft with three to five customers and with top reps and sales engineers. Ask: do buyers describe the problem this way? Are the outcomes ones they'd pay for? Would a competitor dispute any differentiator? Revise and record what changed.

### 8. Set ownership and a refresh cadence

Name the owner, approval process and review cadence (at least quarterly and after major launches or competitive moves). Log changes so training and assets can be updated.

## Quality checks

- Every before scenario has a matching PBO, metric and at least one required capability.
- No PBO is a feature in disguise ("customers get real-time dashboards" is a capability; "managers catch at-risk accounts two weeks earlier" is an outcome).
- Every differentiator passes the competitor swap test and has proof.
- Persona-specific language: the CFO's version is not the same as the end user's.
- A new rep could answer the three essential questions after reading it once.

## Common mistakes

- **Inside-out writing:** starting from the product and working toward the buyer.
- **Generic outcomes:** "increase efficiency" with no metric or context.
- **Too many differentiators:** more than four or five per persona dilutes the message.
- **Ignoring do-nothing:** the status quo is often the main competitor; consequences must address it.
- **Set and forget:** frameworks go stale as products, competitors and markets change.

## Using AI in this step

- Draft before scenarios and PBOs from interview notes, call transcripts and win/loss data.
- Run the competitor swap test and flag feature-as-outcome sentences.
- Produce persona variants from a master framework for review.

AI will invent plausible outcomes, numbers and proof; every metric and proof point must trace back to a real source. Keep customer data in approved tools.
