# Advocacy — Pragmatic Framework Skill

## What This Skill Does

This skill helps you build and operationalize a **Customer Advocacy Program** as defined by the Pragmatic Institute Framework. The Advocacy activity lives in the **Programs** category of the framework and is defined as:

> Identify customers who are willing to give testimonials, case studies and references and amplify their voice in the market.

The output is a structured advocacy program that systematically identifies willing customers, captures their stories in compelling formats, and deploys that evidence across the buyer experience to accelerate trust, shorten sales cycles, and reinforce positioning.

## Framework Context

**Category:** Programs (Execution side of the framework)

**Official Definition:** "Identify customers who are willing to give testimonials, case studies and references and amplify their voice in the market."

**Key Responsibilities (from Framework Roles & Responsibilities):**
- Identify customers who are willing to participate in testimonials, case studies, or reference accounts
- Capture and share stories of customer success
- Utilize advocates in a variety of programs including events and thought leadership

**Required Skills:**
- Analytical, with the ability to gather data, synthesize the data, and tell a story
- Sales-oriented thinking
- Written and verbal communications

**Adjacent Framework Activities:**
- **Inputs from:** Positioning (messaging foundation), Buyer Personas (who needs to hear the story), Buyer Experience (where in the journey advocacy assets deploy), Win/Loss Analysis (identifies satisfied customers and success patterns), Marketing Plan (strategic context for advocacy programs), Nurturing (where advocacy content fuels the funnel), Launch (advocates support strategic launches)
- **Outputs to:** Content (advocacy assets become content), Sales Tools (references and case studies arm sales), Awareness (customer voices raise market profile), Revenue Growth (social proof accelerates new customer acquisition), Revenue Retention (customer community deepens loyalty), Measurement (advocacy KPIs feed program tuning)

**Primary Owner:** Product Marketing Manager
**Key Collaborators:** Customer Success, Sales, Marketing Communications, Product Management

## Core Pragmatic Principle: Outside-In Thinking

Advocacy is the purest expression of Pragmatic's outside-in philosophy. Instead of the company telling the market why the product is valuable, **the market tells itself**. Your opinion, although interesting, is irrelevant — Rule #6. Customer advocacy programs let the customer's voice replace vendor claims with peer evidence. This is why Advocacy sits in Programs alongside Awareness, Nurturing, and Launch: it provides the authentic proof that makes every other program more credible.

## How to Use This Skill

When a user asks for help with customer advocacy, follow this six-step workflow.

### Step 1: Assess the Current State

Before building anything, understand where the user's organization stands. Ask about:

- **Existing advocacy assets:** Do they have any case studies, testimonials, or reference customers today? How old are they?
- **Customer health data:** Do they track NPS, CSAT, or customer health scores? Are customer success teams engaged?
- **Buyer personas:** Which buyer personas are they trying to influence? (If personas don't exist yet, suggest they complete the Buyer Personas activity first or create lightweight personas.)
- **Buyer experience stage:** Where in the buying process do prospects stall or need proof? (This tells you where advocacy assets will have the most impact.)
- **Sales requests:** Is the sales team asking for references or case studies? What types? How often?
- **Competitive context:** Do competitors have visible advocacy programs, customer stories, or third-party review presence?

Read `references/advocacy-program-guide.md` for the detailed framework on program structure, advocate identification criteria, and recruitment strategies.

### Step 2: Define the Advocacy Program Strategy

Based on the current state, help the user define:

**Program Goals** — What does success look like? Common goals include:
- Build a reference pipeline of N advocates per product/segment
- Produce N case studies per quarter
- Achieve coverage across all buyer personas and key verticals
- Reduce sales cycle length by providing on-demand reference calls
- Increase third-party review presence (G2, Gartner Peer Insights, TrustRadius)

**Advocacy Asset Types** — Determine which formats the program will produce. Read `references/advocacy-program-guide.md` for the full taxonomy, but the primary types are:
- Customer quotes and pull-quotes (lowest commitment, highest volume)
- Written testimonials (short-form endorsements)
- Case studies (structured problem → solution → results narratives)
- Video testimonials (high-impact, higher production investment)
- Reference calls (live conversations between prospect and advocate)
- Speaking engagements (advocates present at events, webinars, conferences)
- Online reviews (third-party review platforms)
- Customer Advisory Board participation (strategic input plus advocacy signal)

**Segment Coverage Map** — Map advocacy needs by buyer persona, vertical/industry, company size, use case, and buying stage. This reveals gaps: "We have three enterprise case studies but nothing for mid-market" or "All our references are in financial services but we're targeting healthcare."

### Step 3: Identify and Recruit Advocates

This is the most critical operational step. Read `references/advocacy-program-guide.md` for the Ideal Advocate Profile framework and recruitment strategies.

**Identification Signals** — Find potential advocates by looking for:
- High NPS or CSAT scores (Promoters: 9-10)
- Recent successful implementations or renewals
- Customers who have expanded usage or added products
- Active community members or support forum contributors
- Customers who gave positive feedback to their CSM or in support tickets
- Sales team knowledge of enthusiastic contacts
- Win/loss data identifying customers who chose you decisively

**The Ask** — Recruitment works best when it is:
- Personal (from someone the customer already trusts — their CSM or account manager)
- Specific (asking for a particular activity, not a vague "would you be a reference?")
- Reciprocal (offering value back — early product access, executive connections, co-marketing opportunities, event invitations, professional recognition)
- Low-friction initially (start with a small ask like a quote, then build to larger commitments)

**The Advocacy Ladder** — Move advocates through escalating levels of engagement:
1. Online review or short quote
2. Written testimonial or brief Q&A
3. Full case study (written or video)
4. Reference call availability
5. Speaking engagement or co-presentation
6. Customer Advisory Board membership

### Step 4: Capture Customer Stories

Read `references/case-study-framework.md` for the detailed case study creation guide and `templates/case-study-template.md` for the structured template.

**The Problem → Solution → Results Structure** — Every advocacy asset, regardless of format, follows this narrative arc:
- **Situation/Problem:** What challenge did the customer face? Ground this in the market problems your positioning addresses. Use the customer's language, not vendor jargon.
- **Solution:** How did the customer use your product to address the problem? Be specific about features, approach, and implementation.
- **Results:** What measurable outcomes did the customer achieve? Quantify wherever possible — percentages, dollars, time saved, revenue gained.

**The Case Study Litmus Test** — Pragmatic Institute's standard for effective case studies:
- Does it describe a real, named customer (or a credible anonymized profile)?
- Does it articulate a genuine market problem the customer faced?
- Does it show how your product specifically solved that problem?
- Does it include quantifiable results?
- Would the buyer persona reading it see themselves in the story?

**Interview Best Practices:**
- Prepare 8-12 open-ended questions organized around the Problem → Solution → Results arc
- Record the interview (with permission) so you capture the customer's authentic language
- Listen for quotable "golden phrases" — vivid, specific statements that carry emotional and factual weight
- Ask about results in multiple ways to extract specific numbers
- Close by asking "Is there anything else you'd want a prospective buyer to know?"

### Step 5: Deploy Advocacy Assets Across the Buyer Experience

Map each advocacy asset to the buyer experience stage and persona where it has the most impact:

**Awareness Stage** — Prospects are just discovering the problem and potential solutions:
- Customer quotes in blog posts and social media
- Online reviews on third-party platforms
- Speaking engagements at industry events
- Thought leadership co-authored with advocates

**Evaluation Stage** — Prospects are comparing solutions:
- Full case studies matched by industry, company size, or use case
- Video testimonials demonstrating real-world usage
- Analyst reports featuring customer evidence
- Competitive comparison supported by customer references

**Decision Stage** — Prospects are making the final purchase decision:
- Live reference calls with advocates matched to the prospect's profile
- ROI-focused case studies with quantified business impact
- Executive-to-executive reference conversations
- Customer Advisory Board endorsements

**Post-Purchase / Retention** — Existing customers considering renewal or expansion:
- Success stories from peers who expanded usage
- Community-driven advocacy (user groups, forums)
- Customer-led training and best practice sharing

### Step 6: Measure and Optimize the Program

Read `references/advocacy-program-guide.md` for the detailed KPI framework.

**Program Health Metrics:**
- Number of active advocates (total and by segment/persona/vertical)
- Advocate pipeline coverage ratio (advocates available vs. advocacy requests)
- Advocate recruitment rate (new advocates added per quarter)
- Advocate retention rate (how many remain active over time)
- Time-to-recruit (days from identification to first advocacy activity)

**Asset Production Metrics:**
- Case studies produced per quarter
- Testimonials captured per quarter
- Video testimonials produced
- Third-party reviews posted
- Reference calls completed

**Business Impact Metrics:**
- Influenced pipeline (deals where advocacy assets were used)
- Win rate lift (win rate with vs. without advocacy assets)
- Sales cycle compression (deal velocity with vs. without references)
- Revenue influenced by advocacy (attributable closed-won revenue)
- NPS trend among advocates vs. non-advocates (does advocacy deepen loyalty?)

**The Advocacy Scorecard** — Build a simple dashboard that tracks these metrics monthly or quarterly. Connect advocacy measurement to the broader Measurement activity in the Pragmatic Framework to ensure alignment with corporate goals.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Over-relying on the same advocates.** Diversify your advocate pool to prevent burnout and ensure segment coverage. Track "advocate utilization" — no single customer should handle more than 3-4 reference calls per quarter without explicit opt-in.
- **Asking too much too soon.** Start with low-commitment activities (a quote, an online review) before requesting a full case study or keynote appearance.
- **Vendor-centric storytelling.** Case studies that read like product spec sheets fail the litmus test. Let the customer's problem and results drive the narrative, not your feature list.
- **Neglecting the advocate relationship.** Advocacy is reciprocal. Provide value back to advocates: early product access, executive networking, professional development, public recognition, or simple gestures like thank-you notes and branded gifts.
- **Creating advocacy assets but not deploying them.** Assets that sit on a website but aren't integrated into sales processes, nurturing sequences, and event content are wasted effort. Enablement (Sales Alignment, Content, Sales Tools) must be connected.
- **Ignoring negative signals.** If a customer declines an advocacy request, that's a signal worth investigating. It may reveal satisfaction issues that Customer Success should address before they become churn risk.
