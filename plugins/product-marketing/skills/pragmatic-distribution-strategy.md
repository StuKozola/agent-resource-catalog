# Distribution Strategy — Pragmatic Framework (2027)

## Where this fits in the 2027 Pragmatic Framework

- **Category:** Focus — *Where should we play, and where should we invest?*
- **Focus-area question:** *How will people find, buy and get the product?*
- **Position:** Above the bar (market-focused)
- **Other focus areas in this category:** Market Definition, Product Portfolio, Product Roadmap

## What Distribution Strategy Is

Distribution strategy is your plan for how the product reaches the people who buy and use it. It covers four things:

1. **Access** — how people find and get to the product
2. **Purchase** — how and where they buy it
3. **Delivery** — how they receive it and start using it
4. **Support** — how they get help afterward

Options include selling direct, selling through partners, or a mix. The right choice depends on how your market prefers to buy, receive and use products like yours, not on what is easiest for your company.

Start by researching how customers **and non-customers** prefer to buy, then assess what your organization can support. After you implement the strategy, monitor how it performs and revisit it as the market's preferences change.

The fundamental principle is **outside-in thinking**, captured in the Pragmatic rule: "Align your distribution strategy with personas and their problems."

### Internal products and platforms

For an internal product (a platform, an internal tool, a shared service), distribution is how teams get access and how the rollout happens. The same four parts apply:

- **Access** — who is eligible, how they request or are granted access, how they discover the product exists
- **Purchase** — usually funding or chargeback rather than a sale: who approves adoption and who pays (coordinate with Pricing)
- **Delivery** — rollout sequence (pilot teams, waves by business unit or region, opt-in vs mandated), provisioning, migration from the old tool
- **Support** — help desk, internal champions, documentation, office hours

Research how internal teams prefer to adopt tools the same way you'd research external buyers: talk to teams who adopted and those who didn't.

## Key Responsibilities

The person or team owning Distribution Strategy is responsible for:

1. **Matching channels to the market's buying preferences** — how target personas discover, evaluate and purchase
2. **Understanding how users want to receive, use and get support for the product** — delivery and support preferences shape channel requirements
3. **Evaluating the value proposition for channel partners** — why would a partner carry the product, and can you still make a profit?
4. **Monitoring performance and revisiting the strategy** as preferences change

## Required Skills

Analytical capability (gathering data, synthesizing it, telling a story); strategic thinking; financial savvy (margins, channel economics); partner or business development experience; knowledge of the channels relevant to your business.

## How to Use This Skill

### Step 1: Understand the Situation

Before recommending channels, ask about:
- **Product type**: physical goods, software/SaaS, digital content, services, hybrid, or an internal product
- **Target segments**: who are the buyers and users? B2B, B2C, internal teams?
- **Current state**: new market, expansion, or rethinking an existing strategy?
- **Buyer personas**: who decides and who influences?
- **Market Definition work**: are segments defined and prioritized?

If Market Definition or Buyer Personas are missing, flag it. Offer preliminary segmentation, or proceed while noting the dependency.

### Step 2: Research How Customers and Non-Customers Buy

The market's behavior sets the strategy. Include non-customers: they show you the channels you're missing. Explore:

- Where do buyers discover products like yours?
- How do they evaluate and compare?
- Do they prefer buying direct (sales team, website) or through intermediaries (resellers, marketplaces, distributors, service providers)?
- What role does self-service play vs high-touch sales?
- Are there geographic or regulatory constraints on channel access?
- How do they expect delivery, onboarding and ongoing support to work?

For research techniques, read `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/StuKozola/agent-resource-catalog/main/skills/pragmatic-framework/pragmatic-distribution-strategy/references/research-methods.md`.

### Step 3: Assess What Your Organization Can Support

Compare the market's preferences with what you can execute: sales capacity, partner relationships, fulfillment, onboarding and support capabilities, systems (billing, provisioning, marketplace listings). Note gaps you'd need to close, and whether closing them is worth it.

### Step 4: Evaluate Channel Options

Load `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/StuKozola/agent-resource-catalog/main/skills/pragmatic-framework/pragmatic-distribution-strategy/references/channel-types.md` for direct, indirect and hybrid models and their trade-offs.

- **Direct** — company sells straight to the customer (website, sales team, company stores)
- **Indirect** — third parties sell on your behalf (resellers, distributors, wholesalers, VARs, marketplaces)
- **Hybrid** — a mix for different segments or stages

For each candidate channel, evaluate:
1. **Buyer alignment** — does it match how the segment prefers to buy, receive and get support?
2. **Reach** — can it access the segments you target?
3. **Economics** — margins required; can you profit after channel costs?
4. **Control** — over messaging, pricing and customer experience
5. **Support model** — who supports the customer after the sale, and how well?
6. **Scalability** — can it grow with the business?
7. **Competitive dynamics** — which channels do competitors use?

### Step 5: Assess the Partner Value Proposition

If indirect channels are involved: why would a partner carry this product? Consider margin, fit with the partner's portfolio, demand and brand awareness you bring, training and marketing support you provide, and exclusivity or preferential terms.

### Step 6: Build the Distribution Strategy Document

```
DISTRIBUTION STRATEGY

1. Executive Summary
   - Target segments and their buying preferences
   - Recommended channel mix and key rationale

2. Market Buying Behavior Analysis
   - How each segment discovers, evaluates, purchases, receives and gets support
   - What non-customers told us
   - Data sources and methods

3. Channel Strategy
   - Primary and supporting channels, with rationale
   - Channel roles by segment
   - Access, purchase, delivery and support model for each

4. Channel Economics
   - Margin structure, volume assumptions, profitability

5. Partner Strategy (if applicable)
   - Ideal partner profile, partner value proposition
   - Partner enablement plan, channel conflict mitigation

6. Organizational Readiness
   - What we can support today; gaps to close

7. Implementation Plan
   - Phasing (or rollout waves, for internal products), resources, milestones

8. Monitoring and Review
   - Channel performance KPIs
   - Triggers and cadence for revisiting the strategy
```

### Step 7: Monitor and Revisit

Distribution is not a one-time decision. Track channel performance (pipeline and revenue by channel, cost of sale, partner productivity, time to deliver, support satisfaction, or for internal products, adoption by team). Revisit when performance drifts, buyer preferences shift, a new channel emerges, or you enter a new segment.

### Step 8: Connect Downstream

- **Pricing** (Business) — channel margins affect pricing models
- **Marketing Plan** (Go-to-Market) — channel-specific tactics and co-marketing
- **Launch** (Go-to-Market) — channel readiness is a launch gate
- **Organizational Alignment** (Go-to-Market) — sales, success and support must work the same model (`pragmatic-organizational-alignment`)
- **Channel Support** (Enablement) — partners and channels need ongoing information and training to position, sell and support the product (`pragmatic-channel-support`)

## Common Pitfalls

- **Inside-out thinking** — choosing channels because the company already has them
- **Ignoring non-customers** — researching only how current customers bought
- **Forgetting delivery and support** — a channel that sells well but supports poorly hurts retention
- **Ignoring channel conflict** — selling direct alongside partners without a plan
- **Skipping the economics** — choosing channels without understanding margins
- **One-size-fits-all** — the same channel for segments that buy differently
- **Neglecting partner enablement** — expecting partners to sell without training, tools and support
- **Set and forget** — not monitoring performance or revisiting as preferences change

## Output Formats

- Complete Distribution Strategy document (structure in Step 6)
- Channel evaluation matrix
- Partner value proposition one-pager
- Channel economics model
- Internal rollout plan (for internal products)

Use the docx, pptx or xlsx skill based on the user's preferred format.

## Using AI in this focus area

- Summarize interview and survey data on how segments prefer to buy, receive and get support.
- Build first-draft channel economics models and scenario comparisons.
- Scan competitors' public channel footprints (marketplaces, partner directories) to map channel dynamics.
- Rules: check AI output against real market evidence; NIHITO still applies, and buying preferences come from talking to customers and non-customers. Keep partner terms, margins and customer data out of tools not approved for them. The team, not the tool, decides the channel mix.
